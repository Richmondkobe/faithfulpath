# Lesson 10: Room 5, Faith and Church
*Let Christ's word shape an ordinary home.*

**Scripture:** Colossians 3:16 to 17: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God. And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him." (ESV)

## In this lesson, you will learn to:
- Describe your own faith honestly, including what it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday.
- Plan a practical week of worship, church and prayer.
- Identify the important conversations still needed about authority, decisions and raising children in the faith.
- Discern your relationship from who you both are now, rather than who you hope the other will become.

# Recording script and transcript

*Author's note: five-movement pattern, with no word study. The italic headings are for your guidance and are not spoken. About 1,600 words, to be read in your own voice in about 13 minutes, including the Scripture reading and the pause. Please keep the tone humble; this is the most spiritually loaded room, and listeners will be at different places. Change anything that does not sound like you.*

*Movement 1. Read the passage*

Welcome to Lesson 10. This room is about faith and church, and it is where two people can use the same word and imagine very different lives.

Let's begin with Scripture. Colossians chapter 3, verses 16 and 17.

"Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God. And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him."

*Movement 2. Explain the passage*

First, notice who Paul is writing to. This is a letter to a church, not a manual for marriages. The teaching on marriage comes a little later in the same chapter. So these two verses describe the shape of Christian life together, and we draw from them for the home.

Notice what he asks. "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly." Not visit. Not appear on Sundays. Dwell, and richly. The word of Christ is meant to be at home among believers, shaping how they teach, correct and encourage one another, and how they sing and give thanks.

And notice the second sentence. "Whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus." Faith is not one compartment of life. It covers the whole of it, ordinary words and ordinary deeds, done with thankfulness.

*Movement 3. Draw out the biblical truth*

That is the truth this lesson stands on. A Christian home is not one that holds a great many church activities. It is one where Christ's word shapes how you worship, decide, speak and love, on an ordinary Tuesday as much as on a Sunday.

And notice the phrase "teaching and admonishing one another." Paul describes believers ministering the word to one another. Applied to marriage, this warns us against treating one spouse as the permanent spiritual supervisor and the other as merely the pupil.

So there is a question for each of you before you marry, and it is not "Do we both believe in Jesus?" It is: can we build a marriage in which our faith draws us toward God, toward truth, and toward each other, even when our spiritual journeys are not identical?

*Movement 4. Make it real*

Here is how it usually goes wrong.

Two people agree on the word "Christian" without ever defining what it means in the daily running of a marriage. One imagines: "We'll be deeply involved. Serving, evangelising, at church several times a week, our children raised right in the middle of it." The other, just as sincerely: "We'll attend, we'll pray, we'll live as Christians, but our family life won't revolve around church programmes."

Either pattern may be faithful, but only if the couple understands what it will require and freely agrees to it. Both people mean it. Neither has said it out loud.

Then they are married, and within a year one feels their spouse is spiritually passive, and the other feels their marriage is being consumed by commitments they never agreed to. Each is quietly disappointed, and neither can say why, because on paper they agreed about everything.

So the conversation has to move from "are we Christians?" to "what does our faith actually require of the marriage we are building?" Ask questions that bring faith out of the abstract and into ordinary weeks. What role does prayer have? Do I expect us to pray together daily? What does going to church mean to each of us? How much time am I willing to give to ministry before it starts costing the marriage?

And here is a phrase that does almost no work until you explain it: "spiritual leadership." Two people can both use it and picture entirely different arrangements. One imagines someone who decides. Another imagines someone who serves. Another imagines someone who leads devotions on Sunday evenings and otherwise decides jointly. So say what you mean, concretely.

Now, one question is worth sitting with: what would my faith look like if nobody at church could see it? That separates a spiritual life from church activity. They overlap in healthy people, and can be quite distinct in others, and you should each know which you are marrying.

*[Short pause. On the page: "Pause here. What does your faith look like on an ordinary Tuesday, when nobody from church can see it? Take a moment before you go on."]*

Now the most sensitive part. Sometimes both people are genuinely Christians and simply at different places. One prays regularly, reads Scripture, serves, thinks about faith constantly. The other believes in Christ sincerely and rarely does any of those things with much discipline.

That is not automatic grounds for calling off a wedding. But it does matter, and it will not stay quiet. The real question is: can the two of you respect each other's actual current reality, and build a marriage on that?

Two dangerous assumptions turn up here, usually one in each person. The partner who wants more church involvement may think, "Once we're married, I'll bring them along." The partner who wants less may think, "I don't want this level of activity, but I'll agree rather than risk losing the relationship." Both are marrying a future version of the other person, and neither has said so. This is the people-pleasing from Lesson 1, and the hoping-marriage-will-change-it from Lesson 3, appearing together in the most spiritually loaded room in the house.

So each of you answers your own question. To the one who wants more shared spiritual activity: if your partner's present practices stayed the same, could you still freely choose this marriage? And to the other: if your partner's level of commitment stays exactly where it is, can you live peacefully with that for the long term, or are you agreeing to a life you do not want?

Different denominations can be manageable, but only if you discuss what actually touches the marriage: which church, how you will worship, how you hope to raise any children in the faith, and what you will do when your traditions teach different things. A difference in style is usually negotiable. A genuine disagreement about baptism, the Lord's Supper, spiritual authority in the home, or how children come into the faith is a different order of thing, and deserves a proper conversation rather than a hopeful one.

And now the hardest part of this room. Do not let "more spiritually mature" become a synonym for "more spiritually important." Long church involvement does not by itself demonstrate spiritual maturity. Maturity shows in character: humility, faithfulness, self-control, repentance, love, truthfulness, and the willingness to be corrected.

Neither person should use this room to instruct the other to become a copy of themselves. Ask a harder question instead: what kind of Christian spouse am I actually becoming?

And one instruction above all: do not marry someone's spiritual potential. Marry the person in front of you. People grow enormously after marriage, and we should hope and pray they do. But a marriage should not be founded on a private project to fix, convert, disciple or upgrade your spouse. That makes one of you a perpetual disappointment and the other a perpetual supervisor.

If either of you is in a period of real doubt, that is worth saying now. It is far better said now than carried silently into a marriage where the other person believes they are marrying certainty.

*Movement 5. Practise it today*

Your step has two parts. First, about twenty minutes alone with the questions in the worksheet. Where am I actually, spiritually, not where I would like to be? What does my faith look like on an ordinary Tuesday? Where do I struggle? What do I doubt? Many Christians carry questions or doubts they have found difficult to express. You do not have to write anything down.

Then set aside 45 to 60 minutes, together, when neither of you is tired. Have your Lesson 2 rules in front of you. Work through three main questions. First: where are we each spiritually, and what does our faith look like on an ordinary week? You control the details and pace of what you share. But if your present beliefs or doubts materially affect the Christian marriage your partner believes you are building, speak honestly about that before you marry. Second: what would a realistic week of worship, service, prayer, rest and time together look like for us? Third, and it is the honest one, out loud: am I hoping you will become something different from what you are now?

If you find a real difference, do not smooth it over. Record it. Some convictions are non-negotiable, and infant baptism, for instance, becomes concrete the moment there is a baby. Identify those now, not in a maternity ward.

The other important questions, about giving, authority and decisions, and how your children will be raised in the faith, are worth continuing over the coming weeks. You do not need to settle them tonight.

Let me close with a short prayer.

Lord, thank You that Your word is meant to dwell among us. Help us build a home where faith is real, ordinary and honest. Keep us from using Your name to silence one another. Give us humility to receive one another as we are, and wisdom to grow together. Amen.

I'll see you in Lesson 11.

# Worksheet: Conversation Record (Room 5)

*From the book's Chapter 6, Room 5. Downloadable. Complete on your own device or on paper.*

**Before you begin**
Prepare privately first (about 20 minutes). Then choose a time when neither of you is tired. Review the rules you agreed in Lesson 2. Respect each person's conscience. You may stop earlier if you need to, record the next question and agree when to continue.

**Private preparation (about 20 minutes, alone)**
- Where am I actually, spiritually, not where I would like to be?
- What does my faith look like on an ordinary Tuesday?
- What would it look like if no one from church could see it?
- Where do I struggle? What do I doubt?
These answers are yours. You do not have to share them.

**The three main questions (45 to 60 minutes, together)**
1. **Where we each are.** You control the details and pace of what you share from your private answers. But if your present beliefs or doubts materially affect the Christian marriage your partner believes you are building, speak honestly about that before you marry. What does our faith look like in an ordinary week?
2. **A realistic week.** What would a realistic week of worship, service, prayer, rest and time together look like for us? Which church, and how often? Do we serve, in what, and how many hours? Do we pray together, when, and who leads?
3. **The honest question.** Each answers out loud: *Am I hoping you will become something different from what you are now?* And each answers your own question:
   - If your partner's present preferences and practices remained the same, would you still freely choose this marriage?
   - If your partner's level of commitment stays exactly where it is today, can you live peacefully with that over the long term, or are you agreeing to a life you do not want?

**Important conversations to continue before marriage**
You do not need to finish these tonight. Choose which you will continue, and when.
- **Authority and decisions.** What does spiritual leadership mean to me, concretely? How do we decide when we disagree about something we both consider a matter of faith? Who do we go to when we need counsel? What happens if one of us wants to leave our church?
- **Giving.** What do we give, and how do we decide?
- **Reading Scripture together.** Together, separately, or not much?
- **Children and faith.** How are our children raised in the faith, practically, week by week? What if one of them later does not want to go?
- **Disagreeing about Scripture.** What happens when you disagree with my reading of a biblical issue? Read each passage in context, and ask what it calls you to practise before applying it to your partner.

**If you find a real difference**
- **Different levels of church activity.** Agree on specific commitments and enough time for rest and your relationship. More activity does not automatically mean deeper faith. Respect each person's conscience while examining whether your plans can fit together.
- **Different denominations.** Discuss worship, membership and the faith practices of your household before marrying. If you expect to attend different churches, consider how you will share spiritual life and make decisions about children. Do not assume the arrangement will settle itself.
- **Different expectations of faith in daily life.** If either person is agreeing to a life they do not want, the wedding will not resolve that difference.
- **A conviction one of you cannot compromise.** Some are genuinely non-negotiable. Identify these now.
- **One of you is doubting.** Ordinary questions may remain private, and you control the details and pace of what you share. But if your present beliefs or doubts materially affect the Christian marriage your partner believes you are building, such as no longer believing in Christ, rejecting church entirely or changing a foundational conviction, speak honestly about that before you marry. It is far better said now than carried silently into a marriage where the other person believes they are marrying certainty.
