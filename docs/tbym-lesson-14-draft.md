# Lesson 14: Room 9, Conflict Itself
*The other eight rooms were subjects. This one is how you disagree. Decide in advance who you intend to be when it is hard.*

**Scripture:** Ephesians 4:26 to 27: "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil." (ESV)

*A note before you listen: this lesson is about ordinary disagreement between two people who can safely disagree. If disagreement in your relationship makes you afraid, you may prefer to listen privately, or to read the transcript instead, and to read “Need more support?” first. You may stop at any time.*

## In this lesson, you will learn to:
- See your conflict as a pattern: pursue and withdraw, escalate, or avoid.
- Write your Conflict Covenant.
- Use the optional Readiness Review to consider what your experience has shown. It is not a score.
- Know what to do when you find a real disagreement.

# Recording script and transcript

*Author's note: five-movement pattern. The italic headings are for your guidance and are not spoken. About 1,550 words, to be read in your own voice in about 13 minutes, including the Scripture reading and the pause. This is the last lesson, so let the closing feel like a blessing. Change anything that does not sound like you.*

*Movement 1. Read the passage*

Welcome to Lesson 14, the last room. This lesson is about disagreement itself. You may listen privately, and you may stop at any time.

Let's begin with Scripture. Ephesians chapter 4, verses 26 and 27.

"Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil."

*Movement 2. Explain the passage*

Paul is writing to believers about how they live together as one body. This is not a passage about marriage as such. But it speaks to anyone who lives closely with other people, and that includes a husband and a wife.

Notice what Paul does not say. He does not say, "Never be angry." He says, "Be angry and do not sin." Anger is not automatically sin, but it quickly becomes an occasion for sinful words, actions and settled resentment. Paul's concern is both what we do in anger and whether we allow it to remain.

Then he gives a direction: "Do not let the sun go down on your anger." Take that as urgency about not nursing anger, not as a rule that you must stay up all night until everything is settled. Some conversations need a break, and we will come to that. The point is that anger should not be carried and stored.

And he gives the reason: "Give no opportunity to the devil." Anger that is fed and stored can create an opportunity for further harm.

*Movement 3. Draw out the biblical truth*

That is the truth this lesson stands on. Anger is not automatically sin, but unaddressed anger, and what we do when we are angry, is where the danger lies. Truth, self-control, repentance, forgiveness and timely repair are what keep a marriage from being governed by whoever is angriest at the moment.

So the aim of this room is not a couple who never disagrees. It is a couple who knows how they disagree, what they will refuse to do to each other, and how they find their way back.

There is a second mistake to avoid. Some people define a good marriage as one with fewer arguments. But one couple argues often and repairs well. Another rarely argues because one of them stopped speaking honestly two years ago. The second couple looks better, and may be in far more danger. What matters is whether both people are free to speak, and what happens during and after a disagreement.

*Movement 4. Make it real*

Let me describe how this usually goes wrong, and then how to look at it differently.

The irony is that talking about how you fight often becomes a fight. One says, "Whenever we argue, you shut down." The other says, "I shut down because you attack me." The first says, "I only get sharp because you never listen." Within a minute, they are acting out the exact cycle they sat down to discuss.

In an ordinary disagreement, the pattern often looks like this. One person raises something. The other becomes defensive. The first becomes more intense. The second withdraws. The first pursues harder. The second shuts down. At the end, both can say with total sincerity, "I only did that because you did the other thing first."

Some couples fall into pursue and withdraw. Others escalate together, or both avoid the issue. Roles may change by topic. Describe your actual pattern rather than trying to fit a label. And notice that each of you remains responsible for your own actions, even when you can explain what you were reacting to.

So the question is not "Who is the problem?" It is: what happens between us when something becomes emotionally difficult?

*[Short pause. On the page: "Pause here. What usually starts a difficult conversation between us? Take a moment before you go on."]*

Here is a question that changes how you see it: what are each of you trying to protect when you disagree? One may be protecting themselves from feeling controlled. The other, from feeling abandoned. One is fighting for respect; the other, for safety. Understanding that can help explain ordinary conflict. It does not excuse intimidation, and it does not make one person responsible for the other's harmful choices.

Now a caution that matters. Everything I have just said is about ordinary conflict, between two people who can safely disagree. Abuse is not a shared communication loop. Responsibility for abusive conduct belongs to the person using it. If disagreement in your relationship makes you afraid, if one of you cannot refuse, raise a concern or speak honestly without punishment, do not use the language of "our cycle." Stop the joint exercises and speak to someone outside the relationship.

For everyone else, look at repair. How long do our unresolved conflicts stay unresolved? Who usually moves first towards repair? Is it usually the same person? If it is, you may have developed a pattern in which responsibility for repair falls unevenly. Name it before resentment grows. Can I say, "I was wrong"? Can I change my position after the conversation has started? And when a difficult conversation ends, what tells us it is actually finished? Couples often stop talking without resolving anything and treat the silence as resolution. You need a marker that means finished, something you say or do that both of you recognise.

Behind all of it is a larger question. Who are we going to be when conflict comes? That is not a technique. It is an identity, and it is decided in advance, or it is decided by whoever is angriest at the time.

*Movement 5. Practise it today*

Your step has two parts. First, separately, about fifteen to twenty minutes. Each of you completes three sentences in writing. When I am hurt, my instinct is to… What I need my spouse to remember about me during conflict is… And, the person I want to become when we disagree is… The third is the important one. The first two describe who you are. The third describes who you are choosing to become, and it is the only one you have any control over.

Then set aside 45 to 60 minutes, in a private place, when neither of you is tired. Have your Lesson 2 rules in front of you. Work through three main questions. First, exchange your sentences. Read them, and ask questions. Do not argue. Second, describe what happens between you when something becomes emotionally difficult, without trying to decide who started it. Third, write your Conflict Covenant. It is a statement of who you intend to be towards each other when it is hard.

Here is a starting set. Take what fits, change what does not, and add what only applies to the two of you. We will not use conflict to punish each other. We will not threaten to end the relationship as a tactic for frightening or winning against each other. Serious decisions about the relationship will be discussed calmly and may include wise outside support. We will tell the truth before resentment becomes silence. We will take a break when we are overwhelmed, we will agree when to return to the conversation, and we will come back. We will protect private information while remaining free to seek wise, confidential support. We will apologise without waiting for the other to apologise first. And we will not demand agreement as proof of love.

Then close by answering one question out loud. Five years from now, when we have a disagreement we cannot quickly solve, what do you want to be able to say about how I treated you?

The sentence you are building towards is not, "We never had conflict." It is, "Even when we disagreed, you treated me with dignity, and I remained free to speak honestly."

If you find a real disagreement, do not panic, and do not minimise it. A serious disagreement does not automatically mean the engagement should end, but it may be a reason to pause. Do not solve it in one conversation. Understand it first. And if it cannot be resolved before the wedding, do not hide it because the date is approaching. A delayed wedding is far easier to manage than a deeply unresolved marriage.

Asking for help is not a sign that your relationship has failed. Sometimes the wisest sentence available is, "We have taken this as far as we can on our own."

And when you marry, do not put all of this away like a textbook after an exam. Choose something ordinary and regular, once a week or once a fortnight, twenty to thirty minutes. Begin with something you appreciated. That keeps honesty from becoming something you only do when something has gone wrong.

Let me close with a short prayer.

Lord, thank You that You are patient with us. Teach us to be angry without sinning, and to bring what hurts into the light before it settles. Give us honest words, gentle hearts and the humility to say, "I was wrong." Keep us in truth and in love. Make our home a place where each of us can speak and be heard. Amen.

Thank you for walking through these rooms. May the God of peace lead you in truth, love and faithfulness.

**If you find a real difference**
- **First, do not panic, and do not minimise it.** A serious disagreement does not automatically mean the engagement should end, but it may be a reason to pause. Finding no major disagreement is not a failure either; the question is whether your answers were honest and freely given.
- **Identify what kind of disagreement it is.** Is it a preference that can be negotiated? A conviction that requires mutual understanding and respect, without agreement? Or a fundamental incompatibility neither of you can honestly live with? Understand what is at stake before classifying it.
- **Do not solve it in one conversation.** Understand it first. What does each of you want, and why? What sits underneath: a fear, a value, an expectation, an inherited assumption? Then ask what a genuine compromise would look like, and what would simply be one person surrendering. A workable compromise is freely agreed and respects what matters to both people. An agreement made under pressure may leave a serious issue unresolved.
- **If it cannot be resolved before the wedding,** do not hide it because the date is approaching, and do not let the deposit on the venue make the decision. A delayed wedding is far easier to manage than a deeply unresolved marriage.
- **Bring someone in when** the same issue keeps returning with no progress; conversations reliably become explosive or shut down completely; there is serious distrust or something has been concealed; you have a significant disagreement about children, faith, money, roles or family boundaries that you cannot move; or one of you is afraid to speak honestly. Look for someone who listens carefully, respects your faith and autonomy, explains their qualifications and confidentiality limits, and recognises abuse. Ask how they assess safety and when they refer to specialist support. Their job is not to help you win, or to preserve an engagement at any cost. Ask for help before a crisis if you can, but if you are already in a crisis, you have not missed your chance.
