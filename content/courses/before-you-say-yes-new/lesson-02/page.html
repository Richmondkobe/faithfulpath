What Does “Equally Yoked” Actually Mean?

Is “Are they a Christian?” enough?

In this lesson

You will learn

  • What Paul meant in 2 Corinthians 6:14, in its setting in Corinth, and why “only in the Lord” applies it to marriage
  • Why the label “Christian” is the beginning of the question, not the end

  1. Is there an area of my spiritual life that needs attention before I could be a good partner?

Part B: What I have observed about them (skip if you are not dating). For each, think: Observed · Not yet observed · Concern observed · Not discussed. Conversion: their faith shapes decisions when nobody is watching. Fruit: I have seen them repent of something; they treat people who cannot help them with kindness. Doctrine: we agree on the central claims of historic Christianity; I have asked rather than assumed. Church: they belong somewhere they are known. Direction: their faith is growing and they can receive correction.

  1. How did they come to faith, in their own words?
  2. Which fruit of the Spirit have I actually seen in them, and when?

Spiritual freedom and pressure. I can ask spiritual questions without being shamed · they do not use Scripture, prophecy or spiritual authority to end a disagreement · they can admit they may have misunderstood God · I feel free to seek counsel outside the relationship · they have never pressured me to move faster by claiming divine confirmation. Anything marked “Concern observed” here is not a compatibility difference. It is a warning, to take to someone you trust.

Part C: Reading your answers. Foundational unknown (what they believe about Christ, salvation or Scripture): do not rush towards exclusivity or engagement · Important conversation needed (church, baptism, giving, children, spiritual practices): discuss openly and seek counsel · Observation needs time (character, repentance, teachability): slow down and let patterns emerge. Any “Partly” or “Not yet” in Part A is not a disqualification; the yoke has two sides.

Part D: Questions worth asking them, over time, not as an interview: How did you come to know Christ? · Can you remember a time when your faith influenced a difficult decision? · What do you believe that other Christians disagree with you about? · Who in your church would know if you were struggling? · If you could give your life to one thing, what would it be? · What has God been teaching you lately?

Part E. Bring what you have written to God. If you are dating someone, pray for them by name, for their faith, not only for the relationship.

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Is "Are they a Christian?" enough?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 2, and we are still in Part One of the course, Before You Start Dating.

In Lesson 1 we looked into our own hearts and asked what is driving the desire for a relationship. In this lesson we turn to one of the most quoted verses in Christian conversations about dating: "Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers." I will picture it as a wooden yoke lying across the path, waiting to be understood.

And we carry one question with us: is "Are they a Christian?" enough?

2 · The checkbox

For many Christians, this verse has become the whole of Christian dating advice, squeezed into a single test. Is this person a Christian? If yes, go ahead. If no, stop.

That test is not wrong. A Christian should marry a Christian. But I want to take the verse more seriously than the checkbox does. When we look at what Paul actually wrote, and at what Scripture says elsewhere about faith and marriage, "Are they a Christian?" turns out to be the beginning of the question, not the end of it.

3 · Do not be unequally yoked

Let us read the passage. Paul writes, in Second Corinthians chapter six:

"Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers. For what partnership has righteousness with lawlessness? Or what fellowship has light with darkness? What accord has Christ with Belial? Or what portion does a believer share with an unbeliever? What agreement has the temple of God with idols? For we are the temple of the living God."

Notice the questions. Partnership, fellowship, accord, portion, agreement. Paul is asking what can be shared between things that point in opposite directions.

4 · A church in Corinth

To understand the verse, we need to stand where its first readers stood. Paul was writing to a church in Corinth, a busy Greek city full of temples, idols and pagan religious life. Pagan worship ran through much of its public, social and business life.

So when Paul asks, "What agreement has the temple of God with idols?" he is speaking about the whole shape of Christian life in a pagan city: worship, alliances and compromise. And he reminds the church who they are: "we are the temple of the living God." The passage runs on into chapter seven, where he calls them to holiness "in the fear of God."

So this passage is not, first of all, about marriage. It is about belonging to God in a city that pulled the other way.

5 · Only in the Lord

It is worth being honest about that. Applying this verse to dating and marriage is an inference. It is a reasonable one, and Christians have drawn it for a long time, because marriage is the deepest partnership two people can enter.

And Paul gives more direct instruction elsewhere. Writing about a Christian widow, he says she "is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord." The immediate instruction is about widows, but it reflects a wider Christian principle: believers should enter marriage with someone who shares their allegiance to Christ.

The Old Testament makes a similar point. Israel was warned against marrying into the surrounding nations, and the reason given was spiritual, not ethnic: "for they would turn away your sons from following me." So the principle is well founded. A Christian should marry a Christian.

6 · Unequally yoked

Now look at the picture Paul chose. The words "unequally yoked" translate one Greek word, heterozygeo. It joins two parts: heteros, meaning "different", and zygos, meaning "yoke".

A yoke is a wooden bar that joins two animals for shared labour. The law of Moses said, "You shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together." Many commentators think Paul's word echoes that kind of picture. Two animals, different in size and movement, under one yoke, make the shared work difficult. The picture helps us understand the problem of an unsuitable yoke.

So Paul's image is not merely about two creatures standing in the same field. It is about a binding partnership, and the direction in which that partnership pulls.

7 · A foundation, not a guarantee

Applied to marriage, then, being equally yoked means more than carrying the same religious label. It means sharing a genuine allegiance to Christ, from which you can build a life together.

One boundary needs setting before we go further. Spiritual compatibility does not guarantee that two people should marry. Two sincere Christians may still differ deeply in calling, character, communication, family expectations, or their vision for marriage. We will come to those questions in Lesson 15.

This lesson asks only one thing: does the relationship have a genuinely shared Christian foundation? A foundation is necessary. But knowing the foundation is there does not tell you that the house should be built.

8 · Point 1: The problem with the label

Our first point is the problem with "Are they a Christian?"

In some parts of the world, "Christian" is a category on an identity card. In others, it means the family you were born into, the school you attended, or the church you were carried to as a baby. Many people who would call themselves Christian have never read a Gospel, never prayed outside a crisis, and never made a decision that was shaped by Christ.

None of this is said in contempt. The label, on its own, tells you very little. James writes, "You believe that God is one; you do well. Even the demons believe, and shudder!" Believing that God exists is not the same as belonging to him. Church attendance is not the same as discipleship. And a Christian upbringing is not the same as a Christian life, though it can be the soil from which a very real one grows.

9 · Fruit you can observe

But first, one important limit. None of us can see another person's heart. Only God can do that, and it is not your job to decide whether someone is saved.

But Jesus did say, "You will recognize them by their fruits." Fruit is something you can observe, and it ripens over time. So, as our second point, here are five questions that sit underneath the label. They do not judge a heart. They help you notice what you have actually seen.

10 · Point 2: Conversion

The five questions are about conversion, fruit, doctrine, church and direction.

The first is conversion: is their faith their own? Some people have personally embraced the faith in which they were raised. Others simply carry its label. From a distance the two can look alike.

So ask, gently, how they came to faith. Not to grade the story. Some people have dramatic conversions, and some cannot remember a time when they did not believe. Both are real. Listen instead for ownership. Do they speak of God as someone they know, or as something their family does? When they moved city, changed jobs or lost someone, did their faith go with them?

You are not looking for a perfect testimony. You are looking for evidence that their faith would remain, even if you were not in their life.

11 · Fruit

The second question is fruit: what does their faith produce? Paul lists the fruit of the Spirit: "love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control." Not one of these is a religious activity. They are qualities of character, seen in how a person treats other people.

A person's faith is shown less by how they speak about God than by how they treat people who cannot help them, how they respond when they are wronged, whether they keep their word, and whether they can apologise. Does their faith make them kinder, more honest and more self-controlled than they would otherwise be? Or is it a vocabulary laid over an unchanged life?

Look also for repentance. Not perfection, because nobody has that, but a pattern of noticing a wrong, owning it, and turning from it.

12 · Doctrine

The third question is doctrine: do you believe the same things, and which differences matter?

Some beliefs are foundational. Historic Christianity is centred on the Triune God; the full divinity and humanity of Jesus Christ; his atoning death and bodily resurrection; salvation through Christ; and the authority of Scripture. Serious disagreement here is not a small denominational difference. It may show that the two of you mean very different things when you call yourselves Christian.

13 · Christians differ

Beyond the foundation, Christians differ, and it is right to be fair about that. Some differences are real but liveable: how and when to baptise, styles of worship, views of the end times, spiritual gifts, whether to drink alcohol. Many strong marriages hold differences like these.

And some differences are secondary in theology but primary in daily life. If one of you believes children should be baptised as infants and the other believes they should not, that decision cannot be postponed once a child arrives. Baptism appears in both groups for a reason. As a matter of doctrine, many Christians can respect each other's view. But for a couple, it becomes a decision they must make together, and not every couple will find that difference easy to live with. If one of you tithes and the other thinks giving is optional, that becomes a monthly conversation.

The aim is not identical views. It is to know honestly where you differ, and to make sure that what you share is the centre, not only the edges. And do not assume you know what someone believes because of the church they attend. Ask.

14 · Church

The fourth question is church: do they belong somewhere? The normal New Testament pattern is not a Christian on their own, but believers joined to a church where they worship, serve, are cared for and are held accountable. Hebrews urges believers: "not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another."

So the question is not only "Do they attend somewhere?" but "Are they known anywhere?" A season without a settled church is not automatically a warning. A move, illness, persecution or church hurt may explain it. What matters is whether they value Christian community and are moving towards it.

If nobody else knows their spiritual life, you may be the only one observing it. And that is a weak place from which to make a marriage decision.

15 · Direction

The fifth question may be the most important, because a yoke is about movement. It is direction: where are they going?

The prophet Amos asked, "Do two walk together, unless they have agreed to meet?" His question was about God and Israel, not marriage. But the principle applies by analogy to any partnership: two people cannot walk together for long unless they are going to the same place.

If they sense a calling, could you share it? If you sense one, could they? And direction matters more than starting point. A newer believer with a real hunger to grow may be a better match than a lifelong church member who stopped growing years ago. Ask where they will be in ten years if they keep walking as they are, and whether you would want to be beside them.

16 · Point 3: Four mistakes (1 and 2)

Our third point is four mistakes to avoid.

The first is missionary dating: dating someone who is not a Christian in the hope of bringing them to faith. Occasionally, by God's grace, it happens. But hope is not a strategy. If you are attracted to someone who is not a Christian, the honest course is to say so, to pray for them, and to keep the relationship where you are not making promises your convictions will not let you keep. You may share the gospel with anyone. You cannot build a marriage on the hope that they will accept it.

The second mistake is assuming that the more spiritual-sounding person is the safer choice. Fluent prayer, Bible knowledge and visible ministry are good things. But they are not the same as character. Spiritual vocabulary shows that someone has been exposed to Christianity. It does not prove that they have been changed by it.

17 · Four mistakes (3 and 4)

The third mistake runs both ways: demanding a finished product, or accepting an unproven one. A new believer should not be dismissed for being new. Every mature Christian was once a new one. But new faith needs time to take root on its own, apart from a romance, especially if the person came to faith while pursuing you. What matters is direction and teachability: are they growing, and can they receive correction?

The fourth mistake is forgetting that the yoke has two sides. Everything in this lesson applies to you as well. Is your own faith your own? Where are you going? It is possible to be so busy examining someone else's faith that you never look at your own.

18 · Pressure wearing religious clothes

One more thing needs saying plainly. Spiritual compatibility is a serious matter, and sadly some people use it to control others.

If someone tells you that you are not spiritual enough for them, and uses that to keep you anxious and striving, or if they use "equally yoked" to override your judgement, saying "a real Christian would trust me" or "if you were walking with God you would see this", that is not discernment. It is pressure wearing religious clothes. You do not have to prove your faith by submitting to someone's pressure. You may step back from the relationship and seek support from someone trustworthy. Lesson 6 deals with this fully, but if you recognise it now, you do not need to wait.

19 · Point 4: Where you are right now

Our last point is where you are right now.

If you are not dating anyone, this lesson is a gift of time to look honestly at your own faith.

If you are already dating someone who is not a Christian, you may be listening with a heavy heart. You are not being condemned. But you are being asked to be honest, with yourself, with them and with God, about where this can lead. That is a conversation worth having with a pastor or a mature Christian friend, before it becomes any harder to have.

And if you are already married to someone who does not share your faith, this lesson is not written for your situation, and it must not be used as permission to leave. In First Corinthians chapter seven, Paul tells a believer not to divorce an unbelieving spouse who is willing to stay. Seek wise pastoral support for living faithfully where you are. This does not mean you must remain in an abusive or dangerous situation. If you are unsafe, seek appropriate support.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Paul's words were first about belonging to God in a pagan city. Applied to marriage, they call a Christian to marry "only in the Lord". The label is the beginning of the question, not the end. Underneath it are five questions: conversion, fruit, doctrine, church and direction. And the yoke has two sides.

21 · One step now

Here is one step for today. If you are dating someone, think of one thing you have actually seen, not been told, about their faith. Something you observed, and when. Write it down privately if you wish, or simply think it through before God.

If you are not dating, turn the question around. Name one thing about your own faith that someone close to you could actually see.
