Before Parts B and C. These are a conversation guide for two, only for a relationship where both of you can speak freely. Do not use them with someone you fear, someone who pressures you, or someone who monitors you. Nothing here is saved or shared.

Part B: Our line (talk together: unhurried, sober, and not late at night). The boundaries each of you freely chooses to share · the line you agree, in plain words · where you differ: shared physical affection goes only as far as the stricter line, and the other person may decide the difference shows incompatibility, but may not pressure, negotiate in the moment, or punish · your agreement about digital intimacy.

Part C: Our plan. Environment: the situations in which you are most vulnerable, and what you will do about each (leave earlier, meet where others are, change the evening’s direction while it is still easy). Pace and consent: how affection develops, together rather than one pressing and one yielding · what words will mean “pause” or “stop” · how you will respond, at once and respectfully, when either changes their mind · whether either has felt hurried or left holding the line alone. Accountability (your choice): whether to invite someone discreet and wise to encourage you; they need no intimate details and have no authority to monitor or control you · individual support either of you may seek without the other’s permission.

Part D: After a mutually chosen crossing. Only if it was freely chosen by both. If either person was pressured, afraid, incapacitated or unable to refuse, skip this part and go to Part E. What happened, and was it fully mutual? · Does either need medical or sexual-health advice? · What was crossed? · What made it more likely? · What safeguards need to change? · Does either feel blamed, pressured or afraid? · What confidential support would help? If the pattern repeats despite sincere changes, seek help now.

Part E: Safety statement (this stands whatever else is written)

  • Consent is clear, voluntary, informed, specific and ongoing. It is given for each occasion and can be withdrawn at any time. A prior yes, an engagement, a shared home, or a written plan is never consent.
  • Sulking, withdrawal, guilt, repeated requests, threats, or financial, housing or spiritual leverage used to obtain sexual activity can become coercion. Coercion is not a boundary problem to be solved with a better plan.
  • A person who freezes, becomes silent or stops participating has not consented. Pause and check.
  • Anything that happened to me without my consent was not my sin.
  • If pregnancy, infection, injury or assault may be involved, medical help may be time-sensitive.
  • Either person may seek confidential outside help at any time, without the other’s permission. If you fear the person’s reaction, seek support before raising it with them.

Part F: Before you turn the page. “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9). Pray that verse honestly. Then ask God for the practical wisdom Romans 13:14 describes, to make no provision, so that the conviction you hold can be kept in the ordinary rooms and hours where it is actually tested.

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · How do we honour God with our bodies?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 9, in Part Three: Dating With Your Eyes Open.

Lesson 8 was about boundaries in general. This lesson is about physical and sexual boundaries, and it is about practice: what a conviction looks like on an ordinary evening. I will picture it as a map drawn in daylight, so that the way is clear in the evening.

And we carry one question with us: how do we honour God with our bodies before marriage?

2 · The rule is clear; the practice is hard

Much Christian teaching on sex before marriage can be summarised in two words, which most single Christians have heard many times. What they have heard much less often is anything practical: how two adults who are attracted to each other, who intend to honour God, and who spend a great deal of time together, actually keep the conviction they hold. The rule is clear. The practice is where people struggle, and where a great deal of shame lives.

This lesson assumes the historic Christian conviction: that sexual intercourse and deliberately sexual activity belong within marriage. Christians may draw different practical lines around affection, such as kissing. Those differences make honest planning necessary. They do not remove the call to sexual integrity.

This course is written for adults. If you are under eighteen and feel pressured or unsafe, please speak with a safe adult or a safeguarding service.

3 · This is the will of God

Our passage comes from Paul's first letter to the Thessalonians. Paul wrote:

"For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from sexual immorality; that each one of you know how to control his own body in holiness and honor, not in the passion of lust like the Gentiles who do not know God."

4 · Sanctification: hagiasmos

The word translated "sanctification" is the Greek hagiasmos. It comes from the word for holy, the same family of words as saint and sanctify. It means being made holy: set apart for God.

Holiness is first about belonging to God, not only about avoiding something. Your body is not a problem to be managed. It belongs to the God who made it and redeemed it.

The wording of verse four has been translated and understood in more than one way. But the passage's central instruction is clear: abstain from sexual immorality, and treat others with holiness and honour.

5 · A young church in a busy city

Paul was writing to a young church in Thessalonica, a busy port city. Many of its members had "turned to God from idols", as Paul says earlier in the letter, and they were learning a new way of life in a culture with very different sexual habits.

Paul adds that "no one transgress and wrong his brother in this matter". That is a reminder that sexual sin is not only a private matter between a person and God. It can wrong another human being. And Paul ends by saying that God "gives his Holy Spirit to you". Holiness is not a burden carried alone. It is the work of God's Spirit in his people.

6 · Point 1: What Scripture says

Our first point is what Scripture says, and what it does not. To the Corinthians, in a city famous for sexual licence, Paul wrote, "Flee from sexual immorality", and reminded them that "your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you… You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body."

And Hebrews says plainly, "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled." Christian teaching has therefore understood sexual union as belonging within marriage: a bodily giving that fits a publicly made, whole-life covenant.

7 · What Scripture does not say

Now, what Scripture does not say, because much harm has been done by things attributed to it.

It does not say that sex is dirty or shameful. The same Bible contains the Song of Songs, and Paul encourages generosity between husband and wife. God made us embodied people, and sexual desire is not inherently dirty or shameful. But Scripture also calls us to govern our desires, intentions and actions in holiness. Not every desire should be cultivated or acted upon.

It does not say that your worth depends on your sexual history. A person who has sinned sexually and repented is forgiven, fully, and is not a lesser Christian, a damaged gift, or a second-class spouse. If you were taught otherwise, you were taught wrongly.

And it does not make one person responsible for the other's desire. Both people are told to control their own bodies. Each is responsible for themselves, and for not wronging the other.

8 · Not marriage by intention; not your sin

Engagement is a serious promise, but it is not marriage. Planning to marry someone does not mean you are already married. "We're basically married" is not a category Scripture recognises. Christian marriage involves entering a recognised covenant, not simply intending to do so.

And it does not say that a person who was coerced or assaulted has sinned. If sexual activity happened to you without your consent, through force, threat, pressure, or while you were unable to refuse, that was not your sin. It was a wrong done to you. There is guidance about where to find help on the page beneath this lesson.

9 · Point 2: Why "just don't" is not enough

Our second point is why "just don't" is not enough. If the conviction is clear, why do so many sincere couples fail to keep it? Partly because desire is strong, and God made it so. There is no shame in that. But largely because couples rely on willpower in the moment instead of wisdom in advance. They have a rule and no plan. They are alone, late, tired, affectionate and unprepared, and a conviction held in the abstract is very hard to act on in a particular room at a particular hour.

Paul's counsel to the Romans is to the point: "make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires." His words are about sinful desire more broadly, but the practical wisdom applies here: do not keep arranging situations in which keeping your conviction becomes unnecessarily difficult.

10 · Joseph left the house

The Old Testament tells of Joseph, who was pressed again and again by his master's wife. The Bible says: "But he left his garment in her hand and fled and got out of the house."

Joseph's situation involved persistent pressure and a serious imbalance of power, so it is not the same as ordinary dating temptation. But his immediate decision to leave shows an important practical truth: sometimes wisdom means leaving the room, rather than staying to prove your strength.

11 · Point 3: Drawing the line

Our third point is drawing the line. This course will not give a single universal line for every form of affection, because a line drawn in advance by two people, for their own situation and consciences, is far more likely to be kept. What it gives is a framework with three areas.

First, clearly sexual activity. Intercourse and deliberately sexual activity belong within marriage. That is not up for negotiation between the two of you. Second, sexually explicit digital activity: sexual messages, video and images. They are sexual activity, even when a couple is apart, and they carry a risk that nothing physical does: permanence. Third, affection requiring personal boundaries: kissing, long physical closeness, private settings. Each couple should draw lines here that respect conscience, avoid deliberate escalation, and honour the stricter boundary of either person.

And one clarification, because some teaching has hurt people here. An involuntary physical response is not itself a moral failure. Bodies respond. The question is what you deliberately seek or continue once you see where something is leading.

12 · Six questions

Here are the questions, for each of you alone and then together. Can I take part in this with a clear conscience before God? Does this deliberately seek sexual escalation? Can either person stop without pressure, guilt or punishment? Does it respect the convictions we have honestly stated? Would continuing make wise judgement more difficult? Am I hiding it because I know it contradicts what I said I believe?

These questions are for a relationship where both people can speak freely. If you are afraid of your partner's reaction, do not use this as a joint exercise. Seek individual support instead.

Some couples will draw a stricter line than others. That is allowed. What is not allowed is one person drawing the line for both, or the line being negotiated in the very moment it is being tested.

13 · Making no provision: environment and pace

So this lesson is about provision: what you decide together and in advance, so that the moment does not decide for you. A plan has four parts.

The first is environment: where and when you are most vulnerable, and what you will do about it. For many couples, that means late nights, private spaces, tiredness, or a drink. The practical response is not to pretend those situations are safe, but to plan around them: to say goodnight earlier than you want to, to spend time in places where other people are, and to notice when the evening is heading somewhere and change its direction while that is still easy.

The second is pace: how affection develops over time. Couples who agree to move slowly, and to move together, rather than one person pressing and the other yielding, keep their lines far more often. If one of you is always holding the line alone, that is a pattern worth naming.

14 · Digital and accountability

The third is digital. Never pressure anyone to create or send intimate material, never share it without consent, and think carefully before creating any of it at all, in a relationship that may not last. Messages and images can be copied and spread beyond your control. And any sexual image involving a minor is a serious safeguarding and legal matter, whoever made or shared it.

The fourth is accountability, and it is your choice. You may invite someone you trust, outside the relationship, to know your line and to encourage you in keeping it, because a boundary no one else knows about is very easy to quietly move. They do not need intimate details, and they have no authority to monitor or control you. Choose that person with care: someone discreet and wise, not someone who would humiliate you. And either of you is free to seek individual support as well.

15 · Point 4: Consent and coercion

Our fourth point is consent and coercion. Everything so far assumes two people who are each freely choosing. Some situations are not that. Consent is clear, voluntary, informed, specific and ongoing. It requires more than the absence of resistance. Silence, freezing, or eventually giving in after repeated pressure is not freely given consent. Consent concerns each occasion, and it can always be withdrawn. A previous yes, an engagement ring, or a plan agreed last month never removes the right to say no.

And pressure is not persuasion. "If you loved me." "We're engaged, it's practically the same." "You did before." Threats, guilt, repeated asking after a clear no, or using money, housing or spiritual leverage can make consent coercive rather than free. Disappointment is not coercion, but using it as a lever is. But where pressure is being used, that is not a boundary problem to be solved with a better plan. It is one of the patterns Lesson 6 described, and the response is outside help.

16 · If you have been hurt before

And if you are a survivor of abuse or assault, as many people are, know two things. First, your body may respond to closeness in ways you did not choose: freezing, going quiet, panicking. Those responses are not consent, and they are not your failure.

Second, you decide when and how much to tell the person you are dating. A partner worth having will receive what you share with care, will not press for details, and will not treat your history as a problem to manage. If telling them feels unsafe, that is information about the relationship, not about you.

17 · Point 5: When the line is crossed

Our fifth point is what to do when the line is crossed. It happens, to sincere, praying, well-intentioned couples. Grace does not erase reality, but it lets you face it honestly, without condemnation. First, be clear which of four situations you are in.

One: a mutually chosen crossing of a shared conviction. Both of you freely crossed a line you had both agreed, and both regret it. Two: one person crossed their own conviction while the other did not share it. That calls for an honest conversation about whether your convictions are compatible, and whether anyone was pressed. Three: pressure or coercion was present. That is not a shared lapse, and the person who was pressured does not need to confess anything to the person who pressured them. Seek help. And four: pregnancy, infection or another medical concern may be present. Seek timely advice from a qualified health service. Receiving medical care is not evidence of weak faith.

18 · Five steps after a mutual lapse

For a mutual lapse, five steps. First, confess honestly, to God, and truthfully with each other. "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." Seek confidential pastoral support if it would help. Accountability should never become public exposure or detailed reporting.

Second, neither spiral nor shrug. Despair says, "we've ruined everything". Indifference says, "we've gone this far, we may as well". Both are answered by grace received properly: forgiveness that is real, and that makes it possible to try again. Third, revise the safeguards. Ask what did not hold, and change it. Fourth, accept consequences, not as punishment, but as wisdom responding to what happened: perhaps less time alone.

And fifth, do not use marriage as the solution. Paul says it is better to marry than to burn with passion, and desire is one good reason to pursue marriage. But it is not permission to marry an unsuitable person, or to rush a wedding because boundaries have failed. A marriage entered mainly to solve temptation may exchange one problem for a lifelong one.

19 · Where you are right now

Sometimes the line is crossed again, and again, despite sincere changes. Then it is time to ask a larger question, with a pastor or counsellor. If one person consistently pushes, the question is about that person. If both consistently fail, that is best worked out with help rather than alone. Repeated failure is never a reason for repeated shame, and it is not, on its own, a reason to get married.

And where you are right now. If you are not in a relationship, know your own line, and your own vulnerabilities, before someone is in the room with you. If you are dating and have never made a plan, make one. And if you have crossed the line, recently or long ago, you are not disqualified. You are forgiven, if you have asked to be, and you can begin again from where you are.

And if you recognised pressure or something worse in this lesson, put the plan aside. That is not what you need. You need someone safe to talk to, and the page beneath this lesson will help you find them.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Holiness is first about belonging to God, and your body is his. A conviction is kept not by willpower in the moment, but by wisdom in advance: make no provision. Draw the line together, honour the stricter line, and remember that consent is free and for each occasion. After a mutual lapse: grace, honesty and wiser safeguards. But where pressure or coercion is present: safety and independent help, not a shared confession or another joint plan.
