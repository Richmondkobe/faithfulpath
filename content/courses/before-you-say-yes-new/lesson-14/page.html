Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Whose counsel should I seek?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 14, the second lesson of Part Four: Discernment.

In the last lesson we held the lamp of God's word up to the voices at the crossroads. In this one, the traveller does not stand there alone. I will picture it as a small circle of trusted people around the traveller: not a crowd, and not a court, but a circle of counsel.

And we carry one question with us: whose counsel should I seek, and how do I weigh it?

2 · Two ways to get it wrong

There are two ways to get this wrong, and most of us lean towards one of them. The first is to tell no one. So you make one of the largest decisions of your life inside the relationship itself, while both of you have hopes tied to the outcome.

The second is to tell everyone. Parents, friends, your small group, the person who sat next to you at church, a few hundred people online. Every opinion is gathered and none is weighed, and you find yourself defending a decision you have not yet made to people who were never asked to make it.

This lesson is about who should have a voice, what kind of voice, and how to keep the decision where it belongs: with you. And one thing first: counsel is good; control is not. If anyone is forbidding your counsel or pressuring your decision, there is guidance on the page beneath this lesson.

3 · Rehoboam asks the old men

Our passage is a story from the First Book of Kings. Solomon has died, and his son Rehoboam goes to Shechem to be made king. The people come to him with a request: "Your father made our yoke heavy." Lighten it, they say, and we will serve you. Rehoboam asks for three days.

Then King Rehoboam took counsel with the old men, who had stood before Solomon his father. And they said: "If you will be a servant to this people today and serve them, and speak good words to them when you answer them, then they will be your servants forever."

4 · Rehoboam turns to the young men

"But he abandoned the counsel that the old men gave him and took counsel with the young men who had grown up with him and stood before him."

And the young men gave the opposite advice. Speak hard to them, they said. Tell them: "My little finger is thicker than my father's thighs." And: "My father disciplined you with whips, but I will discipline you with scorpions."

5 · Counsel: sôḏ

Proverbs puts the principle in one line: "Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed." The Hebrew word translated "counsel" there is sode. It can describe confidential conversation, a close circle, or counsel shared together. In this proverb, the emphasis is on seeking wise consultation rather than relying on your own judgement alone.

The same word appears in Psalm 55, where the psalmist remembers the close counsel he once shared with someone who later betrayed him: "We used to take sweet counsel together." It shows how personal such counsel could be, and that closeness alone does not guarantee wise or trustworthy counsel.

So the counsel Proverbs commends is not a crowd's opinion or a public vote. And the proverb also speaks of many advisers: not one voice, but more than one. Yet the value lies not in the number of voices, but in their wisdom, character and knowledge. The aim of counsel is a wiser decision, not a consensus.

6 · The two circles Rehoboam consulted

Look again at the two groups Rehoboam consulted. The older men had served Solomon, and they advised Rehoboam to answer the people with kindness. The younger men had grown up with Rehoboam, and they urged him to answer harshly. Rehoboam asked the older men first. Then he rejected their counsel and followed the younger men's advice.

He answered the people harshly, and the northern tribes turned away from the house of David. The kingdom was divided. The writer also tells us that God was at work in those events to fulfil his word, and that does not make Rehoboam's choice any less his own. The narrative does not tell us his motives. But the lesson we draw from his actions is very old: ask the people who will agree with you, and call it counsel.

7 · Point 1: Why you need other voices

Our first point is why you need other voices. Lesson 4 said it: attraction changes how you interpret what you see. You cannot fully see your own relationship from inside it, any more than you can see your own face without a mirror. Other people may notice what attraction, fear or hope makes hard for you to see. They may also misunderstand what they observe, which is why counsel should be sought and weighed, not automatically obeyed.

"The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to advice." Wisdom is not having good judgement alone. It is knowing that your judgement, however good, is partial.

And counsel is not only for warning. A friend who says, "I've never seen you this settled", is giving you information too. If you only seek out the people who will find fault, that is not discernment either.

8 · Point 2: Whose voice (1): family and pastors

Our second point is whose voice, and what each one sees. Different people see different things, and knowing what each is good for keeps you from asking the wrong person the wrong question.

Parents and family may know your history well and recognise patterns before you do. Others may know very little about your adult life, or may be unsafe or controlling. A family blessing, freely given, is a real gift. But no family member can supply or remove an adult's consent to marriage.

Pastors and church leaders may offer valuable biblical and pastoral perspective. But a pastoral office is not expertise in abuse, addiction, mental health, finance or law, and good pastors refer when they need to. A pastor may know confidential things they cannot share; do not read their silence as approval. Christians understand the scope of pastoral authority differently, but on every side, the call to follow church leaders does not authorise a leader to choose your spouse or override your free consent.

9 · Whose voice (2): friends, mentors, professionals

Friends see you most often and most ordinarily. They know what you were like before, and they can tell you what has changed. Some may fear losing your time; others are so loyal they hesitate to challenge you. Weigh the quality of their judgement, not their age. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend."

Mentors and mature couples. Paul asks older believers to teach and model for younger ones. A couple whose marriage shows honesty, respect, repair and humility can offer real perspective. But longevity alone is not enough; an unhealthy marriage is not a model just because it lasted.

Professionals. A qualified counsellor can help untangle patterns within their competence. Wherever safety is in question, go to specialist services without hesitation. Prayer and observation are not substitutes for professional help where harm is possible.

10 · Whose voice (3): their people, and God

Their people: not counsellors to you, but witnesses. Where you naturally can, notice how the person you are dating is known by those who have known them long. But do not ask their friends, family or pastor to disclose confidences, investigate them, or give you a secret character reference.

And God, through prayer and his word. God is not merely one adviser among many. Christians seek him through Scripture and prayer before anyone else. Yet our understanding of what we believe he is saying remains fallible, which is why Lesson 13 insisted on humility, testing and freedom.

11 · What counsel can and cannot do

Counsel can inform you, warn you, encourage you, and show you what you cannot see. It cannot decide for you, and it cannot consent for you. And it cannot guarantee an outcome: wise people may support a relationship that later fails, and one adviser may oppose a relationship that becomes healthy.

Counsel cannot see what only you see: the private moments, the small kindnesses. You are right to weigh what only you know. But advisers cannot see every private harm either, so tell them the truth about what happens when no one else is present.

And be honest about one thing. If you are the only person who sees the good, and everyone who loves you sees a concern, the question is not whether they are all wrong. It is what you are seeing that they cannot, and whether, if you described it plainly, it would still look like a reason.

12 · Choosing your counsellors

So how do you choose? You are not looking for a crowd. You are looking for a few people who are wise: humble, with sound judgement, and aware of the limits of what they know. Honest: they will tell you what you do not want to hear, and have done so before. A person who has never disagreed with you is not a counsellor; they are an audience. Independent enough: able to separate their own preferences and interests from your decision. Knowing: they know you, and, where it comes naturally, they have seen the relationship. Discreet: careful with private information. And safe: they understand the difference between ordinary disagreement and coercive control.

Build a small, varied circle: someone who knows your history, someone who sees your life now, someone with mature Christian judgement, and, where needed, a qualified professional. Three or four is a useful guideline, not a rule. Not only people who will approve, and not only people who will not.

13 · Seeking counsel well

Then seek counsel well. Ask early, before you have decided; counsel sought after a decision is usually a request for endorsement. Ask specific questions, and different questions of different people. "What specific change have you noticed in me?" "Are you concerned about safety, compatibility or preference?" "What would change your view?" "Are you advising me, or asking me to obey you?"

Do not curate the story. If you find yourself leaving things out so that they will approve, you have already learned something. Where the relationship is healthy, let trusted people meet the person naturally, but never through secret tests or forced interviews, and never a joint meeting where safety is in question. And respect the other person's private information. What they told you in trust is not yours to hand on. But confidentiality must not prevent you from seeking help about abuse, threats or serious danger. Share only what is necessary, with an appropriate, safe person or specialist service.

14 · Point 3: Weighing what you hear

Our third point is weighing what you hear. Not all counsel is equal, and weighing it is your job.

Specific observations deserve more weight than unexplained impressions. "He goes quiet and cold whenever you disagree, and I've seen it three times" is an observation. "I just don't get a good feeling" is an impression. Still, an impression of fear should not be mocked; ask what produced it.

Is it independent? Three friends who share the same bias, or who have talked first, are one voice, not three. Do independent voices converge? When several people who have not compared notes name the same specific concern, take it very seriously. When one person objects and everyone else is at ease, examine the one: not because a lone voice is always right, but because it may be.

And is it about safety, or about preference? Weigh concern about how you are treated far more heavily than concern about whether the person is the sort your family imagined. Never dismiss a credible safety concern by outvoting it. Pause, and get specialist advice.

15 · Job's friends: sincere, and wrong

And remember that sincere counsellors can be wrong. Job's friends came to comfort him. They sat with him in silence for seven days. Then they spoke, sincerely, articulately and confidently, and they were wrong. At the end of the book, the LORD said to Eliphaz: "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has."

Sincerity is not accuracy, in counsel any more than in prophecy. Weigh counsel. Do not simply obey it.

16 · Point 4: When counsel becomes control

Our fourth point is when counsel becomes control, and when there are too many or too few voices. A voice is not a vote, and it is not a veto. Even a safeguarding professional cannot consent for you, though their assessment of risk may deserve far more weight than a preference.

Parents who demand the right to approve. Leaders who instruct rather than advise. Friends who make their friendship conditional on your decision. Sometimes that last is control. But sometimes a friend is setting a legitimate boundary, because the relationship exposes them to danger or repeated crisis. Ask which it is. Either way, it is a cost to count, not a command.

Lesson 10 drew the line for family, and it holds for everyone: honour, listen, weigh, and then decide. "For each will have to bear his own load." Paul also tells believers to bear one another's burdens. We help one another, but we cannot hand over our own responsibility. And watch for the opposite failure in yourself: gathering counsel so that, whatever happens, someone else can be blamed. That is not humility. It is abdication, and no counsellor can carry a load that is yours.

17 · Too many voices, too few voices

Too many voices looks like conflicting advice you cannot reconcile, a relationship discussed more than it is lived, and the person you are dating becoming a topic among people they have never met. The remedy is to shrink the circle. And some things are never counsel: posting relationship disputes online, sharing screenshots of private messages, passing confidential details round a prayer group, or treating an AI system as your only counsellor or safety check.

Too few voices looks like this: no one has met them; no one knows how serious it is; you notice you are avoiding certain people, or certain questions. Ask yourself why. If it is fear of a concern, that is the reason to ask, not the reason not to. If the people available are unsafe, choose others. If the person you are dating discourages it, that is information. And if it is simply isolation, a new city, a life abroad, family far away, build a circle deliberately, and start with one person, this week.

18 · A word about safety

A word about safety. The concerns here come in levels. If the person you are dating discourages counsel, ask whether it is a preference, a privacy concern, or a pattern of isolation. If they forbid or punish it, that is potential coercive control; seek help for yourself, individually. If a leader is pressuring the decision, seek advice outside that leader's influence, as Lesson 13 said. If there is violence, stalking or immediate danger, Lesson 6's safety guidance applies. And if family pressure may amount to forced marriage, do not arrange family mediation; Lesson 10's guidance applies.

And if you are afraid to tell anyone about your relationship, that fear is the most important thing to tell someone. Guidance is on the page beneath this lesson.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you have told no one, tell one person. Choose from the qualities we described, and start with the safest. If you have told everyone, choose the three or four whose counsel meets the standard, and let the rest simply be glad for you.

If counsel has converged on a concern, take it to the earlier lessons and the evidence you have gathered. If it converges on encouragement, receive that too. And if you find that the people you would normally ask are the people you have been avoiding, the Counsel Map below this lesson will help you see why.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. You need other voices, because no one sees their own relationship whole. Choose a small circle that is wise, honest, independent enough, knowing, discreet and safe, and not only people who will agree. Weigh what you hear: observation over impression, independent voices, and safety above preference. And remember: a voice is not a vote, and it is not a veto. Counsel informs your decision. It does not make it.

Keep me from hiding from the truth, and keep me from handing my decisions to others. Give the people around me wisdom to speak well, and give me courage to decide faithfully. In Jesus' name, amen.
