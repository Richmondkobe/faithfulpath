The full Reflection: Relationship Readiness Reflection (optional, 20–30 minutes)

Nothing you type here is saved. When you close this page, it is gone. You can print the page, or write your answers in your own journal. You can finish this lesson without filling in anything.

A guided self-reflection, not a test. It will not tell you whether you are “allowed” to date. Answer alone. Nothing is added up or scored.

Part A: six pressures. For loneliness, pressure from church and family, sexual desire, age anxiety, social comparison and the wish to be rescued, ask yourself: Is this pressure present for me? Has it influenced a decision I made? Do I have ways of responding to it that do not involve a dating decision?

Part B: foundations. My relationship with God is steady whether or not I am dating · I am part of friendships and a church community · I have people who would tell me the truth, and I would listen · I could say no to a relationship that was not right · I can receive correction · I am taking responsibility for my own life.

Part D: written reflection

  1. Which motive is strongest for you right now? Were you surprised?
  2. Think of a past relationship, a strong attraction, or a pattern you have noticed. Which motive was most active? What did it cause you to overlook?
  3. “If I never married, the thing I would find hardest to accept is …” What does your answer tell you?
  4. “If I never married, my life would still be worth living because …” If you struggle to complete it, that is not a failure; it belongs in a conversation with someone you trust.

  5. Which one thing would make you more ready than you are now, and what would the next step be?

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Why do you want a relationship?

Welcome to Before You Say Yes. I'm Pastor Richmond Kobe. For more than twenty years I have sat with people before, during and after their relationships, and in this course I want to walk with you the way I would walk with someone who came to my office with a big decision ahead of them.

Across twenty-one lessons we will walk a path together in five parts: before you start dating; choosing wisely; dating with your eyes open; discernment; and finally, whether to continue, slow down or walk away, right up to the threshold of engagement.

And we begin where many dating books do not begin. Not with the other person, but with you, and with one simple question: what is driving my desire for a relationship?

2 · Before the other person

Many dating books begin with the other person. What to look for. What to avoid. How to tell whether someone is right for you.

We will get there. But before you ask, "Is this person right for me?", there is an earlier question: "Why do I want a relationship now, and what am I hoping it will give me?"

That question sounds simple. It is not. Many of us have never answered it honestly, and some of us have never been asked.

3 · Your reasons shape your eyes

Here is why it matters. The motives you bring into dating shape what you are willing to overlook.

Someone who is dating because they are afraid of being alone may tolerate things a person at peace would never accept. Someone who is dating to prove something to their family will often choose differently from someone looking for a companion to build a life with.

Your reasons shape your eyes. And if you do not know your reasons, you do not yet know what your eyes may refuse to see. So before we talk about anyone else, we start with Scripture, and then we look honestly at ourselves.

4 · Not good to be alone

Our passage comes from the very beginning of the Bible. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Genesis chapter 2, verses 18 to 24.

"Then the LORD God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him.' Now out of the ground the LORD God had formed every beast of the field and every bird of the heavens and brought them to the man to see what he would call them. And whatever the man called every living creature, that was its name. The man gave names to all livestock and to the birds of the heavens and to every beast of the field. But for Adam there was not found a helper fit for him."

5 · Bone of my bones

"So the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon the man, and while he slept took one of his ribs and closed up its place with flesh. And the rib that the LORD God had taken from the man he made into a woman and brought her to the man. Then the man said, 'This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.' Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

6 · A helper fit for him

Notice the phrase God uses in verse 18: "a helper fit for him." In Hebrew it is ʿēzer kenegdô. It puts together a noun that means help, with a short expression that means corresponding to him, or suitable for him.

The noun, ʿēzer, simply means help, or one who helps. What is striking is where else the Old Testament uses it. Many times it is used of God himself. Psalm 33 says, "Our soul waits for the LORD; he is our help and our shield." There the word appears in a form that means "our help". So the word does not, in itself, suggest someone lesser. The Bible uses it of the Lord.

The second part, kenegdô, is built from a little word meaning "as" or "like", a word meaning "in front of", and the ending "him". Put together, it means "as one in front of him": corresponding to him, suitable for him. A partner who matches, face to face.

Notice what I am doing here. What the words mean is help, and corresponding to. When we say that the woman is not made lesser, that is a conclusion we draw from how Scripture uses the word. I believe it is a fair one, but it is a conclusion, and you should be able to tell the difference.

7 · Made for relationship

Now some background. Genesis chapter 1 has a refrain: God saw that it was good, and at the end, very good. Then in chapter 2 we hear, for the first time, "not good". And it is said before sin has entered the world. The man is not alone because he has done something wrong. He was made for relationship.

Notice too the order of the story. Before the woman is made, God brings the animals to the man to name. He names them all, and among them there is no one who corresponds to him. The passage lets us feel the gap before it is filled.

In its immediate setting, Genesis 2 leads to the creation of marriage. More broadly, it tells us that human beings are made for relationship, not isolation. Marriage is one good expression of that design. But it is not the only one. Friendship, spiritual family and belonging in the church are others. Scripture does not teach that every person must marry to live a complete and fruitful life. Jesus himself was not married. Neither, as far as we know, was Paul when he wrote his letters.

8 · Point 1: The desire itself is not the problem

Our first point: the desire itself is not the problem. Some Christians have been made to feel guilty for wanting a relationship at all. So let me say this plainly: the desire for companionship is not a spiritual weakness.

Paul, who was himself unmarried and valued that freedom, still wrote that "if they cannot exercise self-control, they should marry. For it is better to marry than to burn with passion." The Bible does not treat the longing for a partner as something to be ashamed of.

So if you want a relationship, you are not in the wrong. The question is not whether the desire is acceptable. The question is what else is mixed in with it, and whether that mixture is leading you well.

9 · Point 2: Seven motives

Our second point is about seven motives that can shape dating, and what each one does to your judgement.

This is not a list of sins. Most of these motives are ordinary and human, and you will probably recognise several of them at once. The purpose is not to condemn you for having them. It is to help you see them clearly enough that they stop steering you without your permission.

10 · Loneliness and pressure

The first is loneliness. Loneliness is real, and it is heavier than people who are not lonely tend to understand. Long evenings. Holidays. Good news, and no one in particular to tell.

Psalm 68 describes God as the one who "settles the solitary in a home". The psalm is celebrating God's care for the lonely and the vulnerable; it is not a promise that every lonely person will marry. But it shows us God's heart. God has not forgotten those who are lonely, and his care reaches us through friendship and the family of the church, as well as through marriage. Yet loneliness makes a poor decision-maker. When you are lonely, presence feels like love, and attention feels like care. So ask yourself: what do I value about this person, besides the relief of not being alone?

The second is pressure from church and family. Relatives ask. Pastors hint. Parents mention grandchildren. It is often well meant. But if you catch yourself thinking, "At least my mother would stop asking", you are not choosing a spouse. You are trying to end a conversation.

Paul taught that the unmarried person can be free to give undivided devotion to the Lord. He did not say singleness is better for everyone. He said it is not a deficiency.

11 · Desire and age

The third is sexual desire. Christians are often shy to name it, yet the Bible names it without embarrassment. For those who want to honour God with their bodies, wanting to marry is one honest response to that desire.

But strong desire is not, by itself, a reason to marry a particular person. Desire can make nearness feel like compatibility. So ask yourself honestly: if the physical attraction were removed, what would be left?

The fourth is age anxiety. "I'm thirty-two." "I'm thirty-eight." Some of this is simply true; life stages do pass. But anxiety about age can lower your standards while telling you that you are being realistic. It whispers, "This may be your last chance." Wisdom answers, "A bad marriage is not a chance. It can bring sorrow that lasts far longer than a season of waiting."

12 · Comparison and rescue

The fifth is social comparison. Engagement photos. Wedding albums. A younger brother or sister who married first. Comparison does not tell you what you want; it tells you what other people appear to have. You see a photograph, not a whole life. Some of the marriages you envy from the outside are struggling on the inside, and you cannot know which. There is no schedule you have failed to keep.

The sixth is the wish to be rescued. Some people hope a relationship will fix something: money troubles, a difficult home, a sense of being stuck. But a relationship adds a person to your life; it does not replace the work of building one.

Genesis 2 speaks of leaving and holding fast: a new bond between two people who build together, not one person becoming the other's rescue.

13 · Genuine readiness

And the seventh, which I say gladly: genuine readiness. Some people want a relationship because they are ready for one.

They are learning to receive their identity from Christ rather than from their relationship status. They have real friendships, they take responsibility for their lives, and they are growing in honesty before God. Their circumstances may not be perfect. But they are not asking another person to become their whole identity, security and purpose. Readiness means responsibility and teachability, not a perfectly organised life.

And nobody is entirely in this category. Every honest person has some loneliness, some pressure, some anxiety mixed in. The goal is not to purify your motives until only readiness remains. The goal is to know the mixture, so that when a decision comes, you can tell which voice is speaking.

14 · Point 3: Contentment is learned

Our third point is an honest word about contentment. Some of you have been told that the answer to all this is simply to "be content in Christ" and stop wanting a relationship. I want to be careful here.

Paul did write, while he was in prison, "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content." But notice that he says learned. Here is how I would put it, from what Paul describes: contentment is not the absence of desire. It is the presence of trust.

Paul was content in prison and content in plenty. He did not stop preferring freedom to chains. He learned that his peace did not depend on which one he had.

That is the standard for singleness too. Contentment does not mean you have stopped wanting a spouse. It means your sense of who you are, and whether God is good to you, does not rise and fall with whether one arrives.

15 · Free to say no

And here is why that matters so much. A person who has that kind of contentment can date carefully, because they can afford to say no. A person who has not yet learned it may struggle to say no, because every no can feel like a door closing on their last hope.

That is why this lesson comes first. Recognising warning signs, holding boundaries, discerning wisely, walking away when you must: a vital part of all that lies ahead is learning that you can remain secure in Christ even when a relationship does not work out. Contentment is not the opposite of dating well. It helps us date wisely, because it gives us freedom to say no.

16 · Point 4: Where you are right now

Our fourth point: where you are right now. If you are already in a relationship, please do not skip this lesson. These questions are more useful, not less, when there is a real person on the other side of them. You can ask: which of these motives helped me say yes to this person, and would I still say yes without it?

And if you come to this after a painful ending, a broken engagement, a divorce, or a relationship that hurt you, some of these motives may weigh more heavily. Loneliness after loss is not ordinary loneliness. Be gentle with yourself. You are not being graded. And if anything that surfaces feels too heavy to carry alone, that is a good reason to talk with a pastor, a counsellor or a trusted friend before you go further.

17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. The desire for a relationship is not the problem; God made us for relationship. Seven motives can shape our dating, and most of us carry a mixture of them. Contentment is learned: not the absence of desire, but the presence of trust. And wherever you are right now, single, dating, or after an ending, these questions are for you.

18 · One step now

Here is one step for today. Look back over the seven motives: loneliness, pressure from church and family, sexual desire, age anxiety, comparison, the wish to be rescued, and genuine readiness.

Which one or two do you recognise most in yourself right now? Just name them. You can write them down privately, or, if writing is hard, simply think them through before God. Do not judge them. Knowing them is the beginning of wisdom.

When you have more time, the full Relationship Readiness Reflection is below this lesson. It is optional. It takes about twenty to thirty minutes, and nobody else will see it unless you choose to show them.

19 · Remember

Remember these two words of Scripture. "It is not good that the man should be alone." And, "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content." Both are true. You were made for relationship, and your peace can rest in Christ, whatever comes.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray, using the psalmist's words, and then our own.

"Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!"

Father, thank you that you made us for relationship, and that you are our help. You know the desires I carry, and the pressures and fears mixed in with them. Teach me to see my own heart honestly, and to rest my worth in Christ, not in whether someone chooses me. Give me patience to wait well, and courage to choose wisely. In Jesus' name. Amen.
