Transcript and Scripture references

1 · How do I know this difficulty can be worked through?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 17, and the first lesson of Part Five: Continue, Slow Down or Walk Away.

Most of this course has been about seeing clearly. Part Five is about what to do once you have seen. I will picture it as two travellers coming to three paths: continue, slow down, or walk away. This lesson is about the first of those paths. It is open, but there are a few stones on it.

And we carry one question with us: how do I know this difficulty can be worked through?

2 · A yes with your eyes open

The last two lessons may have left some of you uneasy. You read about good people who are wrong for each other, and thought, "That is not us, but we do have problems." And then you wondered whether your problems were the kind that should end things.

Many difficulties between people of sound character are ordinary friction, not reasons to end a relationship. Others reveal a deep incompatibility, an untreated personal pattern, or a safety concern. This lesson helps you tell which kind is present, and, where it is the ordinary kind, to work on it honestly. It is about a yes given with your eyes open.

And one thing first: if what you are facing is not a difficulty but harm, this lesson does not apply. Lesson 6 comes first, and there is guidance on the page beneath this lesson.

3 · Bearing with one another in love

Our passage comes from Paul's letter to the Ephesians, written while he was a prisoner:

"I therefore, a prisoner for the Lord, urge you to walk in a manner worthy of the calling to which you have been called, with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace."

4 · Bearing with: anechomai

The phrase "bearing with" translates the Greek anechomai. It means to bear with or tolerate someone patiently. In this passage, Paul is calling believers to show patience and love towards one another despite ordinary differences and weaknesses.

Paul uses the same word in his letter to the Colossians: "bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other." Bearing with is patience with ordinary weakness. It is not the carrying of harm.

5 · A word to the church

Paul wrote these words to churches: people from very different backgrounds, learning to live as one body. To the Colossians he says the same thing in another way: "Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience", and "above all these put on love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony."

So be careful how we use these words. "Bearing with one another" is Christian patience within the church. It is not a command to remain in every dating relationship. But the virtue it names applies wherever imperfect people share life, and dating is where you find out whether two people can practise it together.

6 · Point 1: Continuing is a decision, not a drift

Our first point is that continuing is a decision, not a drift. Many relationships are never actually decided. They simply go on, because ending them would take a conversation nobody wants to have, and marrying would take a conversation nobody is ready for. Months pass. The relationship becomes a habit, then an assumption, then a wedding. Nobody ever said yes; they just never said no.

A wise yes is a deliberate one. It says: I have looked at what is difficult here, I have named it, I believe it can be carried or changed, and I am choosing to keep going on that basis.

7 · A yes that can be reviewed

The difference matters, because a deliberate yes can be reviewed. A drift cannot. There is nothing to review, only a growing sense of obligation.

So if you cannot yet say what you are choosing to continue despite, or towards, go back to Lessons 15 and 16 first. A yes with nothing named is not a decision. It is a postponement.

8 · Point 2: Four kinds of difficulty (1)

Our second point is four kinds of difficulty. Not every problem is the same problem, and treating them all the same way wastes time and, sometimes, causes harm. Most difficulties fall into one or more of four categories.

The first is a skill difficulty. You both want the same outcome, and you lack a method for reaching it. You both want to handle conflict well, and you are handling it badly. Skill difficulties generally respond to effort, because the destination is shared; what is missing is a way of getting there.

The second is external strain. Health, grief, work, immigration, family or financial pressure is bearing on one or both of you, and the relationship is absorbing the weight. It is not a fault in the relationship, but it can distort every conversation about it. Name the strain before treating its effects as a verdict.

9 · Four kinds of difficulty (2)

The third is a personal pattern. One or both of you brings an established pattern, with anger, withdrawal, fear, dishonesty, money or substances, that has appeared before and is now appearing here. Personal patterns are not usually solved inside a dating relationship, and it is not the other person's job to solve them. They call for individual work, and often for professional help.

The fourth is a directional incompatibility. You want materially different lives or marriages. Directional differences do not respond to effort the way skill difficulties do, because no amount of trying harder changes what a person actually wants.

10 · Sort it, and sort it again

Only the first category is a straightforward repair. The second needs patience, and sometimes practical help with the strain itself. The third needs individual work that the relationship cannot do for the person. The fourth is a reconsideration, and Lesson 16 has already said what to do with it.

And the categories overlap. A conflict may begin with external strain, expose a personal pattern, and eventually reveal a directional difference the strain had been hiding. So do not sort a difficulty once and stop looking. Sort it now, work on it, and sort it again at the review.

11 · Point 3: What makes a difficulty workable: named

Our third point is what makes a difficulty workable. Lesson 16 gave a test, and it is the heart of this lesson. A workable difficulty has three features: it can be named specifically by both people, both people are willing to address it, and there is some evidence that honest effort produces change.

First, named specifically. "We fight a lot" is not a named difficulty. "When one of us is hurt, we both withdraw, and the silence lasts for days" is. You cannot work on a fog. Specificity also separates a difficulty from a label. "They lied about debt three times" is specific. "They are a terrible person" is a verdict, and verdicts cannot be worked on. And being specific does not mean pretending every problem is equally shared. Naming it honestly may mean naming one person's conduct.

12 · Mutually addressed

Second, mutually addressed. This is the feature most often missing. One person sees the problem, names it, reads about it, prays over it, raises it. The other agrees it is a problem, in the sense that they do not argue, and then nothing changes, because they never took it up as their own. A relationship in which one person carries all the effort is not being repaired. It is being managed, and the manager will eventually run out.

Mutual does not mean equal in every season, and it does not mean equal responsibility. If one person repeatedly lies, the other's task is not to accept half the blame. Mutual work means that both people treat the difficulty as real and act on their own part of it, not that both pretend the parts are the same size.

13 · Evidence of change

Third, evidence of change. Not a promise of change; evidence. Promises are cheap at the point of crisis and expensive to keep afterwards. What you are looking for is something that actually happened differently. The silence lasted a day instead of four. The apology came without being extracted. Ordinary, repeated, small change is far better evidence than one grand gesture, because it shows the person can do the thing without an audience.

One qualification. When a difficulty has only recently been identified, the absence of evidence is not automatically unwillingness. Nobody can show sustained change in a problem they learned about last week. Agree on a specific response, allow a period in which change could reasonably show, and during that time, do not move towards engagement merely on the strength of good intentions.

14 · What movement looks like

It is tempting to say that a workable difficulty, worked at, simply gets better. That is too neat. Movement is usually uneven: better for a month, then a setback, then better again. And a brief improvement immediately after threatened consequences is not necessarily sustained change. Someone who behaves differently for three weeks after hearing "I will leave if this continues" may have begun to change. But three weeks of improvement does not establish a lasting pattern. Look for change that continues when the immediate crisis has passed.

So the evidence you are looking for is a cluster. The relationship is safer and more honest than it was. The problem is recognised earlier, by both of you. Recovery afterwards is quicker and less costly. The change has lasted outside a crisis. The burden on the other person has reduced. And where help is needed, there is willingness to seek it. Two people trying hard in a relationship that leaves both of them lonelier is not a repair in progress.

15 · Point 4: Continuing well (1)

Our fourth point is continuing well. Suppose the difficulty looks workable and you have decided to continue. What does continuing with wisdom involve?

Say it out loud, together. Tell the other person what you have decided, what you have named, and what you hope will change, and let them do the same. This is not a secret performance review or a disguised ultimatum. A relationship in which one person is privately assessing the other is not a partnership.

Agree what you are working on: one or two priorities, not everything. Couples who try to fix six things at once fix none of them, and conclude that effort does not work. Begin with what is most urgent, or most likely to unlock wider change. Then agree what change would look like: not perfection, but something observable, like "we will talk within a day rather than a week." And make it realistic. A person living with trauma, illness, neurodivergence or a demanding schedule may need a different pace, and a measure that ignores that becomes a stick rather than a guide.

16 · Continuing well (2)

Get appropriate help. Seek it when a pattern is entrenched, keeps damaging the relationship, involves addiction or mental health, or does not respond to what you have tried. Match the help to the problem: a pastor for spiritual support, a qualified counsellor for emotional or relational patterns, specialist services for addiction, debt, legal questions, or coercion. Whatever the problem, the dating partner must never become the other person's treatment programme.

Keep your counsel informed. Tell appropriate advisers what you have decided and why. But do not ask friends to monitor, test or report on the person you are dating. They are not a community project.

And set a review point suited to the problem. Some habits may show movement within weeks; recovery, financial repair or longstanding patterns may take much longer. A review date exists to prevent indefinite waiting, not to create an artificial deadline. Lesson 18 gives a structure for it. And do not become engaged while a major unresolved concern is still being tested. Engagement is not proof that a problem has been solved.

17 · When continuing is the wrong decision (1)

I have written this lesson for relationships worth the work. But it would be a failure of care not to say clearly when a yes is the wrong answer, because the desire to make things work is one of the most easily misused instincts a Christian has.

Do not continue because ending feels like giving up. Marriage calls people to persevere through ordinary hardship, but it never makes violence, coercion or abuse spiritually acceptable. And in dating, perseverance must not be confused with an obligation to turn every relationship into a marriage. Before the vows, stopping is not giving up. It may be the discernment you were praying for.

Do not continue because you have already invested so much. Years, money, a shared church: none of it obligates you to invest more. What you have invested deserves honest consideration, but it does not obligate you to continue. The decision must be based on what the relationship is now, and whether it is wise to move forward. And do not continue on the strength of a promise. A promise may begin change, but it cannot substitute for it.

18 · When continuing is the wrong decision (2)

Do not continue indefinitely where the other person acknowledges a serious problem only when a breakup is threatened, and then repeatedly refuses the help or action it requires. Acknowledgement that exists to prevent an ending is not the same as taking responsibility.

Do not continue because you are afraid there will be no one else. Lesson 20 is for that fear. It is a real fear, and it is not a reason.

And do not use this lesson at all if what you are facing is not a workable difficulty but harm. If there is fear, coercion, controlling behaviour, intimidation, violence, or a sustained pattern of degrading contempt, do not use the language of "working on it" to rename harm as a shared repair project. That language is exactly what keeps people in danger. Repair assumes two people of goodwill facing a shared problem. Where that fails, go back to Lesson 6, and if you are afraid, use the safety guidance there first.

19 · A word on grace

Everything I have said may sound like a project plan. But a relationship is not a problem to be solved, and enough work does not make any pairing workable. The reason to continue, when continuing is right, is not that the difficulties have been engineered away. It is that you have seen the other person clearly, their faults, their habits, the friction between your two lives, and you still choose them, and they still choose you, and both of you are willing to keep being changed.

Be careful what you take forbearance to mean. Christian forbearance means responding patiently to ordinary human weakness while continuing to speak truth in love. It does not mean carrying another adult's responsibilities, concealing harm, or accepting conduct that destroys safety or dignity. Every good marriage is built on it, and dating is where you find out whether you both can.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Continuing is a decision, not a drift. Know which kind of difficulty you are facing: a skill, a strain, a personal pattern, or a difference in direction. A workable difficulty is named, mutual, and showing evidence of change. And if you continue, continue well: out loud, together, with help, with counsel, with a date to look again, and never as a way to rename harm.

Lord, thank you for the gift of patience. Help me bear with ordinary weakness in love, without carrying what is not mine to carry.
