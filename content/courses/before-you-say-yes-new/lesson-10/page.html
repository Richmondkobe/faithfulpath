Transcript and Scripture references

1 · How much does family matter?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 10, in Part Three: Dating With Your Eyes Open.

This lesson is about families: what they teach us, what we owe them, and how a new home can honour them without being ruled by them. I will picture it as two family homes, with a road from each, and a new home where the roads meet.

And we carry one question with us: how much does their family, and mine, matter?

2 · You marry into a family

You do not marry a family. But you marry into one, and you marry someone who was formed by one. Both of those facts will be in the room for the rest of your life, and it is better to look at them now, while you are still deciding, than to discover them at the first family gathering after the wedding.

This lesson is for people from very different cultures. In some, families are involved from the first conversation, and marriage joins two households as much as two people. In others, families are consulted late, or barely at all. Whatever the culture, family involvement should never replace the free choice of the two people marrying.

Most of this lesson is about ordinary family differences. But some situations are different, and there is guidance about them on the page beneath this lesson.

3 · Leave and hold fast

Our passage is one of the foundation verses on marriage in the whole Bible. The verse comes at the end of the account of the creation of the woman in Genesis chapter two.

"Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

4 · Leave and hold fast: ʿāzaḇ and dāḇaq

The verse turns on two Hebrew verbs. The first, translated "leave", is the Hebrew azav. It can mean to leave, depart from, or forsake. Here, it points to the formation of a new household and a change in primary loyalty. Picture someone setting down one bundle in order to carry another.

The second is davak, translated "hold fast". It means to cling to something, to stay joined to it. The same verb is used of Ruth, who refused to leave her mother-in-law: "Ruth clung to her."

So the verse is about a change of first loyalty. It is not a word about distance, and it is not a command to stop loving or honouring your parents. A new household is formed, and it comes first, while the families it came from are still loved.

5 · Before any culture

Notice where the verse stands. In the story, the first man has no father and mother to leave. So the narrator steps forward and draws a lesson for every marriage that will follow: "Therefore." The verse comes before any human family tradition existed, and Jesus quoted this verse when he was asked about marriage.

The other stories in this lesson, Rebekah and Ruth, come from a very different social world, where families were deeply involved in every marriage. Scripture does not give us a single modern courtship system. But it does give us a pattern that every culture can recognise: a new household, freely chosen, and honour for the families behind it.

6 · Point 1: What a family teaches

Our first point is patterns: what a family teaches. Everyone learned how relationships work before they were old enough to choose what to learn. How conflict was handled: shouting, silence, talking it through, or never at all. How affection was shown, or whether it was. How money was talked about. Who made decisions. What faith looked like at home, as opposed to at church. What was kept secret, and from whom. What brought honour, and what brought shame.

These lessons often become our default responses. But they are not our destiny. Discipleship, counselling and deliberate practice can all change them. Some people repeat their family's patterns; some react so hard against them that they end up in the opposite ditch.

7 · Not "Was it a good family?"

So the question about a person's family is not, "Was it a good family?" A warm family is not automatically a green flag, and a difficult one is not automatically a red one. A person from a loving home may have absorbed patterns that would not work in your marriage. A person from a painful home may have looked hard at what they were given, kept what was good, and set down the rest.

Three questions get at this. What did they take from their family, and do they know it? What did they reject, and have they replaced it with something good, or only with its absence? And what have they not looked at yet? That third question is the one a marriage will eventually ask, whether or not anyone asks it now.

8 · Two cautions, and you too

Two cautions. First, insight alone does not prove change. A person may describe their family pattern accurately and still repeat it. Look for both self-awareness and evidence of a different practice.

Second, looking at a family should not become fault-finding. Ask what each family did well, and what deserves to be carried forward: hospitality, perseverance, generosity, shared faith, respect for elders, care for children, the ability to repair after conflict. A healthy new home is built not only by rejecting what was harmful, but also by keeping what was good.

And of course, the same questions apply to you. You cannot see another person's patterns clearly while your own are still invisible to you.

9 · Point 2: Honour, and what it does not mean

Our second point is loyalties: whom a person belongs to, and what they owe. Scripture calls God's people to "Honor your father and your mother". Christians understand that honour as enduring, though it does not take the same form in every season of life. Adult honour does not necessarily mean obedience, unlimited access, financial compliance, or remaining exposed to abuse.

But it is real, and in much of the world it carries obligations: financial support for parents, care for them in old age, school fees for younger brothers and sisters. Paul's words to Timothy are strong: "if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith." Paul is speaking in a context of real family need and responsibility, especially care for widows. His words do not mean that every financial demand from a relative must be met. Genuine needs, available resources and the responsibilities of a new household must all be considered.

10 · Leaving in two kinds of culture

In extended-family cultures, leaving does not mean cutting off. It means the new household now comes first in loyalty and decision, while the wider family remains close and honoured. In more individual cultures, the danger runs the other way: leaving can slide into abandoning, and the honour due to parents can be quietly dropped.

And note that dating does not yet create the loyalty of marriage. A boyfriend or girlfriend should not demand the priority, money or authority that belongs to a spouse. The question now is whether each of you appears able to form that new loyalty if you eventually marry. The goal is neither automatic compliance nor automatic distance.

11 · Signs of imbalance, both ways

Some signs of imbalance, in both directions. They cannot make a decision, including about the relationship, without their parents' approval. Not consultation, approval. Their family's hostility towards you is dismissed as unimportant, and they will not address it. Or they are hiding the relationship from their family, and the reason is not safety.

Or, in the other direction, they are entirely distant from their family. Here, be careful. Distance from family may be necessary, and no one owes a dating partner the details of trauma before they are ready. As trust grows, you will need enough truthful information to understand how the situation could affect a shared future. But "I am not ready to give details; the distance is necessary" can be an honest and sufficient answer at an early stage. And a person who has left an abusive home owes you no reconciliation with it.

12 · Rebekah: "I will go"

Scripture gives us a picture of family involvement that also honours the individual. Abraham's servant travels a long way to find a wife for Isaac, and meets Rebekah and her family. After the negotiations, the Bible records this: "They said, 'Let us call the young woman and ask her.' And they called Rebekah and said to her, 'Will you go with this man?' She said, 'I will go.'"

This belongs to a very different social world, and it is not a blueprint for modern courtship. The narrative records Rebekah being asked whether she would go, and her answer matters. It does not tell us everything about her circumstances, or establish a complete model of consent. For us today, the principle is clear: family involvement must never replace a person's own free and informed decision about marriage.

13 · Ruth: belonging can be chosen

And there is Ruth. After her husband died, her mother-in-law Naomi was going home to Bethlehem, and urged her to stay in her own land. Orpah kissed her mother-in-law and turned back, "but Ruth clung to her". That is the same word as "hold fast" in Genesis. And Ruth said, "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God."

Her words are a voluntary expression of extraordinary loyalty after bereavement. They are not a command for anyone to abandon their own people or to submit to a new family. But her story shows that belonging across family and cultural lines can be chosen, with courage and with faith.

14 · Point 3: Protecting what you are building

Our third point is boundaries: protecting what the two of you are building, not from family, but within it. In dating, you are not making those decisions yet, but you should be finding out whether the two of you could make them together.

Who decides? Can the two of you make decisions together while seeking counsel, or is a parent given the final word whenever you disagree? What flows outward? How much of your private life will each of you share with family? What flows inward? Financial support to family is honourable, but a spouse should not discover after the wedding that a third of the household income is already committed. And the children, if there are children: which languages and customs to pass on, where you would worship, and how grandparents would take part.

You do not need every detail in early dating. But before engagement you need real agreement about children, where you will live, money owed to family, family involvement, and how final decisions will be made. "We will work it out later" is not enough where the expectations are already opposed.

15 · Meeting the family

At some point you will meet them, and they will meet you. A few things are worth noticing, without turning a family lunch into an inspection. Notice how the person you are dating treats their parents and siblings, and how they are treated.

Notice how the family responds to you. Welcome is a gift. Coolness may be prejudice, protectiveness, culture, or simply reserve, and you cannot know which from one meeting. Hostility is worth naming to the person you are dating. Their response matters greatly. And if the hostility is severe or persistent, you must also consider what it would mean for your safety and future marriage. And notice how they behave around their family. Many adults slip back into familiar roles at home. The question is whether their adult judgement and their own voice remain.

But be fair: one visit shows you one day, and cultures express warmth very differently. And do the same, honestly, about your own family.

16 · Point 4: When families object

Our fourth point is what to do when families object. Here are five kinds, and an objection may contain more than one, so ask which part is supported by evidence and which part is shaped by fear, prejudice or control.

First, specific conduct or safety concerns. They have seen something concrete: how you are treated, how you have changed. Listen carefully. Families are sometimes wrong, but they are often the first to see a pattern that attraction has hidden from you. Second, practical concerns: money, distance, caring for relatives, immigration, education, health. These are about facts, and facts can be looked at together. Third, faith or conscience. A difference of church background is not automatically prejudice. It may be about real differences in belief, worship or how to raise children. Look at what is actually being said.

Fourth, prejudice or status: objection to a person's ethnicity, tribe, nationality, caste, class, disability or family background, with no concern about their character. And fifth, control: a parent's demand to make an adult's decision for them.

17 · Prejudice, control and customs

A family's prejudice does not make a relationship wrong. But serious or persistent hostility can create real costs, and sometimes real danger. You are free to consider whether the relationship can continue safely and wisely. And prejudice does not become wisdom because it comes from a parent. And control? An adult may honour parents through listening, gratitude, care and respectful speech, without giving them final authority over whom to marry.

Where customs such as bride price, dowry, or a family's formal blessing carry real weight, dating is the time to find out what each family expects, and whether you could honour it. A couple who discover at engagement that the two families' expectations cannot be reconciled have found out too late.

And be clear about what customs cannot do. No payment makes a person someone else's property, or removes their right to say no. An arranged marriage and a forced marriage are not the same. In an arranged marriage, both people remain free to accept or refuse. Where refusal is removed through threats, violence, confinement, shame, money or immigration pressure, the arrangement has become coercive.

18 · A word about safety

Family is where some people learned that love and harm can come from the same hands. If the person you are dating comes from an abusive family, your role is to support them, not to fix the family or insist on reconciliation. If their family is hostile or cruel to you, it is not your job to win them over, and a partner who expects you to absorb mistreatment for the sake of peace is asking too much.

No family can consent to a relationship for you, and none can force you into one. If forced marriage, confinement or threats may be involved, do not begin by arranging a family meeting, asking a community leader to mediate, or telling the relatives involved. That can increase the danger. Contact a specialist service privately, using a safe device. If travel is being planned and you fear being taken somewhere against your will, get specialist advice before you travel. Guidance is on the page beneath this lesson.

And when "my family expects" is used again and again to end a discussion, it has stopped being honour, and become the kind of pressure Lesson 6 described.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you are not yet dating, know your own family's patterns, strengths and expectations before you meet someone whose family will ask about them. If you are dating and these conversations have not happened, start them: not as an interrogation, but over time, in ordinary talk. And if the families have objected, sort the objection honestly. Treating prejudice as wisdom, or wisdom as prejudice, will cost you.

And if this lesson has touched something painful in your own family story, be gentle with yourself. You do not need a family story that looks like anyone else's. You are only asked to know it, and to bring it honestly into the light, at a pace that is safe for you.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Leave and hold fast: a marriage forms a new first loyalty, while honouring the families behind it. Know what each family taught, and keep what was good. Find out now how decisions, money, home, children and customs would work. And sort objections honestly, remembering that no family can consent for you.

22 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Father, thank you for the families that shaped us, with all their strengths and all their wounds. Help me honour what is good in my family, and change what is not. Give me wisdom to understand the family of the person I may marry. Protect anyone who is being pressured or controlled. And help me build a home that receives the best from both families, without being ruled by either. In Jesus' name, amen.
