Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Is it unloving to have limits?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 8, and the first lesson of Part Three: Dating With Your Eyes Open.

Part Two was about choosing wisely. Part Three is about what happens once you are dating, and it begins with boundaries. I will picture a boundary as a garden with a low fence and a gate that opens. Not a wall, but a line that protects something good.

And we carry one question with us: is it unloving to have limits?

2 · Two uneasy reactions

The word "boundaries" makes some Christians uneasy. It sounds secular, or selfish, or cold, as though love were being replaced by a set of rules. Others have seen the word used as a weapon: "I'm setting a boundary" as a way of ending every conversation and never being questioned. Both reactions are understandable, and both miss what a boundary actually is.

This lesson is about boundaries in dating. Not physical boundaries, which have their own lesson next, but the emotional, spiritual, digital, financial, time and relational limits that shape whether a relationship is healthy.

It is called "without shame" because shame is what most often stops Christians from having boundaries at all, and shame is also what is most often used against them when they do.

3 · Simply Yes or No

Our passage comes from the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus said:

"Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'; anything more than this comes from evil."

4 · Yes, yes; no, no

In the Greek, Jesus' words are very simple: nai nai, ou ou. Literally, "yes, yes; no, no". A plain yes, and a plain no.

People in Jesus' day often stacked oaths on top of their words to make them sound believable: by heaven, by Jerusalem, by my own head. Jesus says that a disciple should not need any of that. Your yes should simply mean yes, and your no should simply mean no.

This is speech that can be trusted as it is.

5 · About honest speech

In its setting, this verse is about truthful speech and unnecessary oaths. People added oaths to make their words sound more binding. Jesus calls his followers to a life in which every word is true. So the verse is not first about dating, and it is not a rule about boundaries.

But by application, it reminds us that Christian speech should be honest, rather than manipulative or deliberately unclear. A person whose yes and no are plain is easier to love, not harder, because they can be known. A person who never says no is not more generous. They are harder to know, and often quietly resentful.

6 · Point 1: What a boundary is

Our first point is what a boundary is. A personal boundary identifies what you will take part in, what access you will give, and what you will do when a legitimate limit is crossed.

Notice what that does not say. A boundary does not guarantee another person's compliance. You cannot make someone else behave well by announcing a boundary. Requests and agreements may go alongside it, but your boundary is ultimately about the choices that are yours to make.

7 · Four different things

Much confusion comes from using one word for four different things. A boundary is what I will do to protect a legitimate limit: "I will end the call if I am being shouted at." A request is something I ask you to consider, which you may accept or decline: "Would you put your phone away while we have dinner?" An agreement is a commitment we both freely make: "We agree not to post photographs of each other without asking." And control is an attempt to remove another person's reasonable freedom through pressure, monitoring or punishment: "You must give me your passwords so I can check whom you contact."

Not every statement about another person's conduct is controlling. Relationships need requests and agreements. The question is whether both people keep the freedom to discuss, to decline, or to recognise that they are not compatible, without pressure or punishment.

8 · Not a wall, a test, or a punishment

Three things a boundary is not. It is not a wall. A wall keeps everyone out. A boundary is a line that lets the right things in at the right pace. It exists so that closeness can happen safely, not so that it cannot happen at all.

It is not a test. You do not set a boundary to see whether the other person will respect it, though you will certainly learn that. You set it because it is true, because it protects something that matters.

And it is not a punishment. A consequence protects your participation or your safety. Punishment aims to cause suffering, to force submission, or to get even. Ending a call when shouting begins protects your participation. And taking space because you need time or safety is not punishment. Deliberately withholding contact or affection to make someone submit is manipulation. The difference lies in the purpose and the behaviour, not simply in how long someone needs space. And you are allowed to name a limit later, after an experience has shown you that it is needed.

9 · Boundaries run both ways

And boundaries run both ways. The other person also has the right to identify and communicate their limits. That does not mean every proposed limit is wise, or that you must agree to it. Sometimes two legitimate boundaries reveal that two people are not compatible. Sometimes a supposed boundary is really an attempt to control.

But a relationship in which only one person is allowed to say "this far and no further" is not a relationship with boundaries. It is a relationship with a ruler.

10 · Point 2: He withdrew to pray

Our second point is why boundaries are not shameful. Some Christians have absorbed the idea that a loving person has no limits: that to say no, or not yet, or that is not mine to share, is to fail at love. Scripture does not teach this.

The Gospels do not use modern boundary language. But look at Jesus. Luke tells us that great crowds gathered to hear him and to be healed. And then: "But he would withdraw to desolate places and pray." Jesus did not make himself continuously available to the crowds. He regularly withdrew to pray. His example shows us that faithful love and service can include time away from other people's demands.

11 · Burdens and loads

Paul tells the Galatians to "bear one another's burdens", and three verses later says that "each will have to bear his own load". He uses two different Greek words, but we should not make them carry more meaning than the passage allows. His wider teaching brings together two responsibilities: care for one another, and take responsibility for your own conduct. This is not a modern formula for boundaries, but it reminds us that Christian love involves both helping others and acting responsibly ourselves.

Proverbs offers an image to keep, with care: "A man without self-control is like a city broken into and left without walls." The verse is about self-control, and it should not turn relationships into fortresses. Its point is that appropriate protection keeps what is valuable. Walls in that sense are not hostility. They are what makes a city a place where people can live.

12 · Only what could be withheld

There is a further point, and it is the one that most often changes people's minds. You can only give freely what you could have withheld. A gift that could not have been refused is not a gift. A closeness that had no limits was not chosen.

Boundaries are what make it possible to give yourself to another person as an act of love, rather than as an absence of alternatives. Far from being the enemy of intimacy, they are what make intimacy mean something.

13 · Point 3: Emotional and spiritual

Our third point is six kinds of boundary. This is a map, not a rulebook. The right lines will differ between people and cultures. But every dating relationship needs some line in each of these six areas.

The first is emotional: how much of your inner life you share, and how fast. Whether you become the other person's only source of support. The Song of Songs repeats a refrain: "do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases." It is poetry, not a dating timetable, but it captures the wisdom of not forcing intimacy ahead of its season. Let closeness grow at the pace of trust, and do not ask one person to be your counsellor, your pastor, your best friend and your partner all at once.

The second is spiritual. Your walk with God remains yours. Praying together can be good, but shared devotion is a powerful accelerator of intimacy. Make sure each of you keeps direct access to Christian community and trusted counsel. Neither of you holds spiritual authority over the other. If you no longer hear from God except through the person you are dating, something has been crossed.

14 · Digital and financial

The third is digital: phones, passwords, location sharing, how fast you reply, whether photographs may be posted. A dating partner is not entitled to your accounts, and demanding access can itself become controlling. Never pressure someone to create or send intimate images, and never share another person's content without consent. And if you suspect your own device is being monitored, do not simply change passwords; sudden changes can alert a controlling person. Get personal advice from someone qualified, as Lesson 6 described.

The fourth is financial: who pays, whether you lend, whether you share purchases or debts. In dating, the wise answer to most of those is "not yet". Money merged early is very hard to separate. Gifts do not purchase affection, access, or commitment. And be especially cautious about any request for money from someone you have not met in person.

15 · Time and relational

The fifth is time: how much of your week the relationship takes, and what it pushes out: work, sleep, church, friends, family, rest. Whether you are expected to be available at every hour. The person you are dating should be one part of a full life, not the whole of a shrinking one.

The sixth is relational: your friendships, your family, former partners, and who is told what about the relationship. Lesson 6's distinction applies. Privacy protects appropriate personal space. Secrecy hides what materially affects the other person's safety, consent or decisions. Be fair in both directions. A partner should not control your friendships through intimidation or monitoring. But both of you may raise honest concerns and agree on standards that apply to you both. Transparency does not mean surrendering all privacy.

16 · How to set a boundary

How do you set a boundary? First, know it where you can. Lesson 3 asked you to understand your own values and limits. But you will discover some only through experience, and you are still allowed to name a boundary after something has happened.

Second, state it plainly: not as an accusation, and not as an apology. "I don't lend money in a relationship" is enough. "I know this might seem strange, and I hope you don't take it the wrong way, but" invites negotiation of something that was not up for negotiation.

Third, say what you will do. "If the conversation turns to my past before I'm ready, I'll say so and change the subject" is a boundary. Fourth, follow through where you can, and get help where you cannot. Fear, financial dependence, trauma and coercion can make action hard, and difficulty does not make another person's violation your fault. And fifth, watch the response. Acceptance, or respectful clarification, is a green flag. Pressure, punishment, sulking, or spiritual reframing, such as "you're being legalistic", is not.

17 · Point 4: When a boundary is crossed

Our fourth point is what to do when a boundary is crossed. First, name it, calmly and specifically: "We agreed we wouldn't message after eleven, and last night you called at one." Not, "you never respect me."

Second, consider both the crossing and the response. Severity may make one incident decisive. In less serious situations, tell a misunderstanding from a lapse from a pattern. A person who hears you, apologises and adjusts has shown you something good. Third, allow honest disagreement. The other person may think a boundary is unreasonable, and they are allowed to say so, as long as they discuss it respectfully and do not violate it meanwhile. If you cannot agree, the result may be incompatibility rather than wrongdoing. Fourth, act on what you said you would do, if you safely can. That is not punishment. It is you being the person you said you were.

And if the crossing involves coercion, intimidation or control, then this is no longer a boundary conversation. Boundaries are not a technique for making an unsafe person behave better. Seek help. There is guidance about where to find it on the page beneath this lesson.

18 · Shame in three directions

Shame attaches to boundaries in three directions. First, shame for having them: "a better Christian wouldn't need limits." That is false. Limits are how finite people love each other well.

Second, shame used against you for keeping them: "You're cold." "You're legalistic." "If you trusted me." If someone uses ridicule, spiritual pressure or withdrawal to make you abandon a reasonable boundary, that behaviour is theirs to answer for. You may still examine the wisdom of your boundary, without accepting manipulation.

And third, shame at yourself. If you failed to keep one of your own limits, the answer is not grim resolve but grace. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." Draw the line again, tell the truth about what happened, and start from where you are. But if you crossed another person's boundary, grace removes condemnation without removing responsibility. Acknowledge the harm, accept their response, and change, without demanding immediate trust.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you are not yet in a relationship, the first part of the tool below this lesson is for you: know your lines, as far as you can, before someone tests them.

If you are dating and have never discussed any of this, have the conversation. It is awkward for about ten minutes, and then it is one of the most useful things you will have done.

And if you have discovered that a boundary you would have wanted has already been crossed, the answer is not despair, and not pretending. Draw the line where you are now, say so plainly, and watch what happens. But if speaking about the boundary could put you at risk, do not confront the person simply to complete this step. Seek independent support and decide what is safe.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. A plain yes and a plain no can be trusted. A boundary is about your own choices: not a wall, not a test, and not a punishment. Every dating relationship needs lines in six areas, set plainly and kept with help when it is hard. And shame has no place: not for having limits, and not for starting again.
