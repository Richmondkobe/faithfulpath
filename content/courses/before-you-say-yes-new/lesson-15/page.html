Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Beyond faith, can our lives fit together?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 15, in Part Four: Discernment.

I will picture this lesson as two travellers standing on a level piece of ground, where a foundation has already been laid. Beside it is a pile of building stones of every shape and colour, and on a distant hill, an unfinished tower. The question is whether these two could build a house here together.

And we carry one question with us: beyond faith, can our lives fit together?

2 · A foundation is not a house

Lesson 2 asked whether the two of you share a foundation. This lesson asks whether you could build a house on it.

Christians sometimes run the two together. "We're both believers, we pray together, our pastor approves" is a foundation. It is not a house. Two people can share a real faith in Christ and still want different lives: different countries, different paces, different pictures of family, different ways of handling money, conflict and time.

Being equally yoked does not settle whether two people should marry. It makes the question askable. This lesson asks it. And one thing first: these are conversations for a relationship where both of you can speak freely. If safety is in question, Lesson 6 comes first, and there is guidance on the page beneath this lesson.

3 · By wisdom a house is built

Our passage comes from the Book of Proverbs, in the sayings of the wise:

"By wisdom a house is built, and by understanding it is established; by knowledge the rooms are filled with all precious and pleasant riches."

Notice the words: wisdom, understanding, knowledge. The house is not built by feeling alone, however strong. It is built by wisdom, made steady by understanding, and filled by knowledge: the patient work of knowing each other truly.

4 · A counterpart, not a copy

When God made the first companion, he called her "a helper fit for him". You met the Hebrew word in Lesson 1: kenegdo. It means corresponding to him. We might put it this way: a counterpart, not a copy.

The Preacher also teaches the value of companionship: "Two are better than one", because "if they fall, one will lift up his fellow." This passage is about mutual support, not a requirement that two people must be different. But it reminds us that a good partnership is more than two people being alike. They must be able to help one another. And many strong marriages are built between people who differ a great deal in temperament, background and gifts.

So the question is not "Are we alike?" It is "Can we build one life out of two, with the differences we actually have?"

5 · Houses in Proverbs

In Proverbs, a "house" is more than walls. It is a household: the shared life of a family, its table and its days. And Proverbs is interested in the order in which a house is built. A few verses after our passage, it says: "Prepare your work outside; get everything ready for yourself in the field, and after that build your house."

And Proverbs knows that the same people who build a house can tear it down: "The wisest of women builds her house, but folly with her own hands tears it down." These proverbs are not a modern marriage checklist. They teach the importance of wisdom, preparation and conduct in building a household. Those principles help us ask better questions before marriage.

6 · Point 1: Compatibility is not sameness

Our first point is that compatibility is not sameness. And the question has two parts: what the differences are, and whether the two of you can handle differences at all.

The second part is often the more important. Two people who differ on many things, but can talk honestly, repair conflict, and give way to each other, will usually build well. Two people who agree on nearly everything, but cannot disagree without punishment, will struggle at the first real difference. And there is always a first real difference.

Many couples spend their dating years looking for agreement: the same music, the same plans, the same opinions. Agreement is pleasant, but it is not the foundation of a marriage. The ability to disagree well is. So as you look at the areas in this lesson, watch two things at once: the difference itself, and what happens between you when it comes up.

7 · The most important compatibility

How do you know whether you can handle differences? Lesson 7's green flags are the evidence here: mutuality, honest disagreement, and repair. Have you seen the two of you disagree, and come back to each other? When mutuality, honest disagreement and repair are present, many ordinary differences become easier to navigate. But some major differences still need agreement, and some may remain incompatible. When these qualities are absent, even a couple who agree on many things may struggle to build well. That, more than any single topic, is the most important compatibility.

Think of a recent disagreement, even a small one. Did either of you go cold, punish, or give in just to end it? Or did you each say what you thought, listen, and find your way back? Neither of you has to be perfect at this. But both of you have to be willing, and you should have seen it more than once.

8 · Point 2: Seven areas (1): temperament, communication, calling

Our second point is seven areas to look at. Think of them as a map of the ground on which a shared life is built. You have surveyed much of it in earlier lessons; here we bring it together.

First, personality and temperament. Introvert or extrovert, planner or improviser, quick or slow to decide. None of these is right or wrong. The question is whether the differences complement or grind. Does their energy restore you or exhaust you, over time and not only on a good day?

Second, communication and conflict: how each of you raises a concern, hears one, handles anger, needs space, and comes back. A person who processes out loud and a person who needs silence can build a life together, but only if neither reads the other's way as rejection.

Third, calling and direction. What does each of you believe your life is for, and could those two purposes be lived in one house? A calling to overseas mission and a calling to care for ageing parents in a home town are both good. They are not obviously compatible. Would you be sharing their calling, or supporting it, and could you support it gladly for decades?

9 · Seven areas (2): location, expectations

Fourth, location: where you would live, and near whom. Country, city or countryside, distance from each family, willingness to move. It sounds merely practical, but it is one of the most common causes of quiet, long-term unhappiness in marriage, because it is so often assumed rather than discussed. "I always thought we'd end up back home" is a sentence to say before engagement, not after.

Fifth, expectations of marriage. Who works, who manages money, how decisions are made, what roles look like, how much time is spent together and apart. Compare your pictures. Where they differ, is it a difference of preference, which can be negotiated, or of conviction, which may not be? Two people with opposed convictions about household roles may both be sincere Christians and still be unable to build the same marriage.

10 · Seven areas (3): children, lifestyle

Sixth, children. Whether, how many, when, and how they would be raised. What if children do not come? What about children either of you already has? Some of these are questions for engagement. But the first of them, whether, and the outline of the rest belong before engagement, because they are among the few differences that cannot be split down the middle.

And seventh, lifestyle: the daily shape of things. Pace, rest, food, health, hospitality, money habits, how a weekend goes, how much company and how much solitude. These are small until they are daily, and then they are everything. A person who needs quiet evenings and a person who needs a full house every night will each feel, in time, that the other is asking them to live a life that is not theirs.

11 · Point 3: Sorting the differences: Liveable

Our third point is sorting the differences. Once you can see them, sort them into three groups.

The first group is liveable. These are differences that will be part of the texture of your marriage, that both of you can accept, and perhaps come to value. Temperament. Many lifestyle preferences. Different ways of resting, celebrating, or showing affection. These require patience and humour, not resolution.

12 · Needing agreement before engagement

The second group needs agreement before engagement. These differences cannot simply be lived with. They must be settled, not perfectly, but substantially, before you commit. Children. Location. Household roles. Family obligations and involvement. Money expectations. The direction of your lives.

"We'll work it out later" is not enough here. Lesson 10 said so about family, and it is true of all of these. Settling does not mean one person simply surrenders. It means you have talked it through, each of you has said what you need, and you have reached an answer both of you can live with gladly.

13 · Incompatible

The third group is incompatible. These are differences that are real, that neither of you can honestly change, and that would require one person to give up something central for the marriage to exist: a calling, a conviction, a longing for children, a country.

These are the hardest to admit, because they can exist between two good people who love each other. Lesson 16 is about exactly that. Here, only this: an incompatibility is not a failure of love. It is information about what a shared life would cost, and honesty about it now is a kindness to both of you.

14 · Sorting honestly

Be careful in the sorting. Attraction wants to move everything into the first group. Fear wants to move everything into the third. Neither is honest. Use the evidence of the earlier lessons, and where you are unsure, use the counsel of Lesson 14. Let wise people help you put the stones back where they belong.

One more help. For each difference, ask: "If nothing about this changed for forty years, could I live with it gladly?" This is a question to help you think, not a test that decides the answer by itself. Some circumstances will change, and serious commitments still need honest discussion. If the honest answer is yes, it is probably liveable. If the answer is "only if they change", it probably needs agreement. And if the answer is "only if I stop being who God made me to be", it may belong in the third group.

15 · Point 4: The man who counted the cost

Our fourth point is counting the cost. Jesus told of a man building a tower: "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it? Otherwise, when he has laid a foundation and is not able to finish, all who see it begin to mock him."

Jesus was speaking of the cost of following him, not of marriage, and we should say so plainly. Nothing compares with that cost. But the picture of sitting down before you build is exactly right for a decision this large, and Proverbs says the same in its own way: by wisdom a house is built.

And notice what the builder does not do. He does not refuse to build because building costs something. He sits down first, so that if he builds, he can finish.

16 · Counting the cost of a shared life

Counting the cost of a shared life means asking, honestly, what each of you would be giving up, and whether you could give it gladly. Every marriage costs something. A person who moves country for a spouse, or sets aside a career path, or takes on a partner's family obligations, has counted a cost and paid it, and many are glad they did.

The danger is not cost. It is uncounted cost: the sacrifice that was never named, that one person assumed the other had accepted, and that surfaces years later as resentment. So name it. What would I be giving up? What would they? Have we said so out loud? And is the life we would build one that both of us actually want, or one that one of us has quietly agreed to for the sake of the other?

17 · What this lesson is not

Two things this lesson is not. It is not a premarital course. A premarital guide, like Talk Before You Marry, takes engaged couples through detailed conversations. This lesson asks a prior question: is there enough here to justify those conversations? If the sorting leaves you with liveable differences, a manageable list of things to settle, and nothing in the third group, then engagement, with all its deeper work, is a reasonable next step to consider.

It is also not a guarantee. Compatibility is the ground; faithfulness, repair, patience and grace are what build on it. Two very compatible people can neglect their marriage into ruin. Two people with real differences can build something beautiful by attending to it daily. What this lesson can tell you is whether you would be building on level ground or on a slope.

18 · Talking about it together

When you are ready, talk about it, alone first and then together. Share what each of you has noticed, area by area. Where do we agree? Where do we differ? Is the difference preference or conviction? Has either of us been assuming the other had accepted something we never said?

And notice, as you talk, whether you can disagree and stay connected. That is itself the most important compatibility. If you cannot say what you really think without fear, or the conversation is used to pressure you, stop. Lesson 6 comes first, and guidance is on the page beneath this lesson.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you are early, this lesson is for later. Note the areas, watch them as ordinary life reveals them, and come back.

If you are exclusive and moving towards engagement, do the reflection honestly, alone first and then together. If the third group is empty and the second group is short, you have found something worth building on. If the second group is long, that is the work of the next season, and it is good work. If the third group has anything in it, Lesson 16 is next.

And if you find yourself moving things out of the third group because you cannot bear for them to be there, stop, and take it to someone wise. The reflection is only useful if it is honest.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. A shared faith is a foundation, not yet a house. Compatibility is not sameness; the deeper question is whether you can handle difference. Look honestly at the seven areas, and sort what you find: liveable, needing agreement before engagement, or incompatible. And count the cost, out loud, before you build.
