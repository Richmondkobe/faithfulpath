Transcript and Scripture references

1 · How should I weigh a past?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 12, the last lesson in Part Three: Dating With Your Eyes Open.

This lesson is about the past: theirs, and yours. I will picture it as a road already walked, with footprints behind, some straight and some wandering, and light ahead.

And we carry one question with us: how should I weigh someone's past, and my own?

2 · Everyone has a past

Everyone you might marry has a past. Some of it will be ordinary. Some of it may include a broken engagement, a divorce, a child, an addiction, a season away from God, or harm that was done to them. You will need to know some of it. You are not entitled to all of it. And what you do with what you learn will say as much about you as about them.

Christians tend to get this wrong in two opposite directions. Some treat the past as destiny: a divorced person is disqualified, a person who has struggled will always struggle, and no amount of grace changes the verdict. Others treat the past as irrelevant: who they were does not matter, only who they are now. Neither is true, and neither is Christian.

What happened matters, especially where it created lasting obligations or affected someone's safety. What also matters is how honestly and responsibly the person has responded to it. Wisdom considers both the history and what has followed.

3 · I received mercy

Our passage comes from Paul's first letter to Timothy. Looking back on his own life, Paul wrote:

"though formerly I was a blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent. But I received mercy… and the grace of our Lord overflowed for me with the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus."

And then: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost. But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience."

4 · "I received mercy": ēleēthēn

Twice Paul says, "I received mercy". In the Greek it is one word, eleethen, from the verb that means to show mercy. Notice its shape: it is something done to Paul. Paul did not do it. Mercy was something given, not something earned.

And notice the tense of another phrase: "of whom I am the foremost." Not "I was", but "I am". Paul neither hid his past nor was ruled by it. He told the truth about it, and he told the truth about the mercy that met it.

5 · Who Paul had been

Paul's past was not a private struggle. As a young man named Saul, he approved of the killing of Stephen, and "was ravaging the church", going from house to house and dragging men and women off to prison. Then, on the Damascus road, the risen Jesus met him.

When Saul came to Jerusalem, the disciples were initially afraid of him and did not believe he was a disciple. His changed life did not remove their caution overnight. Years later, writing to Timothy, Paul says Christ "judged me faithful, appointing me to his service". His history shows the reach of grace. It does not mean that every repentant person is suitable for every relationship.

6 · Point 1: What the past cannot tell you

Our first point is what the past cannot tell you. It cannot tell you whether a person is forgiven. That is between them and God, and God's word is clear. "He does not deal with us according to our sins… as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us." And, "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come."

God's forgiveness is not ours to grant or withhold. We must not treat a person's past as proof that they are spiritually inferior. But we can still consider present conduct, trustworthiness and safety when deciding about marriage.

7 · Declining is not condemning

But forgiveness does not erase consequences, restore trust automatically, or create a right to marriage. You may recognise God's grace in someone's life and still conclude, without condemnation, that you should not marry them. Declining a relationship is not the same as denying forgiveness. Hold on to that sentence. It is the one that keeps grace from becoming pressure.

It cannot tell you their worth. A repentant person is not a lesser Christian, a damaged gift, or a second-class spouse. And it cannot, on its own, tell you whether they would be a good husband or wife. Some of the most faithful spouses alive are people whose twenties would have alarmed you. Some of the most damaging have nothing in their history that would have warned anyone.

8 · Point 2: Four questions

Our second point is what the past can tell you. It can tell you about present character, through four questions.

Truth: are the important facts being shared honestly, at the right time, and does the account stay consistent over months? A person who is honest about a difficult history is showing you something more valuable than a clean one. Responsibility: where they caused harm, can they own it without minimising or blaming others? Not every past involves fault, and a person who was harmed should never be pressed into false equal blame. Response: what concrete steps have followed? "I've dealt with that" is a claim. What they have done matters more than how well they describe it. And present effect: what obligations, risks or patterns are still active?

Truth, responsibility, response, present effect. Those four are the frame for everything that follows.

9 · Previous relationships and divorce

A few kinds of past come up often enough to need their own word. First, previous serious relationships. What ended, and why, and what did they learn? Several relationships ending in similar ways deserve careful exploration. It raises a question. It does not supply the answer.

Divorce. Is it legally final? What do they say about their former spouse: with discretion, or with contempt? Can they speak about it with enough steadiness to make room for a new relationship? Where they contributed to what went wrong, do they own it? But never require a survivor of abuse to manufacture equal responsibility for what was done to them. And understand the divorce as far as you honestly can, without contacting the former spouse behind the person's back.

10 · Christians differ: remarriage

Here, Christians differ. Christian traditions differ on when remarriage after divorce is permitted, and this course does not settle that question for you. Know your own church's teaching, and theirs, and take the question to your pastor early, before the relationship goes far.

Before engagement, both of you will also need clarity about children and custody, support obligations, property, any unresolved legal matters, necessary contact with the former spouse, and any safety concerns.

11 · Children and addiction

Children. A child is a person, not baggage. Be honest with yourself about whether you could love and commit to a child who is not yours, and to the arrangements that come with them. The child's wellbeing comes before your convenience. And dating a parent does not make you a parent to the child. Do not seek emotional authority, discipline the child, or ask the child to keep secrets.

Addiction. Recovery is a process rather than a flawless record, and it is not always a straight line. A person in genuine recovery can usually describe the structure supporting it: treatment, a recovery community, trusted support, a plan for hard days. The serious concern is not whether someone has ever struggled, but an active, unaddressed addiction with deception or refusal of help. And supporting someone in recovery does not make you their therapist or their monitor. A partner cannot be the treatment plan.

12 · Sexual history, criminal history, faith history

Sexual history. Lesson 9 was clear that worth does not live here. You are not entitled to the details, and demanding a list is a warning sign about you. But current sexual behaviour or commitments, such as a present struggle with pornography, deserve honesty. And sexual health information that materially affects the other person should be shared before engagement, with medical advice, not used to judge anyone's worth.

Criminal history. Some conduct affects safety, and that is not a matter for grace alone. Consider its nature, seriousness, how recent it was, and evidence of accountability. Where violence or sexual offending is involved, get specialist safeguarding advice. Dating discernment cannot replace a professional risk assessment.

Faith history: a season away from God, or a church left badly. What do they believe now, how long has that direction been visible, and does their life increasingly align with it?

13 · Trauma is not a past to evaluate

Some of you will be dating a survivor of abuse, assault or serious harm, and some of you are survivors yourselves. This is for both. What was done to a person is not their history in the sense we have been using. It is not something they did that reveals character. It is a wound.

Survivors get to decide when, how much, and whether to tell. A person who says early on, "There are things in my past I'm not ready to talk about", has given a complete and honest answer for that stage. Do not press. Do not treat a survivor as fragile or damaged. Many of the most whole people you will meet have done hard work with hard things.

And be careful about "consistency" here. Disclosure of trauma can be delayed for years, and memories can come in fragments. Inconsistency is not automatically deception.

14 · When someone tells you

When someone tells you, the response that helps is simple, and it is not a set of questions. "Thank you for trusting me. I am sorry this happened. It was not your fault. You do not have to give me details. What would help you feel respected and safe now?" What does not help is "why" questions, "are you healed?" or "how will this affect marriage?": not on that day.

And if you are the survivor: you owe no one your story before you are ready. As trust grows, a serious relationship will need enough of the truth to understand how what happened might shape a shared life. But the timing is yours, the detail is yours, and a partner who cannot receive it with care is showing you something about them, not about you.

15 · Point 3: What needs to be known, and when

Our third point is what needs to be known, and when. The test is this: any fact a reasonable person would need to make an informed decision about safety, legal status, children, finances, health, faith, or the life being proposed.

Early dating: nothing detailed. Before exclusivity: a current marriage, children, a current relationship with someone else, anything that creates an immediate safety risk, active addiction seriously affecting life, and any history of violence relevant to present safety. Before engagement: previous marriages and continuing obligations, custody and support, significant debt or legal matters, relevant criminal history, health information that materially affects the other person, and anything whose concealment would prevent an informed decision to marry.

What you are not entitled to, at any stage: the details of someone's trauma, a sexual history, an exhaustive account of every past relationship, or anything that is another person's confidence to keep. And disclosure is mutual, to the same standard, on the same timetable.

16 · Giving and receiving

How to receive a disclosure: with thanks for the honesty, first. Without humiliation or retaliation; what was shared in trust must never come back as ammunition. With time; you are allowed to need to think. And you remain free to slow down, seek counsel, or end the relationship. A thoughtful decision is not punishment. Keep it confidential, unless someone is in danger, a child or vulnerable person may be at risk, or the law requires action.

How to give one: at the right stage, plainly, without minimising or performing. Say what happened, what you did about it, and where you are now. Choose a setting with privacy and an independent way to leave; where retaliation is possible, a remote conversation can be safer. Then let them respond. If they cannot receive it, that is painful, and it is also information.

17 · Point 4: Neither record decides it

Our fourth point: neither record decides it. This needs saying to two kinds of listener.

To the one drawn to someone with a difficult past, wondering whether they are settling: a difficult past does not prevent someone from becoming a faithful spouse. Paul was "formerly a blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent", and yet he "received mercy". His story shows the reach of grace, though it does not mean every repentant person is suitable for every relationship.

And to the one whose record is the cleaner one: an outwardly simple past does not guarantee character. Someone who has never had to apologise and repair may be untested rather than proven. Evaluate neither person by comparison. Look for present truth, responsibility, wisdom and sustained conduct.

18 · A word about safety

A history of violence towards a partner, a child, or anyone vulnerable is not a past to be evaluated with the four questions. It is a present safety matter, and Lesson 6 applies. Do not accept "that was the old me" without evidence that is long, external and specific, and even substantial evidence of change does not oblige you to enter or continue the relationship. Watch whether present behaviour, anger, control, contempt, matches the past account.

Where a child or vulnerable adult may be in danger now: do not confront the person privately, and do not contact a possible victim to investigate. Seek specialist safeguarding advice, and contact emergency services if danger is immediate. Do not promise secrecy. If you have been asked to keep something quiet that affects other people's safety, seek confidential safeguarding advice promptly. Guidance is on the page beneath this lesson.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you are not dating, ask the four questions of yourself first, before you ask them of anyone else.

If you have learned something about their past that has unsettled you, do not decide in the moment. Ask the four questions, with evidence, and take what you find to someone wise. Then decide, remembering that declining is not condemning. If you have something in your own past that you have not told them, and the relationship has reached the stage where they should know: tell them. Choose the timing and setting carefully, and seek support if the response may be unsafe.

If serious harm was done to you, you deserve care and appropriate support. If you caused serious harm, take responsibility and seek qualified help. Where anyone's safety may be at risk, put safeguarding first. That is not a lesson's work. It is a season's.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. "I received mercy": the past is neither destiny nor irrelevant. Ask four questions: truth, responsibility, response, and present effect. Know what is needed, and when, and never demand what is not yours to know. And remember: neither record decides it, and declining is not condemning.

21 · One step now

Here is one step for today. Write one thing about your own past that grace has changed. For example: "I used to hide my mistakes; now I can name them", or "I no longer believe my past makes me second-class".

If it is safe, write it privately, or simply think it through before God.

22 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Father, thank you that you remove our sins as far as the east is from the west. Help me to see my own past as forgiven. Help me to treat other people's pasts with grace and honesty. Give me wisdom to see what still matters today, without holding against anyone what you have forgiven. And where harm has been done to me or to someone I love, bring healing and safe help. In Jesus' name, amen.
