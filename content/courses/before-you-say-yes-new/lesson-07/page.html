Transcript and Scripture references

1 · What good signs should I look for?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 7, the last lesson in Part Two, Choosing Wisely.

In Lesson 6 we looked at danger: the red flags that Christians are tempted to spiritualise. This lesson is about goodness. And goodness may be harder to see, because goodness in a person is usually quiet. I will picture it as small green flags along the path.

We carry one question with us: what good signs should I actually look for?

2 · Goodness is usually quiet

Red flags can be subtle when they are hidden beneath romance or spiritual language. Green flags can be just as easy to miss, for a different reason. Genuine goodness is often undramatic. It shows in what happens when nothing in particular is happening: a promise kept without comment, a truth told at some small cost, a no accepted without a sulk, a friend of fifteen years who still calls.

Nobody makes a film about these things. They are easy to overlook, especially if culture, or church, or past relationships have trained you to look for something more visible.

So this lesson has two jobs. First, to put in proportion the things that look like green flags but are not enough on their own. Second, to name what health between two people looks like, so that you can recognise it when it is in front of you.

3 · That your love may abound

Our passage is a prayer. Paul wrote to the church at Philippi:

"And it is my prayer that your love may abound more and more, with knowledge and all discernment, so that you may approve what is excellent, and so be pure and blameless for the day of Christ."

4 · Approve: dokimazō

The word translated "approve" is the Greek dokimazo. It means to test or examine something, and so to recognise and approve what proves to be genuine. It was used of testing metal or coins, to see whether they were what they claimed to be.

And the phrase "what is excellent" can also be translated "the things that really matter", or "the things that differ".

So Paul is not praying for a warm feeling of approval. He is praying that love would grow together with knowledge and discernment, so that believers can tell what is truly good, and recognise it when it has proved genuine.

5 · A prayer for a whole church

Paul wrote from imprisonment to believers he loved. His letter would be brought to the church and heard by the gathered believers. So this is not a prayer about dating. It is a prayer for a whole community of believers, that they would grow in love and wisdom together.

But notice what Paul assumes. Love and discernment are meant to grow together. Love without discernment is easily misled. Discernment without love becomes cold and suspicious. That is the balance this lesson needs when we apply Paul's prayer to choosing someone to marry: warmth that can see clearly.

6 · Point 1: Good signs that are not enough alone

Our first point is the encouraging signs that are not enough by themselves. None of the following is bad. Each is positive information. The point is only that none of them, alone, establishes character.

Politeness is good, but it does not establish deeper respect. It tells you a person knows how to behave. It does not yet tell you how they behave when courtesy has no audience. Church attendance may reflect sincere commitment, but attendance alone cannot prove it. Lesson 2 spent a whole lesson on this.

Romantic gestures can be sincere and beautiful: flowers, surprises, long messages, generous gifts. But an impressive gesture tells you less than the pattern around it. Gifts become concerning when they create obligation, speed up intimacy, or alternate with withdrawal and mistreatment. A gesture is a moment. Consistency is the evidence.

7 · Words, reputation and agreement

Spiritual language may reflect real faith, but fluency alone does not establish transformation. Lesson 5 said so. A good reputation matters, but popularity is not the same as integrity. Some people are widely liked because they are good; some because they have learned that being liked is useful. Time tells the difference.

And agreement can indicate shared values, but constant agreement may hide fear, people-pleasing or mirroring. Early in a relationship, some people discover what you value and reflect it back, and it feels like extraordinary compatibility. Real compatibility includes disagreement. A person who never voices a view of their own has not yet shown you who they are.

More reliable evidence comes through consistent conduct across time and circumstances. That is what the rest of this lesson describes.

8 · Point 2: Ten green flags · 1

Our second point is ten green flags. These are relational: each describes something that happens between you, not merely a quality in the other person. Each is something Scripture commends, and each is something you can observe rather than something they claim.

The first green flag is that they respect your no. You decline something, change a plan, or set a limit, and the answer is respected. They may ask what you mean, or return to the subject respectfully at a good time, but they do not treat your no as an obstacle to wear down. They do not punish you, pressure you, or make affection depend on agreement.

Where physical closeness is concerned, be clear about one thing now, before Lesson 9: consent concerns each occasion and can always be withdrawn. A person who can hear your no is showing you what it will be like to say no to them for years to come.

9 · Flags 2 and 3

The second green flag is that they make room for honest disagreement. You can disagree about something that matters, and basic respect and safety remain. The mood may change for a while; that is normal. But if either person needs time, they say so and come back to the conversation, rather than using silence as punishment. Paul's phrase is "speaking the truth in love". It assumes both: truth that may be uncomfortable, and love that does not stop while the truth is spoken.

The third green flag is that they take responsibility and repair harm. A healthy apology names what was done, acknowledges its effect, does not blame you for raising it, and is followed by an honest effort to change. Proverbs puts it in a sentence: "Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy." Confessing, and forsaking. The second word is the one to watch for.

10 · Flag 4: the master green flag

The fourth green flag is that their words and behaviour increasingly align. They say they value honesty, and they are honest when it costs them. They say family matters, and their choices show thoughtful care for the family relationships that are safe and appropriate. They say they will call, and they call.

Lesson 5 called this integrity, and named it the master window. Here it is the master green flag, because it is the one that makes everything else they say believable. James writes, "be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves."

11 · Flags 5 and 6

The fifth green flag is that they respect your separate relationships and identity: your friends, your family, your church, your work, your interests. They do not demand that you surrender your identity or abandon healthy relationships, and they are glad when you are with people they are not. They are not required to tolerate mistreatment from your family, but they do not ask you to leave the people who are good for you. And you remain yourself around them: your opinions, your humour, your convictions, intact.

The sixth green flag is emotional steadiness without demanding perfection. A plan collapses, a request is refused, and they can express disappointment without making it a licence to punish or blame. They are allowed to feel it; the flag is that the feeling does not become someone else's problem. And it runs both ways: they do not require you to be flawless either. They can bear an ordinary bad day of yours without treating it as a crisis.

12 · Flags 7 and 8

The seventh green flag is that they tell relevant truths even when it is inconvenient. They admit a mistake before you find it. They say "I don't know" rather than inventing an answer. They correct a good impression that was not accurate. They tell you the truths that affect your safety, your consent or your shared future, and they do not use privacy as permission for real deception. Paul writes, "Having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor."

The eighth green flag is that they are teachable and able to reconsider. They can consider another view, admit uncertainty, and revise a conclusion when the evidence calls for it. Some people need time to do this. But they do not treat changing their mind as humiliation. A partner who can be wrong is a partner with whom problems can be solved. A partner who cannot is one with whom every problem becomes a contest.

13 · Flags 9 and 10

The ninth green flag is that they celebrate healthy growth without controlling its direction. They are glad when you flourish, even in places they have no part. They pray for you, not only about the relationship. They may raise honest concerns, about overwork, for example, but they do not need you smaller in order to feel larger. Paul describes love as one that "does not envy or boast". The test is simple: what happens when something good happens to you that has nothing to do with them?

The tenth green flag is that the relationship allows mutuality. Both people can speak, choose, give and receive. Neither is always the one who adapts, apologises or gives way. And each of you is known by people who can be honest with you, which is where long-term friendships come in. Friendships that have lasted through change can be good evidence. Their absence is not automatically a concern: moving country, ministry, disability, caregiving, betrayal or social anxiety can all thin a person's friendships. Quality, reciprocity and openness matter more than how old a friendship is.

14 · Why these are easy to miss

Every item on that list is undramatic. None of them produces a feeling of being swept away. A person who keeps their word, accepts your no, apologises properly and lets you keep your friends may seem, in the early weeks, less exciting than a person who sends flowers and says God told them you were the one.

This is where the earlier lessons come together. Lesson 4 warned that intensity is not meaning. Lesson 5 said that charisma is not a green flag. Lesson 6 showed that the exciting behaviours are sometimes the warning ones. And here is the other side: the good ones are often the quiet ones. If you are looking for fireworks, you may look straight past them.

None of this means you should choose someone you are not drawn to. It means that when you are drawn to someone, the things to be encouraged by are the ones on this list.

15 · Green flags and the response to failure

You will not find a person who shows all ten flags all the time. Nobody does. The most meaningful green flag is not flawless conduct, but a growing capacity to recognise harm, receive honest feedback, make repair, and change over time.

That capacity is evidence, not proof. It suggests that the goodness you have seen is who they are trying, imperfectly, to be, rather than a performance that will end when the audience leaves.

But serious abuse, intimidation or coercion is not an ordinary failure to be balanced against someone's green flags. Safety comes first.

16 · Point 3: The decision to become exclusive

Our third point is a decision. At some point in a relationship that is going well, a question arises: are we exclusive?

Exclusivity means that both people freely agree to stop pursuing other romantic possibilities for a season, while they explore this relationship more intentionally. It is not engagement, it is not ownership, and it is not permission to speed up physical or emotional intimacy. It does not remove either person's freedom to slow down or to end the relationship.

In some cultures and church traditions, dating is understood to be exclusive and intentional from the start, often with families involved early. If that is your setting, the questions still apply; you may simply be asking them earlier. Either way, it is a real decision, and it deserves to be made rather than drifted into.

17 · Before you become exclusive

Because exclusivity narrows your options and deepens your attachment, it should come after a certain amount has been seen, not before. These are strong recommendations, not universal laws. Have you had enough time, in enough ordinary settings, for several of the windows from Lesson 5 to open? No abuse or immediate safety concern has been found. You have seen some of the green flags, with evidence, not impressions. There is a shared foundation in faith. Nothing in your foundational requirements from Lesson 3 has been bent. Where it is safe and appropriate, you have also begun to see each other among friends, family or trusted members of your communities. You can say no in the relationship, and you are still yourself.

And you have both said, in plain words, what exclusivity means to you, and you mean the same thing. That matters more than it sounds. Exclusive means different things to different people: about pace, about physical boundaries, about who is told, and about what you are moving towards. Two people who assume they agree, and do not, are heading for pain.

18 · Online and long-distance; safety

A word about online and long-distance relationships. Online contact can create deep attachment before ordinary life has been seen. Video calls and long conversations reveal something real, but they do not replace confirmed identity, conduct in person, and observation in different settings. Do not make financial commitments, travel into a situation of dependence, or become engaged before basic identity and circumstances have been independently confirmed. If most of what you know has come through a screen, the honest answer to several of those questions is "not yet", and that is a reason for patience, not shame.

And one more word. If there is abuse, intimidation, coercion or a credible concern for your safety, do not move towards exclusivity. Seek individual support from someone trustworthy and independent. There is guidance about where to find help on the page beneath this lesson.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you are early, and the green flags are just beginning to appear, enjoy them. If it is safe, make a private note of them with dates; you will want the evidence later.

If you are considering exclusivity and the honest answer is "not yet", that is not a verdict on the person. It is a statement about how much you have seen, and the remedy is time. If you are already exclusive and realise you agreed to something you never discussed, have the discussion now. It will be harder in six months than it is today.

And whichever is true, ask one more question: am I offering the green flags I am looking for? Can I receive a no? Can I disagree without withdrawing love? Can I apologise and repair?

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Love and discernment are meant to grow together. Encouraging signs are good, but not enough alone: a gesture is a moment, and consistency is the evidence. Watch for the ten quiet green flags, and for the response to failure. And make the decision to become exclusive with your eyes open.
