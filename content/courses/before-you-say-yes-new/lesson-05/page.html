Transcript and Scripture references

1 · How do I see who someone really is?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 5, in Part Two of the course, Choosing Wisely.

In Lesson 4 we learned that attraction may open a door, but discernment decides whether to walk through it. In this lesson we ask what discernment looks for. I will picture it as a house with nine windows, and we will open them one by one.

And we carry one question with us: how do I see who someone really is?

2 · Unusual presence

Most of us have met someone with unusual presence. When they walk into a room, the room seems to adjust. They pray beautifully. They lead worship, or preach, or run the youth group, and people are drawn to them. When they show interest in someone, that person feels chosen.

Sometimes such people are also honest, kind, faithful and safe. Sometimes they are not. And in the early stages, genuine character and polished presentation can be difficult to tell apart.

This lesson is about the difference between charisma and character: between what a person is able to present, and what a person actually is.

3 · At the city gate

Our passage is a story from Second Samuel. Absalom was King David's son. The Bible says:

"After this Absalom got himself a chariot and horses, and fifty men to run before him. And Absalom used to rise early and stand beside the way of the gate. And when any man had a dispute to come before the king for judgment, Absalom would call to him and say, 'From what city are you?'"

And Absalom would say to him, "See, your claims are good and right, but there is no man designated by the king to hear you." Then he would say, "Oh that I were judge in the land! Then every man with a dispute or cause might come to me, and I would give him justice."

4 · He stole their hearts

"And whenever a man came near to pay homage to him, he would put out his hand and take hold of him and kiss him. Thus Absalom did to all of Israel who came to the king for judgment. So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel."

5 · Stole the hearts

The phrase "stole the hearts" joins two Hebrew words: the verb "to steal", and the word for heart, the same word for heart we met in Lesson 3, the centre of thinking and choosing.

This is a Hebrew way of speaking about deception. In Genesis, when Jacob slipped away from Laban without telling him, the ESV says that Jacob "tricked" Laban, and its footnote gives the Hebrew: he "stole the heart of" Laban.

So when the writer says Absalom stole the hearts of the people, he does not mean that they simply came to like him. He means that Absalom won their loyalty by deception.

6 · Not every charming person

The story goes on. After four years of this, Absalom went to Hebron and had himself declared king. His warmth at the gate had been strategic. He used sympathy and personal warmth to build loyalty while he prepared to seize power.

But notice what the story does not say. It is not a profile of every charismatic person. Charm itself is not sinful. Many gifted, likeable people have good character. What Absalom's story shows is that making people feel seen can be either genuine care or a means of influence, and that time and conduct reveal the difference.

So this lesson does not teach you to distrust charm. It teaches you to look past it.

7 · Point 1: Charisma and character

Our first point is what charisma is, and what character is. Charisma is the ability to attract attention, inspire confidence, or make others feel engaged: confidence, charm, eloquence, presence, humour, spiritual fluency. It may come from genuine warmth and goodness, or it may exist without either. That is why charisma is neither proof of character nor evidence against it. Many godly people have it, and it can be used well.

But the danger is this. Charisma does not merely fail to show character. It can hide its absence. This is true in churches especially, where visible gifts are quickly rewarded with visible roles, and where fluent spiritual language can be mistaken for the life it describes.

Character is something else. Character is a settled pattern of conduct: the way a person actually behaves, over time, especially when behaving well is unobserved, inconvenient or costly.

8 · Known by fruit

Jesus said that a tree is known by its fruit: "A healthy tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a diseased tree bear good fruit." He was speaking about the overall fruit by which false prophets are recognised. He was not teaching that faithful people never sin. Individual failures need honesty and repair. But persistent fruit reveals the pattern.

Paul wrote in the same spirit about people who "profess to know God, but they deny him by their works."

9 · Faithful in a very little

And Jesus gave a principle, in his teaching on faithfulness, that is one of the most useful in this whole course: "One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much, and one who is dishonest in a very little is also dishonest in much."

This means character does not need a crisis to become visible. It shows in small things: the small lie to get out of an arrangement, the small unkindness to someone who cannot answer back, the small refusal to admit a small mistake. Ordinary moments can be especially revealing, because people do not think of them as moments to impress.

Notice, too, how the New Testament assesses character. When Paul lists what to look for in a church leader, almost everything is observable conduct: how they manage their household, whether they are quarrelsome, how they handle money, their reputation with outsiders. That is about church leadership, not marriage, and it is not a dating checklist. But the method is instructive. Scripture does not ask whether a person is impressive. It asks what they have consistently done.

10 · Point 2: Nine windows (1 and 2)

Our second point is nine windows into character. You cannot see a heart. You can see conduct, and conduct in certain situations is especially revealing. These windows help us see character over time. Many observations need context, but serious harm or danger can be decisive even the first time.

The first window is family relationships. A warm family is not automatically a good sign, and distance is not automatically a bad one. Some families are loving; others are controlling, neglectful or abusive. Notice whether the person handles their family with honesty, wisdom and appropriate boundaries. And where there has been abuse, do not require them to restore closeness.

The second window is how they treat people from whom they have nothing to gain: waiters, cleaners, drivers, shop assistants, junior colleagues, children. Courtesy in these moments is not conclusive, but repeated contempt is significant. Be fair across cultures and temperaments: quietness or awkwardness is not contempt. But someone who is charming to you and dismissive of the waiter is showing you which one is the performance.

11 · Windows 3 and 4

The third window is how they speak about former partners. Where a past relationship involved ordinary conflict, can they recognise their own part? Where they describe coercion, betrayal or abuse, do not demand that they share the blame for being harmed. Beyond that, notice whether they describe the facts consistently over time, whether they respect privacy, and whether there is a pattern in the story: the same complaint about every person, which may be telling you something about them.

The fourth window is authority and accountability. Can they disagree honestly, without deception, retaliation or contempt? Conflict with every employer, pastor and church deserves a closer look. But so does unquestioning submission, because authority can be abused, and healthy character sometimes includes leaving. Ask what happened, rather than assuming that leaving proves rebellion, or that staying proves faithfulness.

12 · Windows 5 and 6

The fifth window is money. Lesson 11 is given over to it, so only a note here. Money is a window because it is where words and behaviour most often separate. Watch for honesty, generosity, and whether their spending matches their stated values. Someone who is careful with their own money and careless with yours is worth noticing.

The sixth window is disappointment. A plan falls through, or a request is refused. What happens? Some people are gracious. Some sulk. Some punish. Some rage. How they treat you in that moment, especially when you are the cause, and especially when you have said a reasonable "no", is among the most important things you will learn about them.

13 · Window 7: Correction

The seventh window is correction: being told they are wrong, by you or by anyone. Can they listen without intimidation, punishment or immediate counterattack? Can they consider it, ask questions, and come back to it after their first reaction? Or does correction produce defence, retaliation, or an argument about how it was said, to avoid what was said?

James asks believers to be "quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger". A person who cannot be corrected cannot grow, and a marriage with a person who cannot grow is a marriage with a ceiling.

A fairness note for windows six and seven. Some people freeze, go quiet, or need time. That may reflect temperament, anxiety or past hurt, not manipulation. A delayed response is not automatically avoidance. The question is whether they say that they need time and return to the conversation, or use withdrawal to control and punish.

14 · Windows 8 and 9

The eighth window is temptation and small dishonesties. Not the great sins, which you probably will not see early, but the small ones. Do they tell white lies easily? Do they cut corners when they think no one is looking? Do they keep small promises, and return what they borrow? This is where Luke sixteen ten does its work.

The ninth window is how they speak about other people in general: friends, colleagues, church members, strangers. Is there a running current of contempt, mockery or suspicion? Do they share other people's private business? Or is there, underneath ordinary honesty, a basic charity towards people? The way a person speaks about others when they are absent is a fairly good guide to how they will one day speak about you.

15 · How to look through the windows

How do you look through these windows? Three reminders from Lesson 4. First, you are watching, not testing. Do not engineer situations to see how they react. Over time, ordinary life will open many of these windows. Some may stay closed for good reasons. But do not make a major commitment while important windows remain closed.

Second, you are looking for patterns, but severity matters. A single awkward moment may tell you little. A single act of violence, sexual coercion, credible threat or major deception may tell you enough to seek safety and help immediately.

Third, you are collecting evidence, not impressions. "He seems kind" is an impression. "When the taxi driver got lost and made us late, he stayed calm and thanked him anyway" is evidence. If it is safe, you may make a private note of what you have seen.

16 · The response to failure

And here is one addition. Pay particular attention to the response to failure. Nobody will pass all nine windows cleanly. You are not looking for someone who never fails. Such a person does not exist, and anyone who seems to be one is either not being seen clearly or is very good at presentation.

You are looking for what happens after the failure. A person of real character fails, notices, owns it and repairs it. A person of charisma without character fails, explains, deflects, or makes you feel that noticing was the problem. In ordinary failures, the response and lasting change can reveal a great deal about character. But some actions are serious enough that an apology or promised change does not make the relationship safe.

17 · Point 3: Integrity, the master window

Our third point is integrity, the master window. Underneath the nine windows sits one question: is there integrity between this person's public and private conduct, and do their words match their behaviour?

That does not mean identical behaviour everywhere. Everyone has more energy for a platform than for a kitchen. It means that the public virtues they display are not contradicted by private cruelty, dishonesty or contempt. Are they as patient with you alone as they are in company? Do they keep the promises they make in private?

Proverbs says, "a man is tested by his praise." Praise can test character. Notice how someone handles admiration, recognition and attention. A person who repeatedly seeks admiration, or reacts badly when attention moves elsewhere, is showing you that the public self is the one that matters to them. That is worth knowing before you become the private audience.

18 · Point 4: Your own bias

Our fourth point is a word about your own bias. Some of us overvalue charisma. We find confident, articulate, gifted people compelling, and quiet people invisible. If that is you, be honest about it, because confidence and visibility may be getting more weight in your judgement than faithfulness, kindness and integrity. Quietness is not proof of character, any more than charisma is proof against it.

Others have the opposite bias. Having been hurt by a charismatic person, they have become suspicious of anyone with presence. That is not discernment either. Charisma is not a red flag. It is simply not a green one. The person you are drawn to may have both gifts and goodness. The point of this lesson is to make sure you have actually looked for the second.

19 · Where you are right now

One word before we finish. This lesson helps you observe ordinary character. But if any of the nine windows has already shown you cruelty, contempt, intimidation, a pattern of deception, or a person you are afraid to disappoint, do not keep observing. Lesson 6 deals with this, and you should talk to someone you trust now. There is guidance about where to find help on the page beneath this lesson.

And where you are right now. If you are early in knowing someone, most windows are still closed. That is fine. If you have known someone for months, notice which windows are still empty. Sometimes that is because ordinary life has not yet opened them. Sometimes it is because you have been kept away from them.

And if you have recognised yourself in the description of charisma without character, this lesson is for you as much as for anyone you might date. Character can be built. It is built the same way it is seen: in small things, over time, with repair after failure.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Charisma is what a person can present; character is what they actually are. Character shows in small things, because whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful in much. Look through the nine windows, patiently, and watch the response to failure. Integrity is the master window. And know your own bias.
