First: is anyone afraid?

Part One is for the start of a slowdown; Part Two is for the review. Reflect separately first. If both of you freely wish to discuss what you have learned, and it is safe and appropriate, you can talk together. A joint meeting is not required to make a decision about your own future.

Part One: At the start

1. The reason. The specific concern I need to see more clearly. Which it is: a newly named difficulty without evidence yet · a pace that has outrun knowledge · external strain · a concern from counsel · unequal readiness · my own uncertain feelings. What evidence I already have, rather than fear or suspect; what is missing; whether time can clarify it, or it needs a decision now. And truthfully: I am not slowing down to avoid a decision I have already made, to test or punish, or to keep the other person while I decide.

  1. The specific concern, in one or two sentences:

2. The shape. What continues; what pauses; whether we are exclusive; how often we will communicate and meet; what we will tell family, friends or church; what privacy each of us keeps; what neither of us will do before the review (such as engagement, moving in, merging finances); what happens if one of us no longer wishes to continue. Have we agreed this together, in words?

3. What I am looking for. What would show the concern is resolving; what would show it is not; what would show it was the wrong question. Is it observable, or am I expecting the other person to prove an inner motive? Can it emerge naturally, without testing, provoking, monitoring or invading privacy?

4. The period. The review date, and why this period fits the concern. For addiction, financial instability, poorly managed health difficulties or a long-standing destructive pattern: what qualified help has been sought, and whose judgement have we asked about what “long enough” means?

5. Support. Who knows; what they may know; what remains private; what help either of us will seek; at least one source of counsel independent of the relationship.

6. Freedom. The other person may decide not to wait, and that is a legitimate answer. Either of us may reconsider before the review date if new information, an incompatibility or a safety concern arises.

Part Two: At the review

1. What I saw. What suggested the concern is resolving, and what suggested it is not. Was the period spent in ordinary conditions, or mainly in conversations about the relationship? What happened when we disagreed, or when one of us was tired, ill, disappointed or under pressure? Did anything new come up?

  1. What I saw:

2. The quality of any change. Did words and behaviour increasingly agree? Did the change depend on reminders, monitoring or fear of losing the relationship? Did it continue when the conversation had passed? Were setbacks admitted or hidden? Did either of us feel continually examined rather than known?

3. What I now know. What I know now that I did not know at the start; which of Lesson 17’s four kinds of difficulty it has turned out to be (skill · external strain · personal pattern · directional incompatibility); whether the slowdown produced clarity, or only temporary relief from deciding.

4. The whole relationship (Yes / Not sure / No). Shared spiritual foundation intact · No safety concern has arisen · Core life direction compatible · Attraction and freely chosen desire present, in both of us · I can say no without being punished · I have stayed connected to independent people · There has been enjoyment and friendship, not only scrutiny · I have taken the concern back to wise, independent counsel and considered their observations.

5. Other explanations. Am I continuing because the concern resolved, or because the date arrived and I do not want another hard conversation? Am I ending because the concern was confirmed, or because I am tired of waiting? Has the same question come back unanswered from a previous pause?

6. The decision. Continuing requires two freely given yeses. Walking away requires only one person’s no; nobody must persuade the other person to remain. Continue (Lesson 17’s agreement, then forward) · Slow down again, with a narrower question and a new review date (if this is not the first pause, take what each pause clarified to counsel first) · Walk away (Lesson 19). Write why, in one sentence.

7. Before you turn the page. Thank God for the truthfulness of the last weeks, and ask for the courage to act on it: to go forward without pretending the concern never existed, or to end without pretending the relationship had no good in it. “Let your ‘yes’ be yes and your ‘no’ be no” (James 5:12) is about truthful speech, not dating, but it is what a slowdown is for.

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · When is "not yet" the right answer?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 18, in Part Five: Continue, Slow Down or Walk Away.

Last time we walked the first of the three paths: continuing with wisdom. This lesson is about the middle one. I will picture it as a walker who slows, but does not stop: still on the road, still carrying the lamp, but taking the next stretch more carefully, with a signpost ahead marking when they will look again.

And we carry one question with us: when is "not yet" the right answer?

2 · The most misused answer

The second honest answer to "should we continue?" is "not yet". It is the least discussed of the three answers, and the most misused. Some people use it to avoid the other two: neither committing nor ending, holding a relationship in suspension until the decision makes itself. Others use it as a punishment or a test, a probation imposed by one person on the other.

Neither is what this lesson means. A wise "not yet" is as deliberate as a yes or a no. It says: there is something I do not know yet that I need to know before I can say more, and I am going to find it out, rather than guessing, or waiting for the question to go away.

And one thing first. Do not slow down an unsafe relationship. If there is fear, coercion or control, Lesson 6 comes first, and there is guidance on the page beneath this lesson.

3 · Desire without knowledge

Our passage is a single proverb:

"Desire without knowledge is not good, and whoever makes haste with his feet misses his way."

4 · Missing the way: ḥāṭāʾ

The word translated "misses" comes from the Hebrew verb chata, which can mean to miss a mark or to sin. In Proverbs 19:2, it describes someone who goes wrong through haste. In the Book of Judges, the same verb is used of skilled slingers who could aim at a hair and not miss.

The picture helps us understand the warning: wanting to reach a destination is not enough. Moving without sufficient knowledge can lead us away from the right path.

5 · A season, and a refrain

Scripture does not give a dating timetable. This proverb is about life generally, not courtship, but the principle transfers: wanting something is not the same as knowing it, and speed is not a virtue when the road is unclear. The Preacher says it another way: "For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven."

And in the Song of Songs, a love poem, a refrain returns several times: "that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases." It is poetry, not a rule, and it is not a timetable for dating. But it captures the wisdom of not forcing closeness ahead of its proper season.

6 · Point 1: What slowing down is

Our first point is what slowing down is. It means reducing the pace at which commitment deepens, for a defined period and a stated reason, so that something specific can be seen.

It is not usually about seeing each other less, although sometimes that helps. It is about not taking the next step, whether that is exclusivity, meeting families as a prospective in-law, moving towards engagement, or merging finances and plans, until a particular concern has had time to show itself for what it is.

7 · A reason, a shape, an end

Three things make it a decision rather than a drift. First, it has a reason. Something has been named. "I just need more time" is not a reason. "I need to see whether the pattern with money changes now that we have both named it" is.

Second, it has a shape. Both people know what is being paused and what is not. Are you still exclusive? Still seeing each other's families? These do not need the same answer for every couple, but they need an answer, or one of you thinks you are still moving forward and the other thinks you have stopped, and you are each living in a different relationship.

And third, it has an end: a point at which you will look at what has been learned and decide again. Without that, "not yet" quietly becomes "never", and the person waiting is being kept rather than dealt with truthfully.

8 · Point 2: When "not yet" is right (1)

Our second point is when "not yet" is the right answer. There is no complete list, but these are the most common.

The evidence is missing. Lesson 17 found a difficulty that is named and accepted by both of you, but too new to have shown change. The absence of evidence is not evidence of unwillingness. But it is also not evidence of change. So allow time in which change could reasonably show, and do not deepen commitment on the strength of intentions.

The pace has outrun the knowledge. Some relationships move very fast: intense feeling, early talk of marriage, families involved within weeks, and two people who know each other's hopes far better than each other's habits. If you are already talking about a date but have seen little ordinary life together, slowing down is not a lack of faith. It is catching up.

And external strain is distorting the picture: grief, illness, a job crisis, a visa decision. The strain may not disappear neatly. Illness, grief and family responsibilities can last years. So ask whether you have gained enough clarity, not merely whether the circumstances have ended.

9 · When "not yet" is right (2)

Counsel is concerned, and you are not sure they are wrong. If several of the people you trust have independently raised the same concrete concern, and you cannot say with confidence that they are mistaken, pause and look at what they are seeing before you commit further.

Readiness is unequal. One of you is ready to move towards engagement and the other is not, and the reluctance is not a verdict on the relationship, but a real difference in where each person is. Slowing down here protects both people: the one who is not ready is not pressured, and the one who is ready is not left guessing.

And you are not sure what you feel. If you cannot tell whether what you feel is love, comfort, fear of being alone, or habit, more time in ordinary conditions may help you understand what you are feeling, especially when you combine it with honest reflection and wise counsel. But be truthful about the horizon. A person cannot wait indefinitely for you to discover whether you want them.

10 · Point 3: When "not yet" is wrong (1)

Our third point is when "not yet" is the wrong answer. Slowing down can be a way of doing nothing. Be truthful about whether that is what you are doing.

When you already know. Some people reach this lesson having recognised their relationship in Lesson 16, and are looking for a way to postpone acting on it. Slowing down will not resolve a directional incompatibility. It will only make the eventual ending later and more costly. If you know, Lesson 19 is the lesson you need.

When it is a punishment or a test. "We are slowing down until you prove yourself" is not a decision made together. It is a sentence imposed by one person, and it may produce short-term compliance rather than trustworthy change. If a specific concern needs to be seen, say so, together, as two people looking at the same thing.

11 · When "not yet" is wrong (2)

When it is a way of keeping someone. Some people slow a relationship down because they are not sure they want the other person, but are sure they do not want to lose them. That is not fair. The other person is being held in reserve, and they cannot consent to that, because they do not know it is happening. If you are unsure whether you want them, say so, and accept that they are free to decide what to do with that.

When the relationship keeps cycling through pauses and resumptions. Repeated pauses are evidence too. Ask what each pause actually clarified, and whether the same uncertainty keeps returning unchanged. If it does, the answer is probably not more time.

And when the relationship is unsafe. Do not slow down an unsafe relationship. Slowing down assumes that both people can speak and act freely while a question is answered. Where there is fear, coercion, control, intimidation or violence, there is no question to answer and no safe way to wait. Lesson 6 comes first, and Lesson 19's safety guidance.

12 · Point 4: Slowing down well (1)

Our fourth point is slowing down well.

Say it, and say why. A pause that one person has decided privately is not a pause. It is withdrawal, and the other person will feel it as rejection without being told what it is. Tell them what you are doing and the reason. If you cannot explain the reason truthfully, ask whether it is a reason or an excuse. And if saying it directly feels unsafe, that is a different matter: do not arrange a private confrontation; seek support, and make safety the priority.

Agree the shape together, or recognise that you cannot. You do not need permission to decline the next level of commitment; that is yours to decide. But if the relationship is to continue during the slowdown, both of you must agree what it looks like. What continues? What pauses? What will neither of you do until the review? If you cannot agree a shape, the honest result may be an ending rather than an undefined pause, and that is better than each of you living in a different relationship.

13 · Slowing down well (2)

Name what you are looking for. Not "whether things get better", but the specific thing: whether the pattern with anger recurs once the pressure of the last two months has lifted; whether your reluctance changes as you spend more ordinary time together. Make sure it is observable. You cannot see a motive, and a slowdown that waits for the other person to prove what is in their heart will never end.

Keep living ordinary life together. The point is to see the relationship as it actually is, and that needs ordinary conditions: time with each other's friends, unglamorous errands, small disagreements, the way each of you behaves when tired or disappointed. And let the evidence emerge naturally. Do not test, provoke, monitor or read the other person's messages to speed it up. Evidence gathered that way tells you more about the gatherer than the gathered.

14 · Slowing down well (3)

Respect the other person's freedom. A slowdown is your decision, but the response is theirs. They may agree. They may say they cannot wait, or do not want to, and that is a legitimate answer even if it is not the one you hoped for. A person who is asked to slow down is not obliged to stay and be reviewed, and if they leave, they have not done something wrong.

And do not get engaged. Lesson 17 said it, and this lesson repeats it: do not become engaged while a major concern is still being tested. Engagement is a public promise, not a way to resolve a private uncertainty. And if a partner, a parent or a church is pressing for engagement during the time you agreed to use for finding out, that pressure is itself something to look at.

15 · The review: why ninety days?

The full reflection with this lesson is a 90-Day Relationship Review, and it is worth saying something about the number. Ninety days is a practical checkpoint, not a proven test. It is usually long enough to gather more than a first impression, and short enough to prevent an indefinite wait. But some situations simply will not arise in three months, and some people can keep up changed behaviour for a while when they know it is being noticed.

So the right period depends on what you are looking at. A new way of handling conflict may be visible in six weeks. Recovery from addiction, repair of serious financial instability, or a long personal pattern may need far longer, and may need professional judgement about what long enough means. Set the period to fit the concern, and say what it is.

16 · Doing the review

When the period ends, do the review. Reflect separately first. If both of you freely wish to discuss what you have learned, and it is safe and appropriate, you can talk together. But a joint meeting is not required to make a decision about your own future. The review asks four things: what you were looking for, what you saw, what you now know that you did not know before, and what you will decide.

And be clear about how the deciding works. Continuing requires two freely given yeses. Walking away requires only one person's no, and nobody must persuade the other person to remain.

17 · Three answers, still

The three options at the review are the same as always: continue, slow down again with a new and narrower question, or walk away. It is not a failure if the answer is the third. A slowdown that ends in a clear, kind no has done exactly what it was for.

And if the answer is the first, if the concern has been seen with open eyes, and it has resolved, or turned out smaller than it looked, or been shown to be workable in the way Lesson 17 described, then go forward gladly. You did not lose three months. You spent them finding out, which is what dating is for.

18 · A word about safety

A word about safety. The review is for relationships where both people can speak freely and neither is afraid. Do not complete or compare it with a partner you fear. Do not share private plans, passwords, financial information or sources of support with someone who may use them to control you.

If fear, coercion, control, intimidation or violence is present, do not slow down. Go to Lesson 6, and to Lesson 19's safety guidance, and there is guidance on the page beneath this lesson.

19 · Yes or no, and mean it

And where you are right now. If you are moving faster than you know, slow down. If you are waiting without a reason, a shape or an end, give it one, or be honest that you are not really waiting at all.

James writes, "let your 'yes' be yes and your 'no' be no." He is speaking about truthful speech, not dating. But it is what a slowdown is for: to make it possible for both of you to say one or the other, and mean it. Write down what you would want to know before the next step, and go and find it out.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. A wise "not yet" has a reason, a shape and an end. It is right when the evidence is missing, when the pace has outrun the knowledge, or when readiness is unequal. It is wrong when you already know, when it is used to punish or to keep someone, and when the relationship is unsafe. And if you slow down, slow down well, review honestly, and decide: two yeses to continue, and one no to end.

Let us pray.

Lord, thank you that you are never in a hurry and never late. Give me patience where I need to wait, and honesty where I need to speak.
