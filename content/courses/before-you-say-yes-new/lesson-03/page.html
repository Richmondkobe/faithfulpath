Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Who am I, and what do I really need?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 3, the last lesson in Part One of the course, Before You Start Dating.

In Lesson 2 we learned that the yoke has two sides. In this lesson we turn the light onto our own side. Before you choose someone else, it helps to know yourself. I will picture this lesson as the traveller's journal, opened at a blank page.

And we carry one question with us: who am I, and what do I really need?

2 · What would they be getting?

Ask most single Christians what they are looking for in a spouse, and they will have an answer ready. Ask them what a spouse would be getting, and the room goes quiet.

That is understandable. Dating naturally turns our attention outward. We study the other person and weigh their qualities. But every relationship has two people in it, and you are one of them. Whoever you marry will live with your values, your habits, your family, your money, your convictions and your history. If you do not know those things about yourself, you are asking someone else to discover them for you, usually after the wedding, and usually the hard way.

3 · Keep your heart

Our passage is one verse, from Proverbs chapter four:

"Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life."

Notice how important the heart is in this verse. Our thoughts, desires and choices shape the direction of our lives. That is why Scripture calls us to guard the heart carefully.

4 · The heart

The word translated "heart" is the Hebrew lev. In English we often use "heart" to mean our feelings. In the Old Testament it means much more than feelings.

The heart is where we think, where we choose, and where we desire. It is the inner centre of a person, the place where decisions are made. So when Proverbs says "keep your heart", it is not only telling us to protect our emotions.

Some translations render the first words, "Above all else, guard your heart." Either way, the verse asks us to watch over the place where our choices begin. And you cannot watch over what you have never looked at.

5 · A father's words to a son

Proverbs chapter four is a father teaching his son. He tells him to guard his heart, and then, in the verses that follow, his mouth, his eyes and his feet. Heart, mouth, eyes and feet: the whole person, kept on a straight path.

Scripture commends this kind of honest look inward. Lamentations says, "Let us test and examine our ways, and return to the Lord!" Notice that the examination does not end with the self. It ends with returning to God.

And Paul told the Romans not to think of themselves more highly than they ought, "but to think with sober judgment". Not too highly, not too low, but accurately. That is our aim in this lesson. Accuracy.

6 · Known by God first

Before we go further, one thing needs saying. This is not an exercise in giving yourself a personality label. And it is not an invitation to anxious self-inspection.

As a Christian, you look at yourself from a secure place. Paul writes, "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation." Before you are anything else, before you are single or dating or married, you are known and loved by God. That is the ground you stand on while you look honestly at the rest.

7 · Point 1: Why self-knowledge comes first (1 and 2)

Our first point is why self-knowledge comes before choosing. There are four practical reasons.

First, you cannot assess fit against something you have not defined. When we have not thought carefully about what we need, we judge others by whatever is most visible: looks, charm, spiritual vocabulary, how we feel around them. Those things are real. But they are not the things marriages are built on.

Second, you will confuse preferences with principles. Without clear thinking, everything you want can start to feel like something you need. The result is either a list so long that nobody could meet it, or, more dangerously, a list so vague that you cannot tell when a real line has been crossed.

8 · Why self-knowledge comes first (3 and 4)

Third, you may repeat patterns you have not noticed. Some people find that they are drawn again and again to similar people, and accept similar treatment, without ever asking why. Recognising a pattern can help you make a different choice when you have the freedom and opportunity to do so.

Fourth, and most important for this course, a person who does not know themselves is easier to define. Someone who arrives in a relationship unclear about what they believe, what they expect and what they will not accept is vulnerable to a partner who is very clear about all three. Several of the warning signs in Lesson 6 depend on exactly that. So knowing yourself is not only wise. It is protective. Knowing yourself can help you recognise pressure sooner. But another person's manipulation or abuse is never your responsibility.

9 · Point 2: Nine areas — faith and values

Our second point is nine areas to understand about yourself. Think of them as a map of the territory, not a checklist for one evening. They are faith, values, convictions, expectations of marriage, family, calling, lifestyle, finances, and relational capacity.

The first is faith. Not only whether you believe, but how you practise. How central are church, prayer, Scripture, giving and serving to your week? What does your walk with God actually look like, day to day, and what would you not change about it for another person?

The second is values: the priorities that shape how you organise your life, such as hospitality, simplicity, justice, family, generosity or rest. A useful question is this: what has made you most angry or most grieved in the last year? Sometimes that reveals a value that feels threatened. But anger and grief can also come from fear, shame or misunderstanding, so examine them rather than simply trusting them.

10 · Convictions and expectations of marriage

The third is convictions. Values describe what you consider important. Convictions are lines you believe you should not cross, even when obedience is costly. Some are shared by all Christians. Others are matters of conscience, where sincere believers differ. Paul says of such matters, "Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind." Know which of your convictions are which. And test them against Scripture, rather than treating them as unquestionable simply because they feel strong.

The fourth is your expectations of marriage. Who works? Who manages money, and how are decisions made? How is conflict handled, and how are children raised? Most of us carry a picture of marriage that we absorbed from our parents, our church or our culture, and have never examined. You do not have to defend your picture yet. But you do need to know what it is, and where it came from.

11 · Family and calling

The fifth is family. Genesis describes a man leaving his father and mother to hold fast to his wife. In many cultures, including much of Africa and Asia, that leaving does not mean cutting ties. It means forming a new first loyalty while the wider family remains close. So know what you expect. How involved will your parents be? What obligations do you carry, perhaps financial support, care for ageing relatives, or responsibility for younger siblings? These are among the most common sources of conflict in marriage, and they are often never discussed before it.

The sixth is calling and direction. If you believe God has given you a direction, a place, a work or a people, it deserves serious attention. Hold it with conviction and humility. Test it through Scripture, prayer, wise counsel and time, because callings can be misunderstood, and they can develop. And notice the difference between sharing a calling and supporting one. Spouses do not always need the same vocation. They do need to stand behind each other's.

12 · Lifestyle, finances, relational capacity

The seventh is lifestyle: the daily shape of things. City or countryside, fast or slow, many people or few, how you rest, and how you spend a Saturday. These sound small. But they are what a marriage is made of, hour by hour.

The eighth is finances. Not your income, but your habits and your attitude. Do you spend or save? Do you carry debt, and how do you feel about it? Are you generous, and to whom? What do you believe money is for?

And the ninth is relational capacity. How do you say what you need, receive correction, and repair a conflict? Can you apologise without defending yourself? Can you stay close to someone without losing yourself in them? This is the area most of us know least about ourselves, and it is the one a spouse will feel most.

13 · Point 3: Your patterns

Our third point is your patterns: your history in relationships, and what it shows.

If you have dated before, look back honestly. Who were you drawn to, and why? What did you tolerate that you should not have? What did you demand that was unfair? Did you tend to rush, or to hold back? How did those relationships end, and what part was yours?

If you have not dated, or not much, ask the same questions of your friendships and your family. How do you handle conflict with the people closest to you? Do you say what you need, or hope it will be noticed? Do you forgive easily, or keep a ledger?

None of this is meant as self-condemnation. It is meant as information. A pattern you can see is a pattern you can pray about, talk about with someone wise, and choose to interrupt. A pattern you cannot see may be repeated without your realising it. Recognising it gives you an opportunity to respond differently.

14 · Not alone with it

One caution. If looking back brings up experiences of being harmed, such as coercion, control, abuse, or betrayal that still weighs on you, that is not something to work through alone with a worksheet. It is a good reason to speak with a counsellor or a trusted pastor.

Knowing yourself includes knowing what you have survived, and being kind enough to yourself to get help carrying it.

15 · Point 4: Four kinds of requirement

Our fourth point is the part many people have been waiting for: the list. What do you require in a spouse?

Most people's lists mix several very different kinds of item, and the mixture is where the trouble starts. Sorting them into four categories brings a great deal of clarity: foundational requirements, personal non-negotiables, preferences, and protective rules to examine.

Foundational requirements are necessary for any safe, faithful and healthy marriage. They are not personal. They are the floor beneath everyone's list. A shared and genuine faith in Christ. Basic honesty. Respect. And freedom from violence, threats, coercion, controlling behaviour and ongoing deception. Be clear about that last one: violence, threats, coercion and control are not compatibility differences. They violate the basic safety and dignity that every relationship requires. Lessons 6 and 19 deal with them directly.

16 · Personal non-negotiables

Personal non-negotiables are conditions without which you have sound reason to believe you could not enter a marriage honestly, keep your existing responsibilities, or build a healthy shared life. They come from your own circumstances, conscience and calling.

They might concern children. They might concern responsibilities you already carry, such as a dependent relative. Health, yours or someone's in your care. A country you cannot leave. A well-tested direction that is central to your life. Or an addiction question: not whether someone has ever struggled, but whether there is an active, unaddressed addiction with deception or a refusal of help.

A personal non-negotiable should be specific, carefully examined, and open to testing by Scripture, trusted counsel and reality.

17 · Preferences and protective rules

Preferences are qualities you genuinely value but can hold with flexibility. Height. Humour. Education. A shared love of music or travel. A particular cultural background. These are legitimate. You are allowed to want what you want. But a preference treated as a non-negotiable will keep you from good people for reasons that do not hold up under prayer.

Protective rules to examine are the difficult fourth group. These are requirements formed partly in response to fear, pain or past harm. "They must never have been in a serious relationship before." "They must have no close friends of the opposite sex." Some of these contain real wisdom. Someone who has been betrayed may have a genuine need for strong transparency. But a rule built quickly after an injury can sometimes be broader than the danger it is meant to prevent. A wise boundary responds to real risks and observed behaviour. It does not have to be based on fear alone, and it does not need to be abandoned simply because it arose from a painful experience. You may examine a rule without deciding to change it. The aim is to identify the real danger, and choose a boundary that responds to it wisely.

18 · Sorting the list

How do you tell the four apart? Ask of each item: where did this come from, and what problem do I believe it would prevent? Foundational requirements come from what every marriage needs. Personal non-negotiables come from your real circumstances and tested convictions. Preferences come from taste. Protective rules may arise from past experiences, genuine risks or fears. Examine what each rule is protecting, and whether it responds wisely to the actual risk. Do not dismiss a sound boundary simply because it began with a painful experience.

And a word about length. A long list is not necessarily wise, and a short list is not necessarily careless. What matters is whether you have thought carefully about what belongs on the list, why it matters, and whether it is truly necessary.

19 · Where you are right now

Finally, where you are right now. If you are already dating, look at your requirements and ask whether any of them has already been bent. It is remarkably common to discover, only when the list is written down, that you have been quietly negotiating something you said you never would, because you liked someone.

For ordinary disagreement, that does not mean the relationship must end today. It does mean you owe yourself an honest conversation: with God first, then with someone wise, then with the person. But if the crossed line involves violence, threats, coercion, sexual pressure or fear for your safety, do not begin with a private confrontation. Seek individual support first. There is guidance about where to find help on the page beneath this lesson.

And if you are not dating, you are in the best position of all to do this well, because there is no one on the other side of the list yet to distort it. Take your time.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Keep your heart, because it is the centre where you think, choose and desire. Self-knowledge comes before choosing, and it protects you. There are nine areas to understand, and your patterns to see. And there are four kinds of requirement: foundational, personal, preferences, and protective rules to examine.

22 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord, you know me better than I know myself. Search my heart and show me who I really am. Help me see my strengths with gratitude and my weaknesses with honesty. Give me clear convictions and a humble spirit. Where my fears have made my rules, bring your wisdom and your peace. In Jesus' name, amen.
