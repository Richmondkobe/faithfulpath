Transcript and Scripture references

1 · If I feel this strongly, doesn't it mean something?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 4, and we begin Part Two of the course, Choosing Wisely.

In Lesson 3 we looked honestly at ourselves. Now we turn to the moment many relationships begin: attraction. I will picture this lesson as two figures on the path, with a warm glow between them, and a slower light that helps us see them clearly.

And we carry one question with us: if I feel this strongly, doesn't it mean something?

2 · Two unhelpful ways

"There was just something about him." "I can't explain it. I knew the moment I saw her." Most people who have been in love have said something like this. Attraction is real, it is powerful, and it is often the first thing that happens between two people who later marry.

Christians tend to handle attraction in two unhelpful ways. Some treat it as the whole story: if the feeling is strong enough, it must be love, and if it is love, it must be right. Others treat it with suspicion, as though wanting someone were shallow, and the truly spiritual choice were the person you feel nothing for.

This lesson is written against both errors. Attraction is a gift. It can invite you to know someone, but it cannot tell you whether to build a life with them. Attraction may open the door. Discernment decides whether it is wise to walk through it.

3 · Trained by constant practice

Our passage is one verse from the letter to the Hebrews:

"But solid food is for the mature, for those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil."

Notice the words "trained" and "constant practice". Discernment is not something you are handed in a moment. It grows.

4 · Trained

The word translated "trained" is the Greek gymnazo. It means to train or exercise, and it is the word behind our English "gymnasium". The picture of an athlete in training helps us understand it.

So the writer says that mature believers have their powers of discernment exercised, trained by constant practice, like muscles that grow stronger with use. Discernment is learned over time. It is not a single moment of certainty.

That matters for this lesson. Discernment is a trained capacity. It grows with practice, with honesty and with patience. It does not come free with a strong feeling.

5 · Milk and solid food

Here is the setting. The writer to the Hebrews had much to say to his readers, but he says they had "become dull of hearing". They had received teaching for some time, but the writer rebukes them for their lack of progress. They still needed milk when they should have been ready for solid food.

Then he describes the mature: people whose discernment has been trained by practice, so that they can tell good from evil.

So this verse is first about spiritual maturity in general, not about dating. But the principle applies well here. Choosing a spouse is exactly the kind of decision that needs a trained, practised judgement, not just a strong first impression.

6 · Point 1: What attraction is

Our first point is what attraction is. Attraction often begins as a response rather than a decision. You do not choose to find someone's laugh warming or their mind interesting. You may not choose the first pull. But you do choose whether to feed it, act on it, or build a relationship around it.

It comes in more than one kind. There is physical attraction, the body's response to another person. Emotional attraction, the sense of being at ease and understood. Intellectual attraction, being interested in how someone thinks. And what many Christians would call spiritual attraction, being drawn to the way someone loves God. These often overlap, and they do not always arrive together.

Scripture does not treat any of this as shameful. The Song of Songs celebrates romantic and physical delight without embarrassment. Desire is part of how God made us. The question is never whether you may feel it. The question is what it can tell you, and what it cannot.

7 · What attraction can tell you

So what can attraction tell you? First, that a door is open. Attraction is enough for a first conversation, a second, a decision to get to know someone. Nobody is obliged to pursue a relationship with a person they feel nothing for simply because that person is godly and available.

Second, it can tell you something about yourself. What you are drawn to says something about what you value and what has shaped you. If you notice that you are drawn again and again to the same kind of person, including a kind that has not been good for you, that is worth taking seriously.

Third, attraction matters for marriage. In First Corinthians chapter seven, Paul treats the physical side of marriage as real and important. Some Christians marry someone they find admirable but not attractive, hoping feelings will follow. Attraction may grow as trust and affection deepen, but that growth cannot be promised. No one should enter marriage pretending to feel desire they do not feel, or assuming that marriage itself will create it. Pretending is not a spiritual discipline.

8 · Point 2: What attraction cannot tell you

Our second point is what attraction cannot tell you. It cannot tell you whether a person is honest, kind, faithful, self-controlled or safe. It cannot tell you where their life is going, how they handle money, what they are like when disappointed, or how they treat people who cannot help them.

The reason is simple. Early attraction responds to limited information. Character becomes clear through repeated patterns over time. What you see at the beginning may be genuine, but it is incomplete. The strength of your response does not prove what kind of person they are.

Chemistry is fast. Character is slow.

9 · The Lord looks on the heart

The Old Testament makes this point in a different kind of choosing. When Samuel was sent to anoint a king from among the sons of Jesse, he saw the eldest, Eliab, and thought, surely this is the one. But the Lord said to Samuel, "Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature", and then, "For the Lord sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart."

The context is the choice of a king, not a spouse. But the principle carries: what is visible first is not what matters most. And you cannot see another person's heart as God does. What you can observe is the fruit that heart produces over time.

10 · Charm is deceitful

Proverbs says something similar, addressed to men choosing a wife, and it applies in both directions: "Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain, but a woman who fears the Lord is to be praised."

The proverb does not call beauty evil. "Vain" here means fleeting, unable to secure what it seems to promise. The proverb simply refuses to treat beauty or charm as reliable evidence of enduring character.

11 · Point 3: Why attraction clouds judgement

Our third point is why attraction clouds judgement. If attraction simply told you nothing about character, it would be harmless. But it does something more active. It changes how you interpret what you see.

When you are strongly drawn to someone, unclear things tend to be read in their favour. A sharp remark becomes "just their sense of humour". Lateness becomes "they're so busy". A flash of temper becomes "they're passionate". Silence about their past becomes "they're private".

The same behaviour in someone you were not attracted to would be noted. In someone you are attracted to, it is explained. Intelligent, normally wise people do this. The explaining is not stupidity. It is attraction, doing what attraction does.

12 · Intensity and familiarity

Intensity makes this worse. A very strong early feeling can seem like evidence that this is different, that this is the one. But intensity is not meaning. It is worth asking what is producing it. Sometimes it is simply two well-matched people. Sometimes it is being pursued after a long time of not being pursued. Sometimes it is a person who is very good at making people feel special, quickly. And sometimes it is unpredictability: attention that comes and goes, warmth followed by coolness, which can pull harder than steady kindness. Lesson 6 returns to this. For now, notice that the strength of the pull is not proof of the goodness of the person.

One more thing, and it connects to Lesson 3. We can be drawn to what feels familiar, and familiar is not always safe. If something resembles patterns from your family or earlier relationships, ask: does it feel comforting because it is healthy, or recognisable because I have known it before? The feeling of "I've known you forever" is worth examining before it is trusted.

13 · Point 4: What discernment is

Our fourth point is what discernment is. Discernment is the slower thing. It is a prayerful, evidence-based judgement about a person's character, direction, fit and safety, made over time, and with the help of people who are not attracted to them.

Discernment does not cancel attraction. It tests it. Proverbs puts the contrast simply: "The simple believes everything, but the prudent gives thought to his steps."

14 · Give it time; let ordinary life show you

In practice, discernment in dating looks like this. First, give it time. Attraction is immediate; character needs months. Nothing in this lesson asks you to be suspicious of someone. It asks you to wait long enough to see them clearly.

Second, let ordinary life show you who they are. People are at their best on a date. Over time, you want to see them tired, disappointed, inconvenienced, with people they do not need to impress, and receiving a reasonable "no".

Do not manufacture pressure, arrange tests or provoke someone to see how they respond. Simply let the relationship unfold through ordinary life, rather than keeping it inside carefully managed dates.

15 · Two columns

Third, separate what you feel from what you know. You can picture two columns, or write them privately if it is safe to do so. The first is what you feel. The second is what you know, supported by what you have actually observed. The first fills quickly. The second is the one that matters.

If the second column is nearly empty, you are not in a position to make decisions yet, however full the first.

16 · What you explain away; who else sees

Fourth, notice what you are explaining away. This may be the most useful discipline in this lesson. Whenever you find yourself explaining away something concerning, pause and examine it. If it is safe, you may make a private note. Not every explanation is an excuse, and one serious incident should not be ignored just because it happened once. Ask three questions: How serious was the behaviour? Has it happened before? What did the person do when it was addressed? Repetition matters, but so do severity and accountability.

Fifth, involve people who are not attracted to them: friends, family, a pastor, a mentor. They see the same person without the halo. Their view is not automatically right; families and churches can be controlling or prejudiced, and Lesson 14 deals with that. But if several trustworthy people identify the same concrete concern, do not dismiss it quickly.

And ask yourself two honest questions. What character concern am I tempted to minimise because I am attracted to this person? And if a friend described the same behaviour in their relationship, what would I tell them?

17 · A word about safety

A word about safety. Intense attraction, a fast pace and gradual separation from your other relationships are, together, a pattern worth taking seriously. Lesson 6 deals with it directly.

Attraction and attachment can remain strong even when someone is harming you. Continuing to want the person does not mean the relationship is safe, or that the harm is less serious. If you fear their reaction, seek individual support first, before confronting them. There is guidance about where to find help on the page beneath this lesson.

18 · Where you are right now

Finally, where you are right now. If you are newly attracted to someone, enjoy it. It is a good thing to feel. Then slow down enough to let the second column fill.

If you are in a relationship and this lesson has made you uncomfortable, perhaps because you recognised the explaining, or the intensity, or the familiarity, that discomfort is a signal worth examining, not a verdict. It may be discernment, anxiety, an old wound or something else. Do not silence it, but do not obey it blindly. Bring it into prayer, compare it with evidence, and talk with someone wise.

And if you feel little attraction to someone you otherwise admire, do not force it and do not feel guilty. Give it honest time in ordinary settings. Attraction sometimes grows as character becomes visible. But if it does not grow, you are not failing anyone by being truthful about that.

19 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Attraction is a gift, and it is real. It can open a door, but it cannot tell you about character. It changes how you interpret what you see. And discernment is trained: by time, by ordinary life, by two honest columns, by honest questions, and by the eyes of other people.

20 · One step now

Here is one step for today. If you are drawn to someone, write down one thing you are attracted to, and one thing you have actually observed about their character. Something you have seen, and when.

If you are not dating, think of someone you were drawn to in the past, and do the same. Write it down privately if you wish, or simply think it through before God.

Let us pray.

Father, thank you for creating us with the ability to love, to desire, and to delight in another person. Help me not to be ashamed of attraction, and not to be ruled by it. Give me patience to wait, eyes to see clearly, and wisdom to know the difference between a feeling and the truth. In Jesus' name, amen.
