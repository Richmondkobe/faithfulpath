Transcript and Scripture references

1 · What if we are both good people, but this is not right?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 16, the last lesson of Part Four: Discernment.

I will picture this lesson as two good people walking together, each carrying a lamp, until the path divides. Both roads are good. Neither is in shadow. And the question is whether these two are meant to walk the same one.

And we carry one question with us: what if we are both good people, but this is not right?

2 · The grief with no one to blame

Some relationships have no villain. Two sincere believers. Sound character on both sides. No red flags, no safety concerns, families who approve, a church that is already planning the wedding. And still, the relationship may not be a workable or mutually desired fit, not because either person is bad, but because goodness alone does not create compatibility.

When there is a villain, ending a relationship is painful but clear. When there is none, ending it can feel like a betrayal of a good person, and an admission that you are the problem. So people stay.

This lesson is about the freedom Christians have, and often do not know they have, to recognise someone's goodness and still say no to the relationship. And one thing first: this lesson is for relationships without abuse or control. If fear, coercion or control is present, do not call it incompatibility. Lesson 6 comes first, and there is guidance on the page beneath this lesson.

3 · Paul and Barnabas (1)

Our passage is from Acts, after Paul and Barnabas had travelled together for years. "And after some days Paul said to Barnabas, 'Let us return and visit the brothers in every city where we proclaimed the word of the Lord, and see how they are.'"

"Now Barnabas wanted to take with them John called Mark. But Paul thought best not to take with them one who had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia and had not gone with them to the work."

4 · Paul and Barnabas (2)

"And there arose a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other. Barnabas took Mark with him and sailed away to Cyprus, but Paul chose Silas and departed, having been commended by the brothers to the grace of the Lord. And he went through Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the churches."

Two good men. Two journeys. Luke records the disagreement and their separation without explicitly declaring which man was right about taking Mark. Barnabas went with Mark to Cyprus, while Paul continued with Silas, strengthening the churches.

5 · A sharp disagreement: paroxysmos

The word Luke uses for "sharp disagreement" is the Greek paroxysmos. It describes something sharp and stirring: a strong contention, not a polite difference of opinion.

Interestingly, the same word appears in Hebrews in a good sense: "let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works." A word that can describe provoking each other to love is used here of two faithful men who could not agree. The word can describe a strong provocation in different contexts. Hebrews uses it positively, but Acts describes a painful disagreement that resulted in separation. Scripture records it honestly. It does not pretend that good people never part.

6 · A ministry disagreement, not a romance

Before we go further, be clear what this passage is. It was a disagreement about ministry, about whom to take on a missionary journey. It was not a romantic relationship, and it is not a model for dating decisions.

Barnabas was Mark's cousin, and he risked something on Mark. Years later, Paul writes of Mark warmly. He tells one church, if Mark comes to you, "welcome him". And near the end of his life he writes to Timothy: "Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry."

Scripture does not record a reconciliation between Paul and Barnabas, and we should not invent one. What the passage shows is simpler: separation does not necessarily mean one person lacks Christian character. Faithful people sometimes part ways.

7 · Point 1: Good is not the same as right

Our first point is that good is not the same as right. And it is the hardest thing to hear, because it sounds ungrateful: a person's goodness is not, on its own, a reason to marry them.

Goodness is a reason to respect them, to be glad you know them, to hope well for them. Sound character is essential to a healthy marriage. Lessons 5, 6 and 7 spent a long time on it. But it is not enough to show that these two particular people should marry. There are many good people in the world, and you will not marry most of them.

And one clarification. "Wrong for each other" does not mean that God created one ideal match and this person is not it. Lesson 13 set that idea aside. It means that the relationship, as it actually exists, cannot support a marriage both people can enter freely and faithfully.

8 · Not gratitude, not charity

The fact that this good person is the one in front of you, and wants you, and has been kind, does not create an obligation. You cannot marry as an act of gratitude, or charity, or because refusing would hurt someone who does not deserve to be hurt. A marriage entered for those reasons will not honour the goodness that prompted it. It will slowly wear it down.

Scripture does not command you to marry a particular person because they are good, available or interested. It sets boundaries: in the Lord, with wisdom, without coercion. And within them, it leaves you free. Paul is speaking specifically about a widow who is free to remarry: she is "free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord." His words illustrate that, within Christian faithfulness, marriage is not meant to be forced upon someone against their will. The wish is not a spiritual defect. It is part of what you are allowed to weigh.

9 · Point 2: What "wrong for each other" looks like (1)

Our second point is what "wrong for each other" looks like. There is no single sign, but there are several that, together, describe this situation. None of them is a fault in either person, and none of them is a verdict on its own.

First, you want different lives: a calling, a country, children, a shape of family that one of you needs and the other cannot honestly give. Ask whether it is a central conviction, or a preference neither of you has seriously explored. And a claimed calling should be tested with the same humility Lesson 13 asked for. "God called me" must not become a way of making the other person surrender every competing hope.

Second, the same person is always the one adapting. Every relationship needs mutual adapting, and it will not be equal in every season. The concern is when the normal shape of the relationship keeps requiring one person to surrender important convictions, relationships or parts of who they are, while the other stays largely unchanged.

Third, the relationship depletes rather than restores, over time. Not on a bad day. Does the relationship itself keep leaving you drained and guarded, or is work, health, grief or family pressure affecting you both? Name the source before treating exhaustion as a verdict.

10 · What "wrong for each other" looks like (2)

Fourth, the recurring disagreements are not about incidents. They come not from a failure to apologise, but from lasting differences in conviction, direction or needs. The question is not whether either person can be fixed, but whether those differences can be carried without chronic resentment.

Fifth, you are less yourself. Be specific. Which part of you has become quieter? Did the other person ask for that? Is it healthy growth, ordinary adjustment, fear, or erasing yourself? Sixth, independent counsel converges on something concrete: advisers who have not compared notes name the same specific thing.

And seventh, a persistent reluctance that honest time has not resolved. You have prayed for the doubt to lift and given real time in ordinary settings. A reluctance like that is not automatically God's warning; Lesson 13 said no feeling is. But neither should it be pushed down because the other person is good. Ask what the feeling is attached to, and whether it changes when specific concerns are addressed. Premarital doubt should not simply be argued away. It should be understood.

11 · What it is not (1)

Be honest in the other direction too, because this lesson can be misused.

It is not ordinary doubt. Every serious relationship produces moments of "what if I'm wrong?" That is the weight of the decision, not a verdict on it. It is not fear of commitment dressed up as discernment. Some people leave every good relationship at the point where it would ask something of them, and call it "not feeling peace". If you have done this more than once, the pattern is worth examining before you act on it again.

And it is not perfectionism. No one will match you in every area. Lesson 15 said that liveable differences are the texture of every marriage. A person who is waiting for the absence of all friction will wait forever.

12 · What it is not (2)

It may not be incompatibility at all if outside stress, like grief, illness or job pressure, is temporarily reducing both people's capacity. And it may not be the relationship itself if an unresolved personal struggle, with anxiety, low mood or past trauma, makes closeness hard in every serious relationship. That is not a reason to stay, but it may call for individual support before you interpret the pattern.

And it is not "needs work". A workable relationship has a specific difficulty both people can name, mutual willingness to address it, and some evidence that honest effort produces change. Lesson 17 is about relationships like that, and they are worth the work. A fundamental incompatibility stays substantially unchanged despite honest effort, because solving it would require one person to surrender something central. Even then, effort alone does not obligate continuation.

Telling the difference is the whole task, and it is rarely possible alone. Use the earlier tools. Use counsel. Use time, honest time, with something specific tried.

13 · Point 3: The theology of a good ending

Our third point is the theology of a good ending, and it needs to be said plainly, because so many Christians do not know it.

Dating is not marriage. In most settings, engagement is a serious intention and a public promise to marry, but it is not yet the marriage covenant made through mutual vows. Ending an engagement can carry substantial moral, emotional, financial and community consequences. But proceeding without free conviction is not the solution to those consequences.

Before the vows, you are two people discerning, and discernment is allowed to conclude "no". That is what it was for.

14 · Pain is not cruelty

God does not require you to marry someone to reward their goodness, or to avoid disappointing them, or because he "brought you together". A meeting can be meaningful, even providential, without marriage being its required outcome. God can work through a relationship without obligating the two people to continue it.

And a clear, respectful no may be painful, but pain is not the same as cruelty. Marrying someone you should not marry, and letting them discover it after the vows, is the deeper harm. Continuing from pity, fear or obligation usually deepens the eventual harm. A clear, honest, gentle no, given as soon as you know it, is one of the most respectful things you can offer a person you care about.

15 · Point 4: When only one of you sees it (1)

Our fourth point is what to do when only one of you sees it.

If they believe it is right and you do not, you are not obliged. Lesson 13 said this about "God told me", and it applies to sincere conviction of every kind. Their certainty is theirs. Your no does not require a competing certainty. It requires only that you have looked honestly and cannot say yes.

You will want to soften it, to leave the door open, to say "not now" when you mean "not this". Do not. Lesson 19 will help with how to end a relationship well, and its first principle is clarity, because ambiguity is a slow cruelty. A good person deserves a clear answer, even a painful one.

16 · When only one of you sees it (2)

If you believe it is right and they do not, this is the harder side, and many people are on it. They are not rejecting God. They are not failing to see what God has shown you. They are using exactly the freedom this lesson has described, and if you argue that their no is disobedience, you have moved into the pattern Lesson 6 named.

Receive the decision as final unless they independently and clearly reopen the conversation. Do not continue contact for the hidden purpose of changing their mind. Grieve it, because it is a real loss. Do not turn it into a spiritual contest. Two good people, and one of them says no. That is painful, and it is allowed, and God's purposes for you are not smaller because of it.

17 · If you are engaged

If you are engaged and substantial uncertainty persists, do not silence it merely because plans have advanced. Consider pausing further arrangements while you identify the specific concern. Speak honestly with your partner, unless safety makes that unwise, and seek independent, qualified premarital counsel.

A pause is not automatically a cancellation. But a wedding timetable should not be allowed to decide what the two of you have not resolved. Where family coercion, threats or safety concerns are present, do not use ordinary joint counselling or family mediation. Lessons 6 and 10 apply.

18 · A word about safety

A word about safety. This lesson is for relationships where no abuse or serious safety concern has been identified, and both people appear to have genuine character. If fear, coercion or control is present, do not use the language of incompatibility to make it sound smaller. Return to Lesson 6.

And if you have decided to end a relationship, but you fear their reaction, do not announce the ending alone. Lesson 19's safety guidance applies, and guidance is on the page beneath this lesson.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you have recognised your relationship in this lesson, and feel the particular grief of it, the grief with no one to blame, you are taking an important concern seriously. That is not ungrateful, disloyal or faithless. Use the reflection below to test what you are seeing before deciding your next step. Then Lessons 17, 18 and 19 will help you decide what to do.

And if you have found nothing of yourself here, if the relationship is good, the fit is good, and what remains is ordinary work, be glad. Lesson 17 is for you.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Good is not the same as right. Look for patterns, not a single sign, and be just as honest about what this is not. Dating is not marriage, and discernment is allowed to conclude no. And whoever sees it first, give a clear and gentle answer, and receive one without turning it into a contest.

22 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord, I keep looking for someone to blame, and there is no one. They are good, and I am not sure this can be built. Take away the idea that loving you means ignoring what I can plainly see. If I have reached a decision, give me courage to say it honestly and safely. Hold them, whatever I decide. And if I am wrong about all of this, show me that instead. In Jesus' name, amen.
