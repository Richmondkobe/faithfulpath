Transcript and Scripture references

1 · What does money show?

Welcome back. This is Lesson 11, in Part Three: Dating With Your Eyes Open.

This lesson is about money: not how much a person has, but what the way they handle it shows. I will picture it as a house with six windows. We will look through them one at a time.

And we carry one question with us: what does the way someone handles money show?

2 · Not "What do they earn?"

When people ask about a possible spouse's finances, the question they usually mean is, "What do they earn?" It is not a useless question. Salary tells you something about present financial capacity. But it tells you much less about character than many people assume.

Two people on the same salary can be as different as it is possible to be: one careful, generous, honest and content; the other reckless, secretive, grasping and never satisfied. How a person manages what they have, and how honestly they discuss it, often reveals more than the amount itself.

Lesson 5 called money a window, because it is where words and behaviour most often separate. Everyone says they value generosity and honesty. Money is where you find out.

3 · Faithful in a very little

Our passage is from Luke's Gospel, where Jesus is teaching his disciples about money. Jesus said:

"One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much, and one who is dishonest in a very little is also dishonest in much. If then you have not been faithful in the unrighteous wealth, who will entrust to you the true riches?"

4 · Faithful, and entrusted: pistos

The word translated "faithful" is the Greek pistos. It means trustworthy, reliable: someone you can rely on.

And notice the next verse: "who will entrust to you the true riches?" The word for "entrust" comes from the same family of words. Jesus connects trustworthiness in handling money with being entrusted with greater things.

So Scripture treats faithfulness with money as a training ground for faithfulness in greater things. By application, it is a reasonable training ground for marriage too.

5 · God and money

Jesus has just told a difficult parable about a manager accused of wasting his master's possessions. Facing dismissal, the manager makes arrangements to secure his future. Jesus then teaches about faithfulness with money, and warns that no one can serve both God and money: "You cannot serve God and money."

And then Luke adds a telling detail: "The Pharisees, who were lovers of money, heard all these things, and they ridiculed him." Religious people, respected people, were ruled by money, and they laughed at the idea that it mattered. Money is not a dirty subject. But it shows what we serve.

6 · Point 1: Not about how much

Our first point is what this lesson is not about. It is not about how much a person has. Poverty is not a character flaw, and neither is wealth. A student with loans, a young worker in a low-paid ministry job, a person supporting parents on a modest income, a widow who has lost most of what she had: none of these is showing you anything about their character by their bank balance. And a wealthy person may be miserly, dishonest or enslaved to their money in ways that the balance conceals.

James warned the church sharply against honouring the rich and slighting the poor. And Scripture's most famous gift makes the same point. God does not measure a person's worth by the size of their resources.

7 · The widow's two coins

Jesus sat opposite the treasury in the temple, and watched people putting in money. Many rich people put in large sums. "And a poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, which make a penny."

And Jesus called his disciples and said, "this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the offering box. For they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on." Whatever else this story teaches, Jesus watched, and he noticed what others overlooked. He was not measuring the amount.

And notice the setting. Just before this, Jesus warned about religious leaders who devoured widows' houses. So this passage must never be used to pressure someone in poverty to give away what they need to live. Jesus saw the widow. He saw the cost of her gift, not merely the coins.

8 · Not an audit

And this lesson is not the full financial conversation that engaged couples need. Talk Before You Marry covers that. In dating, you are not merging finances, and you are not entitled to a full audit. Privacy about the details is legitimate at this stage.

The right level of honesty changes with the seriousness of the relationship. Early in dating, you learn attitudes and habits, seen more than heard. Before exclusivity, you need to know anything that affects safety or the legitimacy of the relationship, such as an existing marriage or an undisclosed dependant. Before engagement, you need substantial financial truth, from both of you. What you are doing in dating is watching character.

9 · Point 2: Spending and debt

Our second point is six windows. The first is spending: not how much, but how. Does the person generally understand what they can afford? When money is limited, do they respond with honesty and adjustment, or with concealment and more debt? Do not judge from isolated purchases; a costly item may have been saved for, or given. But over time, what a person spends on when they have a choice is worth noticing. Jesus said, "where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."

The second is debt: not whether, but what, why, and how they carry it. Do not assume one kind of debt proves wisdom and another proves poor character. Consumer debt can come from poverty or a family crisis, and study debt can be carried recklessly. Ask, when it is appropriate: what is the debt, how did it arise, and what is the plan? Proverbs observes that "the borrower is the slave of the lender": not a command against all borrowing, but a sober description of what debt does to freedom. And willingness to seek professional debt advice is itself a good sign.

10 · Generosity and secrecy

The third is generosity. Paul wrote that "God loves a cheerful giver", and the word cheerful is doing real work. The form and amount will differ with income, culture and conviction. Look for a generous disposition together with wisdom and the ability to say no, including to relatives and church members who ask repeatedly. And watch for the counterfeit: generosity that buys affection, approval or obligation is not generosity.

The fourth is secrecy. Privacy about the details is legitimate in dating; secrecy about material facts is not. Material facts include debts, legal liabilities, dependants, regular family obligations, significant gambling losses, bankruptcy, and commitments that would substantially affect a shared household. You do not need to see statements, and honesty never requires passwords or account access. But watch for a person whose accounts of their finances keep shifting, or who has presented themselves as wealthier or poorer than they are.

11 · Ambition and responsibility

The fifth is ambition. Drive is good, and Scripture commends diligence. Be fair, too: someone working long hours may be surviving rather than worshipping money. The question is what a person would sacrifice for money: their integrity, their church, their family, their rest, you? Paul's warning is precise: not that money is a root of evil, but that "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils." Watch how they handle risk: products they cannot explain, promises of guaranteed returns, borrowing to speculate, chasing losses. "Wealth gained hastily will dwindle, but whoever gathers little by little will increase it."

The sixth is responsibility, read fairly. Payment history can reflect poverty, delayed salary or illness rather than character. So ask: do they take obligations seriously, communicate honestly when they cannot meet them, and seek workable solutions rather than disappearing or blaming? That is faithfulness in a very little, and it is a reasonable training ground for marriage.

12 · Point 3: Money and the two of you

Our third point is money and the two of you. Who pays, and does it create obligation? Cultures differ, and so do couples. What matters is that neither person is keeping score, and neither is using payment as leverage. Gifts do not purchase access, affection, sex, commitment, or influence over your decisions.

How do they treat your money? A person who is careful with their own and casual with yours, borrowing, assuming you will pay, treating what is yours as shared before anything has been shared, is showing you something. So is a person who treats your financial decisions as theirs to approve. And what are your own patterns? If you are the one who over-gives, who cannot say no, who uses money to secure affection, or who hides your own debt, this lesson is looking at you too.

And in many parts of the world, adults regularly send money to parents and siblings. Lesson 10 called this honourable, and it can be. What matters is whether the commitment is freely acknowledged, sustainable, transparent before marriage, and balanced with responsibility towards a future household.

13 · Point 4: Warning signs

Our fourth point is warning signs, and what is beyond warning. Lesson 6 distinguished patterns that predict harm from harm itself. The same distinction applies here. Warning signs deserve attention, and if they persist, a serious conversation and outside counsel.

Repeated borrowing, particularly with entitlement, deception or broken agreements. Hardship and bad character are not the same, so notice honesty and agreed changes. Pressure to lend, co-sign or guarantee. Gambling: not occasional, culturally accepted activity, but concealed losses, chasing losses, or gambling with essential funds. Debt or obligations that are hidden, minimised, or discovered rather than disclosed. Spending that consistently contradicts stated values. Gifts used to create obligation, or money used to control. Treating your money as theirs. Refusal to discuss money at all, once the relationship is serious enough.

Weigh seriousness, repetition, deception and the response when challenged. One serious incident may need immediate action; several smaller concerns may reveal a pattern.

14 · Do not co-sign in dating

Pressure to co-sign deserves its own word. As a general dating safeguard, do not co-sign or guarantee another person's debt. A co-signer may become legally responsible for the entire debt if the borrower does not pay, and missed payments may affect your own financial record. The rules vary by country, but the commitment is potentially serious.

If exceptional circumstances make you consider it, get independent legal and financial advice first, not from the partner, the lender or a salesperson, and go ahead only if you could afford to repay the whole amount yourself. Proverbs' warning about putting up security for another is not a universal law about modern lending, but it is old wisdom about the danger of carrying a debt that belongs to someone else.

15 · Beyond warning: romance scams

Financial exploitation is not a warning sign. It is harm, and the response is the one Lesson 6 gave: safety, outside help, not another conversation.

Any request for money from a romantic interest you know only online should be treated as a serious scam warning, whatever the story and however urgent it seems. Do not send money while you try to work out whether the story is true. The pattern is consistent: intense affection early, a crisis that requires urgent money, reasons why meeting is impossible, and a second crisis after the first is paid. Requests for wire transfers, gift cards or cryptocurrency are typical. And scammers now use video calls and stolen identities; a video call is not proof of identity.

Being deceived does not make someone foolish. Scammers deliberately manipulate trust, affection and vulnerability.

16 · If you have already paid

If you have already paid: stop further payments and contact. Contact the bank, card company, money-transfer service or platform immediately, and ask whether the payment can be stopped or traced. Keep the messages, receipts and profile details. Report the profile to the platform, and the fraud to the right authority in your country. Change any compromised passwords from a safe device. If you shared identification documents or financial account details, ask your bank or the relevant authority how to protect yourself against identity misuse. The money is not always recoverable, and "recovery agents" who promise to get it back for a fee are frequently a second scam.

And never transfer money, cryptocurrency or account access to an investment introduced by an online romantic interest. Scammers build trust, then send people to trading sites that look real but are controlled by them. Check any investment independently, through the regulator in your country.

17 · Financial abuse

Financial abuse looks like this: taking your money, cards or documents. Forcing you to sign financial papers. Opening accounts or credit in your name. Withholding money for essentials. Preventing access to work or education. Demanding receipts or justification for every purchase. Using immigration sponsorship as leverage. Making you financially dependent, step by step, until leaving would mean losing everything.

One person earning less, staying home, studying, living with disability, or receiving agreed support is not itself financial abuse. The concern is whether access, information and freedom are deliberately restricted, or used to stop someone leaving.

If financial control is part of a wider pattern, suddenly freezing accounts or confronting the person may increase the risk. If you fear their reaction, seek confidential domestic-abuse and financial advice before making changes they may notice, and use a safe device. Guidance is on the page beneath this lesson.

18 · What to learn when

You do not need to know everything in dating. You need enough to decide whether a shared future is possible, and enough to keep yourself safe. Early dating: attitudes and habits, seen more than heard. Before exclusivity: facts that affect safety or the legitimacy of the relationship. Before engagement: substantial disclosure of debts, dependants, obligations, income and legal liabilities, from both of you, and verified where appropriate. Before marriage: detailed planning, and, where there are significant debts, assets or cross-border situations, independent local advice.

And as a strong general safeguard while you are dating: avoid merged accounts, co-signed debt, shared property and financial dependence. If unusual circumstances make any of these necessary, use written agreements and independent legal advice before committing.

19 · Where you are right now

And where you are right now. If you are not dating, look honestly at your own money first. Your habits will be among the first things a future spouse learns about you. If you are dating and money has never been discussed, and the relationship is serious enough that it should be, it is time: not a full disclosure, but an ordinary conversation about what each of you believes money is for.

If you have noticed ordinary concerns, seek wise counsel and decide whether a conversation is appropriate. You are free to slow down or end a relationship. If there is intimidation, coercion or exploitation, do not treat it as a normal disagreement to resolve together. Put your safety first and seek independent support.

And if you have recognised exploitation, a request from someone you have never met, your money quietly becoming theirs, a dependence you did not choose, put the conversation aside and get help. You are not foolish. You offered trust, and someone chose to exploit it.

20 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much. Look not at how much, but at how: spending, debt, generosity, secrecy, ambition and responsibility. Between the two of you, no score-keeping and no leverage, and an honest look at your own patterns. And remember: ordinary concerns deserve discernment. Exploitation calls for protection and help.

In Jesus' name, amen.
