Transcript and Scripture references

1 · How the enemy works

Welcome back to Stand Firm. In Lesson 2 we saw that the enemy is real, but he is not God’s equal, and Christ has already defeated him. Today we ask a practical question: how does the enemy work? How do I recognise his schemes when they are happening?

2 · Imagine a woman named Mei

Imagine a woman named Mei. She has led a small group at her church for six years. One evening, after a leaders’ meeting, another leader makes a careless comment about her group in front of everyone. Mei says nothing, but on the drive home she replays it again and again. Over the next few weeks, small things begin to add up. She notices every time that leader is praised. She starts to wonder whether people at church really value her. When she prays, a heavy thought presses in: you have never been good enough for ministry anyway. By the end of the month, Mei is quietly thinking about leaving the church altogether. Nothing dramatic has happened. There has been no obvious attack. And yet something is slowly pulling Mei away from peace, from her church family and from confidence in God’s love. Let us open the Bible.

3 · 2 Corinthians 2:10–11

Second Corinthians chapter 2, verses 10 and 11. “Anyone whom you forgive, I also forgive. Indeed, what I have forgiven, if I have forgiven anything, has been for your sake in the presence of Christ, so that we would not be outwitted by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his designs.”

4 · Point 1: A verse about forgiveness

Our first main point: this is a verse about forgiveness. It is worth noticing where it comes from. Paul is not giving a general lecture on demons. He is writing to a church that has disciplined one of its members. That person has now repented, and Paul urges the church to “turn to forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow,” and to “reaffirm your love for him.”

5 · Two dangers

Only then does Paul mention Satan. The risk he sees is twofold: a repentant man crushed by despair, and a church that withholds forgiveness and becomes hard and divided. Both would serve the enemy’s purposes. That tells us something important. Some of the enemy’s designs have nothing to do with the dramatic. They happen in ordinary church relationships: in unforgiveness, in discouragement and in quiet division. That is exactly where Mei’s struggle is taking place.

6 · Point 2: Five patterns at a glance

Our second main point: five patterns at a glance. The New Testament describes the enemy’s work in several ways. Here are five recurring patterns: lies, accusation, temptation, fear and division. They are not a complete list, but they give us a useful starting point. We will look at each one briefly.

7 · Lies

First, lies. Jesus called the devil “a liar and the father of lies.” From the garden onwards, deception has been part of his work. The serpent’s first words twisted God’s command: “Did God actually say, …?” And deception is not always obvious. Paul warns that “even Satan disguises himself as an angel of light.” A lie can sound spiritual. It can contain some truth. It can quietly shift our focus away from Christ. The answer to lies is truth: God’s Word, understood in context and held together with other believers.

8 · Accusation

Second, accusation. In a vision recorded by the prophet Zechariah, Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the Lord in filthy garments, “and Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him.” But the Lord rebukes the accuser, and Joshua’s filthy garments are removed and replaced with clean ones. Revelation calls the devil “the accuser of our brothers.” We will come back to accusation in a moment, because it needs care.

9 · Temptation

Third, temptation. The tempter came to Jesus in the wilderness, as we saw in Lesson 2. Yet Scripture never allows us to blame the devil for every temptation. James is clear that each person is tempted by his own desire. The enemy can exploit our desires, but he cannot make us choose sin. And God gives a promise for every believer facing temptation: “with the temptation he will also provide the way of escape, that you may be able to endure it.” There is always a faithful path.

10 · Fear

Fourth, fear. We need to be careful here. Fear is a normal human response, and not every fear is a spiritual attack. Much of it comes from real dangers, hard experiences or the strain of life. But fear can also be used to drive us away from obedience, from trust or from God’s people. Paul reminded Timothy that “God gave us a spirit not of fear but of power and love and self-control.” And Peter does not tell us to run from the roaring lion. He says, “Resist him, firm in your faith…”

11 · Division

Fifth, division. Paul writes, “Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil.” Anger is not always sin, but anger left to grow can give the enemy room to work. Division often begins small: a careless word, a hurt left unspoken, a story we tell ourselves about someone’s motives. That is how Mei’s month began. Her hurt was real. But as she replayed it and withdrew from others, she became increasingly isolated.

12 · Point 3: Conviction is not accusation

Our third main point: conviction is not accusation. This is one of the most important things to learn in this lesson. It is important to distinguish accusation from conviction, but the difference cannot be judged simply by how painful or specific a thought feels. Accusation can point to a real sin, and recognising genuine wrongdoing can hurt deeply.

13 · The better question

The better question is this. Does the thought truthfully identify wrongdoing and point towards repentance and God’s mercy? Or does it turn our failures into a verdict that we are beyond his grace? Thoughts like you are worthless, God is finished with you, or you will never change belong to the second kind. Paul tells the Corinthians that godly grief leads to repentance. Godly grief draws us to the cross. Condemnation pushes us away from it.

14 · Not God’s verdict over her

The thought that pressed on Mei, you have never been good enough, should be tested against the gospel, as we learned in Lesson 1. Whatever its source, it is not God’s verdict over her. “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” And if a thought does point to genuine wrongdoing, Romans 8:1 does not cancel the need to deal with it. A real wrong may still need confession and, where possible, repair. The difference is that we do it as forgiven people, not as people trying to earn our way back.

15 · Mei’s way back

Return to Mei. Imagine that, before leaving the church, she decides to speak to her pastor. As they talk, she begins to see the pattern. A real hurt had been left unspoken. Comparison had grown into resentment. And an accusing thought had made it all feel like proof that she did not belong. Her pastor prays with her. Then he encourages her to do something difficult: to speak privately and gently with the other leader. The conversation is awkward at first. But the other leader apologises. It had been a careless comment, not a judgement on Mei’s ministry. Their friendship does not become perfect overnight, but it is restored.

16 · Not every story ends this well

Not every situation ends this well. Some conflicts take a long time to resolve, and some relationships need wise boundaries. Where there is persistent harm or serious misconduct, seeking accountable help, and sometimes a healthier church community, can be a faithful step. But Mei’s story shows how ordinary faithfulness, honesty, prayer, humility and forgiveness, can stand against the enemy’s designs.

17 · Eyes on Christ

Some Christians think that being “not ignorant of his designs” means studying the enemy in great detail, or seeing his hand in every difficulty. That is not Paul’s point. Paul was concerned that the church would not be outwitted, and his answer was forgiveness, comfort and love. We learn how the enemy works so that we can recognise his designs. Then we turn our full attention back to Jesus.

18 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Paul’s words about the enemy’s designs come in a passage about forgiveness. The enemy works through lies, accusation, temptation, fear and division, often in quiet, ordinary ways. And conviction is not accusation: one draws us to the cross, the other pushes us away. Truth, grace and forgiveness equip us to resist the enemy’s schemes.

19 · This week’s practice

Here is your practice for this week. Write down one accusing thought, a thought that tells you that you are beyond God’s grace. Then write Romans 8:1 beside it: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” Then ask three questions. Is there a specific wrong here that needs confession or repair? It is fine if the answer is no, or if you are unsure. What condemning verdict do I need to reject? And in light of Romans 8:1, what is one faithful step I can take? Remember, a thought can point to something genuinely wrong and still carry a false verdict about your worth. And if you would like a short daily guide, you will find the seven-day Battle Plan on the lesson page.

20 · Remember

Remember this word of Scripture: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” And here is this week’s declaration, a statement of trust: I will hold to the truth, receive God’s forgiveness, and seek peace with God’s people. My eyes are on Christ.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, thank you that you know every scheme of the enemy, and that you have already triumphed over him. Give me wisdom to recognise lies, and the courage to hold on to your truth. When I hear accusation, remind me that there is no condemnation for those who are in you. When I am tempted, show me the way of escape. When I am afraid, help me to stand firm in faith. Where there is hurt or division in my relationships, give me humility to seek peace and grace to forgive. Keep my eyes on you. Amen. A wise first step with anger

If there is a hurt between you and another believer, the aim is not to force agreement before bedtime but to avoid nursing resentment. You may need time, rest or help before a constructive conversation. Ask God for wisdom about the first step, and talk with a mature Christian if you need help. Forgiveness does not always mean trust is restored at once, or that harm is excused.
