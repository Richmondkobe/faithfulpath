Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Guarding your heart and passing faith on

Welcome back to Stand Firm. In Lesson 10 we saw that every believer belongs to Christ and is free in him, and that we walk in that freedom together. Now we begin Part 4, living in victory. Today’s lesson asks two questions that belong together. How do I keep my heart, and help someone else’s faith grow?

2 · Imagine a man named Tomas

Imagine a man named Tomas. He has followed Christ for more than twenty-five years. He is a deacon, he leads a home group, and younger believers look up to him. If you had asked him a few years ago whether he could ever drift from God, he would have said no. But slowly, almost without noticing, things have changed. A painful conflict at church left him quietly cynical. His work has become more demanding, and his time with God has shrunk to a hurried prayer in the car. He still leads his home group, but he no longer shares anything personal. Recently he has begun exchanging late-night messages with a colleague who seems to understand him better than anyone else. Nothing physical has happened, but the secrecy is already harming his marriage, and he has not mentioned the messages to his wife. Tomas knows something is wrong. How did I get here? Let us open the Bible.

3 · Proverbs 4:20–27

Proverbs chapter 4, verses 20 to 27. My son, be attentive to my words; incline your ear to my sayings. Let them not escape from your sight; keep them within your heart. For they are life to those who find them, and healing to all their flesh. Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life. Put away from you crooked speech, and put devious talk far from you. Let your eyes look directly forward, and your gaze be straight before you. Ponder the path of your feet; then all your ways will be sure. Do not swerve to the right or to the left; turn your foot away from evil.

4 · Point 1: What the Bible means by “heart”

Our first main point: what the Bible means by the heart, and how hearts drift. In everyday speech, the heart usually means our emotions. But in Scripture the heart is much broader. The Hebrew word is lēb: the inner centre of a person, where we think, desire, decide and trust. Jesus said, “out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks.” That is why Proverbs says that “from it flow the springs of life.” What happens in the heart eventually shapes everything else: our words, our choices and our relationships. If the spring is clouded, the stream will be too.

5 · A father’s instruction

Proverbs 4 is written as a father’s instruction to his son, and around the command to guard the heart, he speaks of the whole person. First he tells his son what the heart is meant to hold: “keep them within your heart.” Guarding the heart begins with receiving and keeping God’s wisdom. Then he speaks of our ears: “incline your ear to my sayings.” Our mouths: “Put away from you crooked speech.” Our eyes: “Let your eyes look directly forward.” And our feet: “Ponder the path of your feet.” So guarding the heart is not only an inward feeling. It involves what we listen to, what we say, what we look at and where we go. The heart shapes the whole of life, and our daily choices shape the heart.

6 · How hearts drift

Few believers wake up one morning and decide to abandon their faith. More often, drift happens gradually. Hebrews warns, “we must pay much closer attention to what we have heard, lest we drift away from it.” Hebrews is warning us to hold on to the good news of Jesus. Here are four ways drift can begin. Neglect: other priorities slowly crowd God out. Nursed resentment: hurt we keep feeding, until it shapes how we see others and God. Isolation: we stop letting anyone see our inner life. And small compromises: sin rarely arrives as a dramatic choice, and secrecy is often the first warning sign. Long years of faith do not make us immune. Paul writes, “let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall.” None of this is meant to make us anxious. It is meant to make us honest, so that we notice drift early and turn back.

7 · God guards our hearts too

Guarding the heart is our responsibility, but we are not left to do it alone. Paul writes, “do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.” Notice that it is God’s peace that guards the heart. And Jude reminds us that God “is able to keep you from stumbling.” So we guard our hearts with vigilance, but not with fear. We keep coming back to the One who keeps us.

8 · Point 2: Rhythms that keep the heart

Our second main point: rhythms that keep the heart. Guarding the heart is not mainly about a few dramatic decisions. It is shaped by ordinary rhythms, repeated over time. Time alone with God: even in his busiest days, Jesus rose “very early in the morning, while it was still dark,” and went out to pray. Rest: when his disciples were worn out, Jesus said, “Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while.” Gratitude: “Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits…” And honest friendship: a heart that is known by others is harder to lose in secret. These rhythms look different in different seasons. A brief prayer, listening to Scripture, or accepting help can sustain faith when long quiet times are hard. Being exhausted is not spiritual failure.

9 · Faithful over the long haul

The Christian life is not only a series of battles. It is a long journey of faithfulness. Hebrews calls us to “run with endurance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith.” Some seasons feel dry. In Psalm 73, Asaph admits that his feet had almost stumbled, yet in God’s presence he could say, “My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.” And near the end of his life Paul could say, “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” That is the goal: not a perfect record, but a life that keeps returning to Christ to the end.

10 · Tomas turns back

Imagine that one evening, after another long exchange of messages, Tomas reads Proverbs 4:23 in his daily reading plan. It stops him. The next day he tells his wife about the messages. She is hurt, and honesty begins a slow process of rebuilding trust. Tomas accepts responsibility without expecting quick reassurance. He ends the private messaging and keeps his contact with his colleague professional. He asks an older friend from church to meet with him regularly and ask him honest questions. Over the following months he sets aside unhurried time with God each morning, talks with his pastor about the old church conflict, and steps back from one ministry role for a season so that he can rest. His heart does not change overnight. But it is turning again towards the springs of life.

11 · Point 3: It begins with our own hearts

Our third main point: passing faith on. A guarded heart does not only keep us. It helps someone else’s faith grow. In Deuteronomy 6, before Moses says anything about children, he calls the people to love God “with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might.” Then he says, “these words that I command you today shall be on your heart.” Here the Hebrew word for heart is lēbāb, a fuller form of the same word we met in Proverbs. Only then does he say, “You shall teach them diligently to your children.” The order matters. Passing on faith begins with our own love for God. That does not mean we must be perfect. Our walk with God, including the way we repent and return to him, is part of what we pass on.

12 · Faith in ordinary moments

Notice where this teaching happens: “when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise.” These are ordinary moments: meals, journeys, bedtimes and mornings. Many of the most lasting conversations about faith happen unplanned: a question in the car, a worry at bedtime. We do not need to be Bible scholars. We need to be available, honest and ready to point to God. And when a child asks why we follow God’s ways, Moses says the answer begins with a story: “We were Pharaoh’s slaves in Egypt. And the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand.” Our answers, too, can begin with what God has done.

13 · A sincere faith passed on

We see this in Timothy’s life. Paul wrote to him, “I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well.” And he reminded Timothy that from childhood he had known “the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.” Their example shows what it can look like to encourage faith across generations.

14 · A calling for every believer

So passing faith on is not only for parents. It is a calling open to every mature believer. Paul was not Timothy’s father, yet he called him “my beloved child.” Passing faith on can include parenting, mentoring, friendship and service in the church. A grandparent who prays, a friend who reads Scripture with someone, a Sunday school teacher, a youth leader: each can shape a growing faith. As the psalmist says, “We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD.”

15 · Without fear, with honest questions

When children are afraid of spiritual things, start with Jesus. Speak simply and calmly: there is evil in the world, but God is greater, and they can always talk to him and to us. John wrote, “he who is in you is greater than he who is in the world.” Give them words to pray, such as “In peace I will both lie down and sleep; for you alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety.” And when young people begin to ask hard questions, listen before answering, admit when you do not know, and look for answers together. Questions are not the enemy of faith.

16 · Faithful, not in control

Many parents take comfort in Proverbs 22:6: “Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it.” It is a wise and encouraging proverb. But proverbs describe how life generally works under God; they are not unconditional guarantees. Each child must come to faith personally. We are called to be faithful, not to control the outcome. If you are grieving someone who has wandered, keep praying and keep showing love. Jesus pictured a father who saw his returning son “while he was still a long way off,” and ran to welcome him.

17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. The heart is the centre of life, and hearts drift slowly, so we notice drift early and turn back. We keep our hearts through ordinary rhythms: time with God, rest, gratitude and honest friendship, trusting the God whose peace guards us. And from a guarded heart, faith is passed on, in ordinary moments, through parenting, mentoring, friendship and service. We guard our hearts in Christ, and a guarded heart helps another person’s faith grow.

18 · This week’s practice

Here is your practice for this week. Choose one daily rhythm that guards your heart, and keep it all week. It might be time with God before you look at your phone, a short prayer of thanks at the end of each day, or reading a psalm on your way to work. Write down when and where you will keep it. Think of Tomas, who might write: Each morning before work, I’ll read Scripture and pray, with my phone in another room. Then name one person whose faith you can encourage this week, and one simple way to do it. You might write: On Thursday I’ll ask a friend from church how I can pray for them. And if you would like a short daily guide, you will find the seven-day Battle Plan on the lesson page.

19 · Remember

Remember this word of Scripture: “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life.” And here is this week’s declaration, a statement of trust: I will guard my heart, for from it flow the springs of life. I will keep running with endurance, looking to Jesus. I will speak of his faithfulness to the next generation, and trust him with their story.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray. Father, you know my heart better than I do. Show me where I have drifted, and give me grace to turn back to you. Help me to listen to your word, to speak what is true, to keep my eyes on what is good and to walk in your ways. Guard my heart and mind with your peace. Deepen my love for you, so that my life points others to you. Help me to speak of your faithfulness in ordinary moments, and to trust you with what I cannot control. Keep me faithful to the end. In Jesus’ name, Amen. This week, keep one daily rhythm that guards your heart, and encourage one person’s faith. Then stop here for today, or continue to Lesson 12.

Kwame and Efua’s story

Imagine a couple named Kwame and Efua. They have two children. Their eight-year-old daughter, Akosua, heard frightening stories from her cousins about witches and evil spirits, and now she is afraid to sleep with the light off. Their fifteen-year-old son, Kojo, has grown quiet about his faith; last week he asked, “How do we even know any of this is true?” Kwame and Efua begin praying with Akosua each night before she sleeps. Efua teaches her Psalm 4:8, and they gently explain that frightening stories are not always true, and that Jesus is always with her and stronger than anything that frightens her. They listen when she is scared, without making her prove that she is brave. After a few weeks Akosua still likes a small night light, and bedtime has become calmer. With Kojo, Kwame tries a different approach. Instead of lecturing, he asks his son to share his questions, and they agree to look into one each week. Kwame admits he does not know every answer. He also asks Kojo whether he would like to meet with a young man from their church whom he respects, and Kojo agrees. Kojo’s questions are not all resolved, but he knows his faith is something he can explore honestly, and that his parents will keep loving him whatever he decides.

Talk about it (for a small group)
  1. How does Philippians 4:6–7 help us guard our hearts without anxiety?
  2. What ordinary moments in your week could become opportunities to talk about faith?
  3. What would it look like for you to be faithful over the long haul?
