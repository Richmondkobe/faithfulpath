Transcript and Scripture references

1 · The breastplate and the shoes

Welcome back to Stand Firm. In Lesson 4 we heard Paul’s call to be strong in the Lord, and we looked at the belt of truth. Today we look at the next two pieces of armour: the breastplate of righteousness and the shoes of the gospel of peace. Together they answer a question many believers carry: what steadies my heart when I feel accused or unsettled?

2 · Imagine a woman named Priya

Imagine a woman named Priya. She grew up in a home where love always seemed to depend on performance. Good marks brought warmth; mistakes brought silence. When she became a Christian at university, she was overwhelmed by grace. But over the years, without noticing, she slipped back into the old pattern. Now Priya serves in three ministries at church, and she rarely says no. On good weeks she feels close to God. But when she fails, she feels as if God’s approval has been withdrawn. One Sunday, after a sharp word to a teenager in the children’s ministry, she sits in her car and thinks: how can someone like you call herself a Christian? Many sincere believers swing between those two places: trying to earn God’s acceptance, and feeling crushed when they fall short. Let us open the Bible.

3 · Ephesians 6:14–15

Ephesians chapter 6, verses 14 and 15. “Stand therefore, having fastened on the belt of truth, and having put on the breastplate of righteousness, and, as shoes for your feet, having put on the readiness given by the gospel of peace.”

4 · Covering the centre, keeping your footing

Let us see what Paul’s two pictures mean. A breastplate covered the chest, where a blow could do the most damage. Some attacks aim at the very centre of our relationship with God. Is he really for me? Do I really belong to him? Am I accepted, or only tolerated? The shoes picture something else. Roman soldiers commonly wore sturdy sandal-boots, often studded with nails for grip. They helped a soldier keep his footing on rough ground and stay steady under pressure. We should not press every detail of the picture, but the main idea is clear: you cannot stand firm if your feet keep slipping.

5 · God wears it first

As we saw in Lesson 4, this armour has deep roots. The prophet Isaiah describes the Lord coming to save his people: “He put on righteousness as a breastplate…” Before Paul ever told believers to put it on, God wore it first. So the righteousness that protects us begins with God himself, not with us.

6 · “Readiness” from the gospel of peace

The word translated “readiness” can mean preparation, or being prepared. Some readers hear it mainly as firm footing; others hear it as being ready to go and share the good news. Both ideas fit the rest of Scripture, and we will look at each. But what matters most is the source: the gospel of peace. Paul is not describing a technique for staying calm. He is describing what the good news of Jesus gives to those who believe it.

7 · Point 1: Righteousness received

Our first main point: the breastplate is righteousness received, and then lived. Bible teachers discuss what Paul means by righteousness here. Some emphasise the righteousness God gives us in Christ; others emphasise the righteous life Christians are called to live. Both ideas are biblical, and they belong together. First, the righteousness God gives. Listen to how Paul puts it. “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” This is the foundation. Our standing before God does not rest on our performance. It rests on Christ: his perfect life, his death in our place and his resurrection.

8 · Righteousness lived

Second, the righteousness we live. In this same letter Paul calls believers to “put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness.” God does not only declare us righteous. He also begins to make us righteous in how we live. These two must never be confused, and never be separated. We do not live rightly in order to be accepted. We live rightly because we have been accepted. And because someone will always ask, Paul answers the question himself: “Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means!” Grace does not make holiness optional. It makes holiness possible.

9 · A welcome given, not earned

Jesus told a parable about a son who comes home. When he returns, the father says, “Bring quickly the best robe, and put it on him…” He welcomes his son before any long record of changed behaviour could be established. The father’s welcome helps us picture acceptance that is given rather than earned. For Priya, that is a powerful reminder of the grace she has received in Christ.

10 · Answering the accuser

So what does Priya do with the thought in her car? Paul gives her a better question to ask. “Who shall bring any charge against God’s elect? It is God who justifies. Who is to condemn? Christ Jesus is the one who died—more than that, who was raised—who is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us.” Notice how Paul answers. He does not say, “You have never done anything wrong.” He points away from us to God and to Christ. When a thought tells Priya that she cannot really be a Christian, the answer is not to list her good deeds. The answer is to look to her Advocate.

11 · Priya learns to rest

This does not mean her sharp words to the teenager do not matter. They do. She should apologise. But her apology can flow from security, not from fear. She does not need to win back God’s love. She needs to walk in it. Imagine that Priya shares her struggle with an older woman in her church, who listens carefully and then asks a simple question: “Do you serve because you are loved, or so that you will be loved?” The question stays with her. Over the following months, Priya steps back from one of her three ministries, not out of guilt, but to make room for rest and time with God. She begins each morning with a short passage about what Christ has done. The old pattern does not disappear overnight. But slowly, her service becomes a response to a verdict already given.

12 · Point 2: The gospel of peace gives us firm ground

Our second main point: the gospel of peace gives us firm ground and prepares us to carry good news. We begin with the firm ground. The deepest peace the gospel brings is peace with God. “Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.” The war between us and God, caused by our sin, has been ended by Christ. That is firm ground. When accusation or fear tries to unsettle us, we are not standing on our feelings or our performance. We are standing on what Christ has done. This connects closely with the breastplate: because we are right with God through Christ, we have peace with him.

13 · Peace that holds in trouble

On the night before his death, Jesus said to his disciples, “I have said these things to you, that in me you may have peace. In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world.” Jesus did not promise a trouble-free life. In the same breath that he spoke of peace, he spoke of tribulation. The peace he gives is not the absence of trouble. It is a settled security in him that can remain while trouble is still present. Feeling anxious or unsettled does not mean that you have lost your peace with God. The shoes of the gospel of peace do not stop the ground from shaking. They help us keep our footing.

14 · Point 3: Ready to carry good news

Our third main point: the gospel of peace makes us ready to carry good news. In Ephesians, peace also has a wider meaning. Paul writes that Christ “himself is our peace,” breaking down the dividing wall between groups once divided by deep hostility. So the gospel of peace is not only about our private relationship with God. It creates a family where former enemies become one. We cannot control how others respond, but we can seek peace as far as it depends on us. Paul’s words also echo the prophet Isaiah: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace…” The gospel that steadies our feet also sends us out.

15 · With gentleness and respect

Peter shows us what that readiness looks like. He writes to believers who may suffer for doing good. Even under pressure, they are to honour Christ and answer with gentleness and respect: “always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect…” First, we are prepared: we have thought about what we believe and why. Second, witness is a conversation, not an attack. Third, we speak with gentleness and respect. The way we share the gospel of peace should match its message. And notice how this joins the two pieces of armour. One day a colleague asks Priya why her faith matters so much to her. For years her answer would have been about trying harder. Now she can speak of grace: she has been accepted because of Christ, and she has peace with God.

16 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. We stand on what Christ has given: his righteousness and peace with God. The breastplate is righteousness received, and then lived. The gospel of peace gives us firm ground: peace with God through Christ. And that same gospel of peace makes us ready to carry good news to others, with gentleness and respect.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Father, thank you that my standing with you does not rest on my performance, but on Jesus, who died for me, rose again and now intercedes for me. When accusation comes, help me to look to my Advocate. Where I have sinned, give me honesty to confess it and grace to make it right. Thank you that through Christ I have peace with you. Give me firm footing when life is unsettled, and prepare me to share the good news with gentleness and respect. In Jesus’ name, Amen. Somchai finds his footing

Imagine a young man named Somchai, the first person in his family to follow Jesus. At festival times the tension grows, and whenever he tries to explain his faith the conversation becomes an argument. An older Christian who also came to faith from a family of another religion advises him to honour his parents in every way he can with a clear conscience, to help at home, to be patient, and to answer simply and kindly when they ask. Over the next months Somchai stops trying to win every argument. The tension does not disappear. But one evening his mother quietly asks, “What is it about Jesus that matters so much to you?” This time, Somchai is ready, and he answers with gentleness.

Where family pressure becomes threatening or coercive, it is right to seek help from trusted church leaders and, where needed, from others who can protect you.

Talk about it (for a small group)
  1. Why is it important that our standing with God comes first, and our way of life grows out of it?
  2. What is the difference between seeking peace and simply avoiding every disagreement?
  3. How might the way we share our faith either match or contradict the gospel of peace?
