# Lesson 6 — Red Flags Christians Spiritualise

> ### Before you begin
> This lesson names behaviour that is not ordinary relationship difficulty. If
> you are afraid of the person you are dating, or if anything here describes your
> relationship, you do not need to finish the lesson before getting help.
>
> **Finding Help Where You Live →** · **Leave this page →**

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name seven patterns that may
signal an unsafe or controlling relationship, recognise spiritual language used
to disguise them, and understand why severity sometimes matters more than
repetition.

**Key Scripture**
"The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for
it." — Proverbs 22:3 (ESV)

Proverbs is describing ordinary practical wisdom, not cowardice and not
suspicion. Notice what the prudent person does with what they see: they act on
it. Seeing danger and then explaining it away is not prudence. And note that
Scripture does not treat self-protection as a failure of faith or of love.

## Core teaching

Warning signs are clearer in someone else's relationship than in our own. From
outside, we are not affected by attraction, hope, fear and spiritual pressure. A
friend describes a boyfriend who checks her phone, a girlfriend who has cut him
off from his family, a fiancé who is always sorry and never different — and we
can see it.

In our own relationships it is harder, and in Christian relationships there is a
particular difficulty this lesson exists to name. **The language of faith can turn
a warning sign into a virtue.** A rushed engagement becomes God's timing. Control
becomes spiritual leadership. Isolation becomes guarding what God has given us.
Secrecy becomes keeping it between us and the Lord. Jealousy becomes protection.
A refusal to be questioned becomes *you need to trust me the way you trust God*.

None of these reframings changes what the behaviour is. Whether used deliberately
or not, they make it harder for you to object, question or slow things down.

**Three categories, kept distinct.** *Compatibility differences* concern fit —
faith practice, family expectations, money or direction — and usually call for
honest conversation. *Concerning conduct* includes the seven areas below.
Repetition strengthens the evidence of a pattern, but severity, context and
effect also matter; some conduct deserves action the first time. *Abuse or
immediate safety concerns* — including violence, sexual coercion, stalking,
credible threats, intimidation and coercive control — require specialist support
rather than patient observation or ordinary relationship work.

A red flag is not a flaw. Everyone has flaws, and Lesson 5 was about watching
character patiently. A red flag is a pattern indicating that the relationship is
unsafe, dishonest, or being steered against your own judgement. The response to a
flaw is patience and honesty. The response to a red flag is boundaries, outside
help, and sometimes the end of the relationship.

**1. Spiritual manipulation.** The master pattern, because it is the disguise the
other six most often wear: the use of God, Scripture, prophecy or spiritual
authority to end a disagreement, override your judgement or place a person beyond
question. *God told me you're my wife. If you really trusted God, you wouldn't
question me. You need to submit. The devil is trying to separate us.*

See what these have in common. Each takes an ordinary question — *is this too
fast? is this respectful? do I agree?* — and replaces it with a spiritual question
you cannot win: *do you trust God? are you being obedient? are you on the devil's
side?* The speaker's preference is placed on God's side of the line and your
hesitation on the other.

Two principles for now, with Lesson 13 doing the fuller work. **A genuine word
from God does not require you to stop thinking in order to receive it.** Paul told
the Thessalonians to "test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians
5:21). The Bereans were commended for examining the Scriptures to check whether
even Paul was telling the truth (Acts 17:11). Testing is not unbelief; it is what
believers are told to do. And **a person's claim that God has spoken to them gives
them no authority over your conscience.** If God has directed them, they remain
responsible for presenting that humbly and leaving you entirely free to discern.

A note on *submit*. Christians read the marital roles in Ephesians 5 differently,
and those differences do not affect two clear conclusions: the passage addresses
marriage rather than dating, and it never permits coercion. A boyfriend cannot
claim a husband's role over a girlfriend. Nor may a husband use the passage to
silence or intimidate his wife — the command to husbands is to love as Christ
loved the church and "gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25), which is
self-giving, not licence. When *submit* is used before marriage to win an
argument, it is not being used as Scripture uses it.

**2. Jealousy disguised as protection.** Wanting to know where you are at all
times. Checking your phone. Objecting to your friends. Commenting on what you
wear. Needing to approve your plans. Interrogating you about where you have been.
The disguise is care: *I just worry about you. I'm guarding your heart.*

Genuine care can include raising a concern. The difference is what happens to
your freedom. Care respects your agency and wants your life to flourish. Control
restricts your choices, relationships and freedom, and punishes resistance. If
you have fewer people, fewer places and fewer freedoms than before the
relationship, that is not protection.

**3. Rushed commitment.** Declarations of love within days. Pressure towards
exclusivity or engagement far ahead of what you know about each other. Talk of
destiny and soulmates. Hurt when you ask for time. The disguise is God's timing,
or *when you know, you know*.

Speed serves anyone who does not want to be seen clearly. Every window in Lesson
5 takes time to open, and someone hurrying you towards commitment is hurrying you
past the point where you could have looked. Some people rush from insecurity
rather than calculation; the effect on your ability to discern is identical.
"Desire without knowledge is not good, and whoever makes haste with his feet
misses his way" (Proverbs 19:2). Be fair: an early declaration, or a courtship
shorter than another culture would expect, is not automatically a warning. The
warning is pressure, commitment disproportionate to knowledge, resistance to
questions, and punishment when you ask for time.

**4. Secrecy.** Being asked to hide the relationship from your parents, pastor or
friends. Accounts of their life that change. Explanations that do not add up.
Information withheld that you needed in order to decide wisely or stay safe. The
disguise is privacy.

Privacy and secrecy are different, and the difference matters, because a dating
partner is not entitled to everything about you. Privacy allows each person
appropriate space: confidential friendships, personal records, control of their
own devices, the right to share a painful history at their own pace. Refusing
phone access is not evidence of deception, and demanding phone access can itself
be controlling. Secrecy conceals the existence of the relationship, gives false
accounts of identity or circumstances, hides another partner, changes stories, or
withholds what directly affects your safety and consent. If someone needs the
relationship to be invisible to the people who love you, ask what would become
visible if it were not.

**5. Repeated boundary violations.** You say no and it is treated as the opening
of a negotiation. You set a limit and it is pushed, apologised for, pushed again.
You are having the same conversation about the same line every few weeks. The
disguise is passion, or *you're being legalistic*.

Respect becomes visible in how someone responds to your limit: do they accept it,
or repeatedly pressure, punish and negotiate against it? Whether deliberate or
not, a cycle of pressure, apology and renewed pressure shows the boundary is not
being respected. An apology without changed behaviour does not restore safety.

**6. Lack of accountability.** Hostility or subtle discouragement when you seek
counsel. Everyone who ever raised a concern has been cut off. The relationship is
framed as something you must protect from outside interference. The disguise is
maturity — *I don't need other people telling me how to live* — or spiritual
independence: *I answer to God.*

The concern is not an unconventional social history. Relocation, disability,
social anxiety, persecution or a harmful family can all explain a lack of long
friendships. The concern is repeated resistance to honest feedback, a pattern of
cutting off everyone who raises concerns, or an effort to separate you from
independent support. Isolation is the environment in which every other pattern
here grows. If the relationship is being moved, step by step, away from everyone
who could see it, that movement is the flag, whatever reasons are given for each
step.

**7. Refusal to accept correction.** Never wrong — or wrong in the abstract (*I
know I'm not perfect*) but never about the specific thing you raised. Apologies
containing no change. Correction producing anger, withdrawal, counter-accusation,
or a long explanation of why your raising it was the real problem. After a while
you notice you are the one who always ends up apologising. The disguise is *I'm
just honest*, or *you're too sensitive*.

Disagreement is not refusal of correction; someone may hear you and still
honestly see it differently. The warning is inability to listen, consider, take
responsibility or change. A person who cannot receive a concern now is unlikely
to become more receptive because you marry — and someone who turns every
correction back onto you is teaching you to stop raising things. That lesson,
once learned, is very hard to unlearn.

### What is serious the first time

The seven patterns above deserve serious attention. Some become clearer through
repetition; others may be serious in a single incident because of their severity
or effect on your freedom and safety.

Physical violence of any kind, including strangulation or choking. Blocking you
from leaving, or confining you. Threats to you, to others, to children or
animals, or involving weapons. Forced or coerced sexual activity. Reproductive
coercion. Threats involving children, immigration status or intimate images.
Stalking, tracking, following, impersonation, intrusion into your accounts, or
technology-enabled surveillance. Intimidation. Destruction of property used to
frighten you. Financial exploitation. Serious, deliberate deception about who
they are.

This list is not exhaustive. You do not need to find your exact experience named
here before seeking help.

**If you have been strangled or choked, seek urgent medical attention even if you
have no visible injuries and currently feel well. Strangulation can cause serious
internal injury and delayed complications.**

**These are not warning signs to observe patiently, and they are not differences
to work on.** Do not wait for a pattern. Do not give it more time to become clear.
If you are in immediate danger, contact the emergency service where you are, move
somewhere safer if you can, and use a device the other person cannot monitor. If
the danger is not immediate, contact a specialist domestic-abuse, sexual-violence
or stalking service and make a personalised safety plan. Do not begin with a
private confrontation, and do not use couples counselling as the first response.
If you fear their reaction, make that plan with a professional before you say
anything to them.

> **Digital safety.** If you suspect your device or accounts are monitored, get
> advice from a safe device before making sudden changes. Deleting messages,
> disabling tracking or changing passwords can alert the person and increase
> risk, so these steps belong inside a safety plan rather than as a first
> reaction.

> **Threats of self-harm.** Take all threats of self-harm seriously, but do not
> take on responsibility for keeping someone alive by staying with them. A
> genuine disclosure deserves compassion and professional help. A threat used to
> stop you leaving, setting a boundary or seeking help is both a safety issue and
> a form of coercion, and both need outside help. Where there is immediate
> danger, contact an emergency, crisis or mental-health service.

### Why this is hard for Christians to name

Several true teachings get distorted in ways that make warning signs harder to
see. In every case the teaching is true and the distortion is not.

**Forgiveness.** Jesus told Peter to forgive seventy times seven (Matthew 18:22).
Forgiveness does not automatically restore trust, remove consequences or require
reconciliation. You can forgive someone and still refuse them the opportunity to
harm you again.

**Not judging.** Jesus' warning against judgement does not forbid recognising
conduct or protecting yourself. In the same sermon he said, "by their fruits you
will recognise them" (Matthew 7:20). You can evaluate fruit without claiming to
know the state of anyone's soul.

**Not gossiping.** Seeking counsel about your own life is not gossip; it is wisdom
— "in an abundance of counsellors there is safety" (Proverbs 11:14). Choose
someone trustworthy, discreet and able to respond safely, ideally a trained
advocate, counsellor, safeguarding leader or pastor who understands abuse.
Sharing widely inside a mutual church or social circle can itself increase risk.

**Suffering as sanctification.** God does use hardship to form us. He does not ask
you to volunteer for mistreatment in order to be formed. Paul, who suffered more
than most, still warned Timothy about a man who had harmed him: "Alexander the
coppersmith did me great harm… Beware of him yourself" (2 Timothy 4:14–15).
Wariness is not unforgiveness.

**Grace.** Grace does not entitle anyone to another opportunity to harm you.
Repentance may be real, but restored trust requires safety, sustained change and
accountability — and in some cases reconciliation will be neither wise nor safe.

### Boundaries are not a technique

Lessons 8 and 9 spend a long time on boundaries, so this needs saying first.
Boundaries are how a healthy person protects what matters in a relationship with
another healthy person. They are not a method for making an unsafe person behave
better. If every limit becomes a fight, and you are exhausted from enforcing
lines that should not need enforcing, the answer is not better boundaries. Some
conduct calls for distance, professional help, or the end of the relationship —
not another conversation.

### What to do when you recognise something

**Consider severity and pattern together.** One sharp word is not a flag. One act
of violence is not *just once*.

**Take the spiritual language off.** Write down what was said, remove the reference
to God, Scripture, the devil or the pastor, and write what is actually being
asked of you. *If you trusted God you wouldn't question me* becomes *don't
question me*. *God told me you're my wife* becomes *commit to me now, without the
time to decide*. In plain words you can usually see it.

**Talk to someone outside the relationship** — not because they will automatically
be right, but because they can see the pattern without being inside it. If the
person you are dating would be angry that you did this, that is itself
information.

**Notice whether raising it is possible.** In a safe relationship you can say
*this bothered me* and be heard. You may not be agreed with, and they may be hurt
or need time. But you will not be intimidated, punished, threatened with
abandonment or spiritually pressured. If you are afraid to raise a concern, the
fear is telling you something the concern alone could not.

**If safety is involved, act on safety first.**

Before the tool, a prayer. Pray it if it fits; leave it if it does not.

> *Lord, give me the courage to call this what it is. I have been explaining it
> away, and I am tired. Give me courage to speak, even while I am afraid, to one
> safe person or specialist service that can help me. Where I have been told that
> seeing clearly is doubting you, show me that it is not. Where I am in danger,
> get me to help, and go with me. Amen.*

## Practical tool — Red Flag Checklist

For clarifying what you have actually observed. It is not a diagnosis of the
other person, and it must not be used to build a case, monitor them or label
them. Nothing here is scored.

> **Privacy and safety.** This checklist saves to your account. If someone may
> monitor your account, device or browser activity, do not record anything here
> that could place you at risk. Use a safer device where possible, leave
> sensitive fields blank, or speak with a specialist service instead of
> completing it at all. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

> **If you are not currently dating**, read Parts A and B to learn what the
> patterns look like, but do not complete the checklist about an imagined partner
> or use it to assess a friend. Part D is worth answering about yourself now.
> Keep the rest for when there is a real person and real evidence.

### Part A — Safety first

Answer *Yes / No*. **If any answer is Yes, stop here.** If you are in immediate
danger, contact the emergency service where you are. Otherwise, find specialist
support and seek individual help before taking any other step. Do not confront
the person alone if you fear their reaction.

- They have used physical force, blocked me from leaving, destroyed property to
  frighten me, or acted violently in a way that made me fear for my safety.
- They have threatened to harm me, another person or an animal.
- They have threatened self-harm or suicide in order to prevent me from leaving,
  setting a boundary, seeking help or speaking to someone else.
- They have pressured or coerced me sexually.
- They track, monitor or follow me without freely given consent, pressure me to
  share my location, access my accounts, impersonate me, or demand passwords.
- They have intimidated me, or I am afraid of how they will react to things I do
  or say.
- They take, restrict or monitor my money, create debt in my name, interfere with
  my work, or pressure me into financial decisions I do not freely choose.
- I have discovered serious, deliberate deception about who they are.

> Any disclosure of imminent self-harm or suicide requires crisis or emergency
> support, whether or not it is coercive. A threat made conditional on your
> obedience or continued presence also requires individual safety guidance for
> you. You are not responsible for preventing suicide by remaining in a
> relationship.

### Part B — The seven patterns

For each, answer *Observed / Not observed / Mixed evidence / Not sure*, with one
line of evidence for anything marked *Observed* or *Mixed*.

**Spiritual manipulation**
- They use God, Scripture, prophecy or a leader's authority to end disagreements
  or override my judgement.
- Questioning them is framed as doubting God, being disobedient or siding with
  the enemy.
- They have claimed divine confirmation to pressure a decision.

**Jealousy as protection**
- They want to know where I am and who I am with, and are unhappy when I have
  been somewhere without them.
- They object to my friendships, my family time, or how I dress.
- My life has become smaller — fewer people, places or freedoms — since the
  relationship began.

**Rushed commitment**
- They pressed for exclusivity, engagement or marriage well ahead of what we
  actually knew about each other.
- Asking for time produced frustration, hurt or pressure.
- I have made commitments that exceed my real knowledge of them.

**Secrecy**
- They have concealed information that materially affects my safety or consent.
- Their identity, relationship status or significant life stories have proved
  false or inconsistent.
- They require me to hide the relationship from safe people without a mutually
  agreed and legitimate reason.

**Repeated boundary violations**
- I have set a clear limit and had it pushed, apologised for, and pushed again.
- They repeatedly treat my no as temporary, negotiable, or something I must
  defend until they are satisfied.
- My limits are called legalism, coldness or lack of love.

**Lack of accountability**
- They discourage or resent my seeking counsel about the relationship.
- They have cut off everyone who ever raised a concern about them.
- The relationship is being moved, step by step, away from the people who could
  see it.

**Refusal to accept correction**
- They are never wrong about the specific thing I raised.
- Their apologies are not followed by change.
- I am the one who ends up apologising, or I have stopped raising things.

> No private claim binds your conscience. A *Yes* identifies an additional warning
> about the claim's content, function or use. Several are a reason to talk to
> someone outside the relationship.

### Part C — Taking the spiritual language off

For any statement they made that used God, Scripture, the devil or a leader to
make a point about your relationship:

- What they said:
- The same sentence with the spiritual reference removed — what is actually being
  asked of me:
- Would I accept that request if it were made in plain words?

### Part D — What is happening to me

Answer *Yes / Partly / No*.

- I can raise a concern without fear of intimidation, retaliation, punishment,
  abandonment threats or spiritual pressure.
- I am as connected to my friends, family and church as I was before the
  relationship.
- I feel more like myself around them, not less.
- I can say no and have it stay said.
- I have not been hiding things about the relationship from people I trust.

### Part E — Reading the checklist

> Do not raise an incident merely to answer this section. If you have not raised
> it because you fear their response, record that fact without confronting them
> and seek individual support.

- Are the things I marked *Observed* incidents or patterns? How serious?
- If I raised them safely, what happened? If I did not, was fear of their
  response part of the reason?
- Which patterns, if any, have been wearing a spiritual disguise?

*Before you finish:* "The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go
on and suffer for it" (Proverbs 22:3). Prudence is not fear, and it is not
unforgiveness. Ask God for the clarity to see what is in front of you, and the
courage to say it out loud to one person who loves you.

## Next faithful step

Choose the one that fits, then write the name of one person you will talk to and
the date you will do it.

- **No significant concern** — continue observing without becoming suspicious.
- **Unclear or lower-level concern** — slow down (Lesson 18) and seek an
  independent perspective.
- **Repeated controlling behaviour, or one serious incident** — seek individual
  professional or specialist advice before confronting the person.
- **Immediate danger, violence, sexual coercion, stalking or credible threats** —
  prioritise safety and emergency or specialist support. This step replaces the
  others; do not wait to finish the course.

## Journal reflection

Answering this does not make you responsible for anyone else's coercion or abuse.
Examine your own conduct honestly, without using your imperfections to minimise
what has been done to you.

1. Which of the seven patterns have you ever used, in this relationship or
   another?
2. What would repentance — not merely recognition — look like, specifically?
   People can admit behaviour without changing it. Repentance includes accepting
   consequences, respecting the other person's distance, seeking specialist help
   where needed, ending coercive conduct, and sustained change without demanding
   reconciliation.

## Need support?

If you have recognised several of these, or one serious thing, and felt while
reading the particular heaviness of seeing something you had been explaining away
— you are not foolish, and you are not alone. Many wise and faithful people have
been exactly here. What matters now is not how you got here but what you do next.

Begin with a specialist abuse service for individual safety guidance, and seek
medical, legal or other professional help where the situation requires it. A
trusted pastor may support you spiritually alongside that help, provided they
understand their limits and do not press you towards confrontation,
reconciliation or joint counselling. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

You do not need to make a decision today.

If you recognised nothing here, be glad, and continue with ordinary wisdom rather
than suspicion. If you recognised a lower-level concern and none of Part A
applies, you may slow down and seek an independent perspective; Lesson 18 can
help structure that process. If you recognised repeated controlling behaviour,
anything in Part A or one serious incident, do not wait for a pattern or continue
observing in order to become certain. Use **Finding Help Where You Live** and seek
appropriate individual support. If immediate safety is involved, act on that
before continuing the course.
