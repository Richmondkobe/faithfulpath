# Lesson 7 — The Quiet Green Flags That Matter

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name what health between two
people actually looks like, distinguish it from encouraging signs that are not
evidence of character on their own, and decide whether exclusivity is a step you
are ready to take.

**Key Scripture**
"That your love may abound more and more, with knowledge and all discernment, so
that you may approve what is excellent." — Philippians 1:9–10 (ESV)

Paul is praying for a whole church rather than for anyone's relationship. But
notice the order he assumes. Love is not asked to do without knowledge, and
discernment is not treated as the opposite of love — they grow together, and what
they produce is the ability to recognise what is genuinely good.

## Core teaching

The last lesson was about danger. This one is about goodness, and it is the
harder of the two to write well, because goodness in a person is usually quiet.

Red flags can be subtle, particularly beneath romance or spiritual language.
Green flags are easy to miss for a different reason: genuine goodness is
undramatic. It shows in what happens when nothing in particular is happening — a
promise kept without comment, a truth told at some small cost, a no accepted
without a sulk, a friend of fifteen years who still calls. Nobody makes a film
about these. If you have been trained by culture, by church or by past
relationships to look for something more visible, you will look straight past
them.

**Start with what is not enough.** None of the following is bad. Each is positive
information. The point is only that none of them, alone, establishes character.

*Politeness* tells you someone knows how to behave. It does not yet tell you how
they behave when courtesy has no audience. *Church attendance* may reflect sincere
commitment but cannot prove it. *Romantic gestures* can be sincere and beautiful,
but the gesture tells you less than the pattern around it: does generosity come
with expectations, is the pace comfortable, does affection stay respectful when
you say no? Gifts become concerning when they create obligation, accelerate
intimacy, or alternate with withdrawal. A gesture is a moment; consistency is the
evidence. *Spiritual language* may reflect real faith, but fluency does not
establish transformation. *A good reputation* matters, but popularity is not
integrity — some people are widely liked because they are good, some because they
have learned that being liked is useful. And *agreement* can indicate shared
values, but constant agreement may conceal fear, people-pleasing or mirroring.
Early on, some people discover what you value and reflect it back, and it feels
like extraordinary compatibility. Real compatibility includes disagreement.
Someone who never voices a view of their own in your presence has not yet shown
you who they are.

**Ten green flags.** Each is relational — something that happens between you, not
merely a quality in them — and each is observable rather than claimed.

**They respect your no.** You decline something, change a plan, set a limit, and
the answer is respected. They may ask for clarification or revisit a subject
respectfully at an appropriate time, but they do not treat your no as an obstacle
to wear down, punish you, or make affection conditional on agreement. On physical
intimacy, be clear about this before Lesson 9: consent concerns each occasion and
can always be withdrawn, and a previous yes never removes the right to say no
later. Someone who can hear your no is showing you what it will be like to say no
to them for years — and there is no marriage in which that will not sometimes be
necessary.

**They make room for honest disagreement.** You can disagree about something that
matters and basic respect remains. The temperature may change for a while;
healthy people feel hurt and frustration. But if either of you needs time, that
need is communicated and the conversation is returned to, rather than silence
being used as punishment. Do not treat every quiet moment as a warning — look at
function and pattern. Paul's phrase is "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians
4:15), and it assumes both: truth that may be uncomfortable, and love that does
not stop while the truth is being spoken.

**They take responsibility and repair harm.** A healthy apology names the conduct,
acknowledges its effect, does not blame you for raising it, and is followed by
real effort to change. Sometimes people notice their failure themselves;
sometimes it must be brought to their attention. The flag is willingness to
listen and take responsibility once they understand. Change may take practice,
support and accountability, but there should be evidence of serious effort rather
than repeated promises without movement. "Whoever conceals his transgressions
will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy"
(Proverbs 28:13). *Confesses and forsakes.* The second word is the one to watch
for.

**Their words and behaviour increasingly align.** They say they value honesty and
are honest when it costs them. They say they will call, and they call. Lesson 5
called this integrity and named it the master window. Here it is the master green
flag, because it makes everything else they say believable. "Be doers of the
word, and not hearers only" (James 1:22).

**They respect your separate relationships and identity.** They do not need you to
give up your friends, family, church, work or interests, and they are glad when
you are with people they are not. They treat those who matter to you with
appropriate respect while keeping healthy boundaries — they are not required to
tolerate mistreatment from your family, but neither do they ask you to abandon
those relationships. And you remain yourself around them: your opinions, your
humour, your convictions intact.

**They show emotional steadiness without demanding perfection.** Something goes
wrong and they can express disappointment without it becoming a licence to punish
or blame. They are allowed to feel it and to be comforted; the flag is that
disappointment does not become permission to punish, control or blame, and that
they do not make you solely responsible for removing what they feel. And it runs
both ways — they can bear an ordinary bad day of yours without treating it as a
crisis or a betrayal.

**They tell relevant truths even when inconvenient.** They admit a mistake before
you find it. They say *I don't know* rather than inventing an answer. They correct
an inaccurate good impression. They tell you what affects your safety, your
consent or your shared future, and they do not use privacy as permission for
material deception (Ephesians 4:25).

**They are teachable and able to reconsider.** They can hold another view,
acknowledge uncertainty, revise a conclusion. Not always instantly — some people
need time — but they do not treat changing their mind as humiliation. A partner
who can be wrong is a partner with whom problems can be solved. A partner who
cannot is one with whom every problem becomes a contest.

**They celebrate healthy growth without controlling its direction.** They are glad
when you flourish, including where they have no part. They pray for you, not only
about the relationship. They do not need you smaller to feel larger. This does
not mean approving every ambition — a healthy partner may raise concerns about
overwork or neglected responsibilities — but they do so without belittling your
gifts or requiring you to stay dependent. Love "does not envy or boast"
(1 Corinthians 13:4), and the test is simple: what happens when something good
happens to you that has nothing to do with them?

**The relationship allows mutuality.** Both can speak, choose, give and receive.
Neither is always the one who adapts, apologises, waits or gives way. And each of
you is known, over time, by people who can be honest with you. Sustained
friendships are valuable evidence, because they show someone can maintain
connection through change and difficulty. Their absence is not automatically
concerning — migration, ministry, disability, discrimination, betrayal,
caregiving and social anxiety can all thin a person's friendships. Quality,
reciprocity and openness matter more than the age of a friendship.

You will not find someone who shows all ten all the time. Nobody does. The most
meaningful green flag is not flawless conduct but a growing capacity to recognise
harm, receive honest feedback, repair and change.

This applies to ordinary human failures. Violence, sexual coercion, stalking,
credible threats or coercive control must not be reframed as opportunities to
observe apology and repair. Safety comes first, and reconciliation is never owed.

**The decision: exclusivity.** At some point in a relationship going well, a
question arises. Exclusivity means both people freely agree to stop pursuing
other romantic possibilities for a defined season while they explore this
relationship more intentionally. It is not engagement, ownership, or permission
to accelerate physical or emotional intimacy. It does not remove either person's
freedom to slow down or end things. It creates a clearer context for discernment;
it does not guarantee an engagement.

In some cultures and church traditions this question barely exists, because
dating is understood to be exclusive and intentional from the start, often with
families involved early. If that is your setting, the questions in the tool still
apply — you will simply be asking them before a first relationship is agreed
rather than months into one. Either way, it is a real decision, and it deserves
to be made rather than drifted into.

Because exclusivity narrows your options and deepens your attachment, it belongs
after a certain amount has been seen. The tool checks that. One item is worth
naming here because it causes more pain than any other: **two people who assume
they mean the same thing by "exclusive" and do not are heading for trouble.** The
word covers pace, physical boundaries, how others are involved, and what you are
moving towards. Say it in plain words.

A word about online and long-distance relationships. Online contact can create
deep emotional attachment before ordinary life has been observed. Video calls and
long conversations reveal something real, but they do not replace verified
identity, in-person conduct and observation across different settings. Do not
make financial commitments, travel into a situation of dependency, or become
engaged before basic identity and circumstances have been independently
confirmed. If most of what you know has come through a screen, the honest answer
to several of the tool's questions is *not yet* — and that is a reason for
patience, not shame.

## Practical tool — Questions Before Becoming Exclusive

For the point at which a relationship is going well and exclusivity has arisen,
or is about to. Parts A to C are for you alone. Part D is for the two of you.
Part E is your decision. Nothing here is scored.

> **If you are not currently dating**, complete Part B about yourself and read the
> rest. Do not fill in Part A about an imagined person. Keep the tool for when
> there is someone real to consider.

### Part A — Green flags I have actually seen

For each, answer *Observed / Not yet observed / Mixed evidence / Concern
observed*, with evidence and a date. *Seems kind* is not evidence. *Apologised on
the 14th for snapping, named what he did, didn't blame me for raising it, and has
been noticeably different since* is.

1. They respect my no.
2. They make room for honest disagreement.
3. They take responsibility and repair harm. When a failure was raised or
   recognised, they listened, took appropriate responsibility, made a meaningful
   effort to repair it and showed change over time.
4. Their words and behaviour increasingly align.
5. They respect my separate relationships and identity.
6. They show emotional steadiness without demanding perfection.
7. They tell relevant truths even when inconvenient.
8. They are teachable and able to reconsider.
9. They celebrate healthy growth without controlling its direction.
10. The relationship allows mutuality — we can both speak, choose, give and
    receive.

### Part B — The green flags I offer

- Can I receive their no without pressure or punishment?
- Can I disagree without withdrawing love?
- When I cause harm, can I listen, apologise and repair?
- Do my words and behaviour align?
- Do I encourage their healthy relationships and growth?
- Can they remain themselves around me?
- Am I telling the truth required for their informed decision?

### Part C — Checking the earlier tools

- *Lesson 2, Spiritual Compatibility* — Is there a shared foundation? Anything
  still a foundational unknown?
- *Lesson 3, Requirements* — Has any foundational requirement or personal
  non-negotiable been bent?
- *Lesson 5, Observation Sheet* — Which windows have opened? Which are still
  closed, and why?
- *Lesson 6, Red Flag Checklist* — Any immediate safety concern, controlling
  pattern, or uncertainty needing specialist advice?
- Where reasonably possible, have you each begun to encounter the other's
  established relationships? What did your people actually say?

> If any abuse or safety concern has been identified, do not proceed to
> exclusivity. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

### Part D — Questions for the two of us

In person where possible, unhurried, with both of you free to say *I'm not sure*.
Notice not only the answers but whether you were each free to give them.

> Complete this section together only if both of you can speak freely and safely.
> If you are afraid to disagree, say *not yet*, seek counsel or end the
> conversation, do not use this discussion to test the relationship. Seek
> individual guidance first. Do not complete it jointly where coercion, abuse or
> retaliation is present.

**What exclusivity means**
- What does "exclusive" mean to each of us, in plain words?
- What changes about dating apps, private messages and conversations with other
  potential partners?
- Will we describe the relationship publicly? If not, why?
- What are we moving towards, and what would tell us this is not going to become
  a marriage?
- What will we do if one of us later wants to end exclusivity?

**Pace**
- What pace feels wise for the next months? Is either of us feeling hurried?
- What would we want to have seen, and how much time to have had, before
  engagement was discussed?

**Boundaries**
- What physical boundaries are we each committed to? (Lesson 9 makes these
  specific; the point here is that each states them and hears the other's.)
- What digital access do we expect, and what privacy will each person retain?
- What other boundaries matter — time, financial, emotional?

**Others**
- Who will know we are exclusive, and when? Is there anyone either of us wants to
  keep this from, and why?
- Whose counsel will each of us seek, and are we both free to seek it?

**Handling difficulty**
- When we disagree, how do we each tend to respond, and what do we need from the
  other then?
- If one of us has a concern about the relationship, how do we want it raised?
- Is there information about my current commitments, relationship status, family
  responsibilities, financial obligations, health needs, convictions or future
  plans that materially affects your decision to become exclusive?

> On that last question: exclusivity does not entitle either person to every
> private detail, complete financial records, passwords, medical files or a full
> trauma history. Relevant truth and appropriate privacy can coexist. Lesson 12
> deals with what needs to be known, and when.

### Part E — My decision

Answer each *Yes / Partly / No*.

- I have seen enough, over enough time, to know this person beyond first
  impressions.
- I have seen green flags with evidence, not just felt good about them.
- I have not identified a safety concern, and I feel free to seek independent
  advice if one arises.
- My foundational requirements and non-negotiables are intact.
- We mean the same thing by exclusivity, and we have said so.
- I can say no in this relationship, and I am still myself in it.
- Where possible, people who love me have met them, and I have listened to what
  those people said.
- I am offering the green flags I am looking for.

Then choose:

- **Ready** — Based on what I presently know, I am freely choosing exclusivity
  while continuing to discern.
- **Not yet** — I am not freely ready to choose exclusivity. What is needed next:
  more time, an important conversation, independent counsel, or clarification of
  a concern.
- **No** — I am not choosing exclusivity. If it is safe, I will communicate that
  honestly and respectfully. If I fear their reaction, I will seek individual
  safety guidance before telling them or arranging an in-person conversation.

If you chose *Ready*, write today's date. You will return to this tool at Lesson
18's 90-Day Relationship Review.

*Before you finish:* pray Paul's prayer from Philippians 1:9–10 for yourself and
for the person you are considering — that what is excellent in each of you would
be seen clearly, and that neither love nor discernment would be asked to do
without the other.

## Next faithful step

If exclusivity is a live question, arrange the Part D conversation: in person
where possible, unhurried, not at the end of a long evening, and not on a day
when either of you is already braced for something. Write the date you will have
it.

If exclusivity is not yet in view, do something smaller and more useful: write
down one green flag you have actually seen this week, with the date and what
happened. The evidence you want at a decision point is the evidence you gathered
before there was a decision to make.

If you are not dating, choose one green flag from Part B that you want to offer
more consistently. Write one ordinary way you will practise it in your existing
relationships this week.

## Journal reflection

1. Have you been counting politeness, church attendance, romantic gestures,
   spiritual vocabulary, popularity or agreement as evidence of character? Which
   ones?
2. Setting those aside, what evidence of character and relational health have you
   actually observed?

## Need support?

Exclusivity can deepen attachment and social expectations, which may make
stepping back feel harder. It never removes your freedom to slow down or end the
relationship. If anything in Lesson 6 was left unresolved, this is the wrong
moment to proceed on hope. Where a safety concern exists, do not raise exclusivity
as a way of testing it — get individual guidance from a specialist service first.

If most of your relationship has happened online, be careful in a different
direction. Requests for money, pressure to travel somewhere you would be
dependent, reluctance to meet or to be verified, and urgency about commitment are
the pattern that financial and romance fraud follows. Confirm identity and
circumstances independently before any financial or travel commitment.

## Continue

If you are early and the green flags are just appearing, enjoy them — and write
them down with dates.

If the honest answer is *not yet*, that is not automatically a verdict on the
person. Identify what the answer requires: time, conversation, counsel or a
decision not to proceed. Do not let *not yet* become indefinite waiting where a
serious concern is already clear.

If you are already exclusive and have realised you agreed to something you never
discussed, have the discussion now. It will be harder in six months than it is
today.

If you are not dating yet, this is where your route pauses. You have done the work
that is most useful before there is a particular person attached to it. Keep what
you have written — Lessons 1, 2, 3 and 7 all produce material you will want later
— and come back to Module 3 when dating becomes relevant.
