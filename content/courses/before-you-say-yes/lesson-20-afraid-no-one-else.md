# Lesson 20 — When You Are Afraid You Will Never Find Anyone Else

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name what you are actually afraid
of, recognise how that fear may be influencing your decisions, and weigh the
relationship's evidence without allowing fear to decide in your place.

**Key Scripture**
"How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" — Psalm 13:1 (ESV)

David says to God what a polite Christian would not. Note that the psalm does not
record the circumstances changing before its final verses; what changes is the
psalmist's grip on what he knows. Honest prayer is not a failure of faith. It is in
the Bible because God can bear it.

## Core teaching

Every lesson in this course has, at some point, pointed here. Lesson 17 said: do not
continue because you are afraid there will be no one else. Lesson 19 said it again.
Lesson 16 sent the person who was told no to this lesson, and Lesson 19's worksheet
sent anyone whose reason for staying was fear. I have kept saying that this fear is
not a reason. I have not yet said what it is.

Start by taking it seriously, because it is usually not taken seriously. It gets
treated as a weakness to be prayed away, a lack of faith, a symptom of not being
content in Christ. Single Christians are told to wait, to trust, to focus on God —
and then quietly judged when the waiting turns into fear. But the fear of never
finding anyone is one of the most ordinary fears a person can have, and it is not a
spiritual failure. **It is what it feels like to want something good, to have no
control over whether it comes, and to watch time pass.**

So let me say it plainly: you may be afraid that you will never find anyone else.
That fear is real, it is common, and God is not disappointed in you for having it.
What matters is not whether you feel it, but what you let it do.

**What the fear does.** Fear is an unreliable adviser. Sometimes it hurries you;
sometimes it paralyses you. Either way, it can make avoiding loss more important
than seeing clearly.

*It lowers the bar.* A red flag that would have ended the relationship two years ago
becomes something you can live with. A directional incompatibility becomes *we'll
work it out*. The list you made in Lesson 3 gets edited, one non-negotiable at a
time — usually by degrees, until the list describes whoever is in front of you.

*It spiritualises staying.* *God has been so good to bring me this person*, said of a
relationship that on any honest reading is not good. Gratitude that you have someone
becomes an argument that you must keep them. Fear is very good at speaking in a
religious voice, and Lesson 13 gave you the tests for exactly that.

*It hurries.* If you are afraid the chance will not come again, you will take it
faster than you should. Lesson 18's *not yet* feels, to a frightened person, like a
risk they cannot afford — when the risk they cannot afford is marrying in a hurry.

*It makes you the one adapting.* If you believe this is your last chance, you will
surrender convictions, relationships and pieces of yourself to keep it, and you will
call that love.

*It makes you unable to hear a no.* For the reader who was told no, fear turns grief
into a campaign. *They will come round. God will change their heart. If I just
explain properly.*

*It makes you invisible to yourself.* Perhaps the most serious. A person afraid of
being alone stops asking what they want, what they are called to, who they are
becoming, and asks only whether they are wanted. Lesson 1's question gets answered by
the fear before you can answer it yourself.

You do not have to make the fear disappear before you can decide well. But you do
need to recognise it, separate it from the evidence, and refuse to let it cast the
deciding vote.

**What the fear is made of.** *Never finding anyone* is usually several fears wearing
one coat. For some it is loneliness — an empty house for the rest of your life. For
some it is being unwanted, which is not the same thing; you can be surrounded by
people and still believe nobody would choose you. For some it is missing the life you
hoped for: marriage, children, a shape of family you have expected since childhood.
For some it is time — an age, a number, a closing window. For some it is what other
people think: the family that asks at every gathering, the church where the singles'
group empties year by year.

And for many it is the fear of what singleness would say about God. If he is good,
and I have asked for this, and it has not come — what does that mean? That is the
fear underneath the others.

These have different answers, which is why the tool asks you to work out which is
yours. Loneliness is eased, though not entirely, by real connection and belonging —
enough to keep a romantic partnership from carrying the impossible burden of meeting
every relational need. Being unwanted is answered by truth about your worth. Time is
answered partly by honesty about time. And the fear about God is answered by
theology, which is not a cold word for it, because what you believe about God is the
ground you are standing on while everything else is uncertain.

**What Scripture does and does not promise.** Here is what Christian singles are
often not told, or are told so gently that they do not hear it: **God has not
promised you a spouse.**

There is no verse. "Delight yourself in the LORD, and he will give you the desires of
your heart" (Psalm 37:4) is a psalm about the righteous being vindicated in a world
where the wicked prosper; it is not a guarantee about marriage, and it has been used
to break the hearts of people who took it as one. "I know the plans I have for you"
(Jeremiah 29:11) is spoken to exiles about the return of a nation. God keeps his
promises. It matters what he actually promised.

What Scripture says about singleness is more surprising than most churches let on.
Paul, in a chapter largely about marriage, wishes all were as he is — unmarried — and
says the unmarried person is free to be attentive to the things of the Lord in a way
the married person cannot be (1 Corinthians 7:7–8, 32–35). He is not disparaging
marriage; he defends it in the same chapter. But he refuses to treat singleness as a
lesser state or a waiting room. Jesus himself was unmarried, so marital status cannot
be the measure of a complete or faithful human life.

That does not mean you have to want singleness or pretend to. You are allowed to want
marriage. You are allowed to grieve if it does not come. What you are not required to
believe — for your own sake, not as a rule — is that God's goodness to you depends on
whether it comes. He is not withholding a promise. He never made it.

What he has promised is that he will not abandon his people (Hebrews 13:5), that
nothing can separate them from his love in Christ (Romans 8:38–39), and that Christ
remains with them (Matthew 28:20). Those do not make an unmet longing painless. They
mean an unmet longing is not proof that God has withdrawn his love.

**Honesty about time.** I do not want to be the pastor who says *there's someone for
everyone* and moves on, because it is not true and you know it is not. Some people
who want to marry do not. Age changes the picture for many people, and if biological
children are part of what you hope for, age and reproductive health may place real
limits on some options. That should neither be denied nor used to frighten you into
marriage; if it is a significant concern, seek individual medical advice, so your
decisions rest on your circumstances rather than general assumptions.

So here is the honest version. You may marry — many people who feared they never
would did, sometimes late, sometimes after they had stopped expecting it. And you may
not, and if you do not, your life will still be a life, and it can be a good one.
Both are true, and holding both is the task.

What honesty about time does not mean is that the odds justify lowering the bar. **The
possibility that you may not marry is not a reason to enter a marriage that the
evidence does not support.** Marriage does not turn incompatibility, coercion or
serious character concerns into smaller problems. That is fear's arithmetic: some
marriage is better than none. It is wrong. A frightened yes to the wrong person does
not solve the fear. It gives it a house to live in.

**For the reader who was told no.** The fear is now sitting where the relationship
was, and it is telling you two things: that you will not find this again, and that if
you had done something differently you would still have it. Both may feel true.
Neither is a reason to act. Do not pursue them, pressure them, or preserve contact
for the hidden purpose of reversing their decision. Do not tell yourself God will
change their mind, because that is a way of making God responsible for your refusal
to grieve.

And when the fear says *no one else*, answer it with what you know rather than what
you feel. One person's decision does not determine your worth or predict your future.
You may have things to learn from the relationship, but learning is not the same as
accepting a verdict that you are unlovable. You do not know what comes next, and
neither does the fear. It is guessing in the dark.

**What to do with it.** *Name it out loud to someone* — a fear never said grows in the
dark, and most people who hear it will not be shocked. Many will say *me too*. *Bring
it to God without editing it*; he does not require you to perform confidence before
him. *Do not let it make the decision* — if you are afraid to end a relationship
because of what comes after, the fear is not an argument for the relationship. *Build
the life the fear says is impossible* — friendships, a community where you are known
rather than merely present, work that matters, a home that is yours and not a holding
pattern, a table people come to. That is not a consolation prize while you wait for
the real thing. *Grieve the life you expected*, if that is what this actually is.
Grief may return at particular ages, weddings or anniversaries; recurrence does not
mean you have failed to grieve faithfully.

Before the tool, a prayer. Pray it if it fits; leave it if it does not.

> *Lord, I am afraid there will be no one, and I have been letting that fear choose
> for me. Take the panic out of my hands. Help me to want a good life more than I
> fear an empty one, and to tell the truth about what I can see even when the truth
> costs me something I have been holding tightly. I do not know what is coming. You
> have not promised me this. You have promised not to leave, and today I am asking
> you to keep that one. Amen.*

## Practical tool — Relationship Reality Check

For anyone who suspects that fear of never finding someone is influencing their
decisions — about a current relationship, a recent ending, or dating in general. It
does not measure how afraid you are and it diagnoses nothing. It helps you see what
the fear is doing, so that you, not the fear, decide.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

### Part A — Naming the fear

Tick all that apply, then underline the strongest.

- Being lonely — an empty house, no one to come home to.
- Being unwanted — that no one would choose me.
- Grieving the life I expected or hoped for.
- Losing the possibility of biological parenthood.
- Time — an age, a number, a window closing.
- Growing older without practical companionship or care.
- Feeling left behind while friends marry and form families.
- What others think — family, church, culture.
- Believing my church has no meaningful place for unmarried adults.
- What singleness would say about God's goodness to me.
- Something else:

### Part B — What the fear is doing

Answer *Yes / Partly / No*, then the prompt.

**In my current or most recent relationship**
- I have accepted things I would not have accepted earlier. *What, specifically?*
- I have told myself God brought us together as a reason to stay.
- I have hurried, or been willing to hurry, past questions I know should be asked.
- I have been the one adapting, and fear is part of why.
- I have found it hard to accept a no, or to give one.

**In dating generally**
- I have edited my Lesson 3 list to fit whoever is available.
- I have stayed in contact with someone who ended it, hoping they will change their
  mind.
- I have said yes to relationships I did not want in order not to be alone.
- I have stopped asking what I want and asked only whether I am wanted.

### Part C — Fear versus evidence

For each, fill in all three lines. *I do not know* is an honest answer, and often the
true one.

**The fear says: no one else will come.** — What I know · What I do not know · What
remains true even if the feared outcome happens

**The fear says: this is my last chance.** — the same three

**The fear says: if I end this I will regret it forever.** — the same three

**The fear says: some marriage is better than none.** — What I know from this course
· What remains true even if I never marry

**The fear says: God is withholding this from me.** — What Scripture actually
promises · What remains true even if the longing is not met

> An example, to show the shape. *The fear says no one else will come. What I know: I
> cannot know that. What I do not know: whether or when I will marry. What remains
> true: uncertainty about my future does not make this relationship wise.*

### Part D — The decision, with fear separated from evidence

If you are deciding about a current relationship, imagine you were advising someone
you love who shared your convictions and circumstances, but whose judgement was not
controlled by the belief that this was their last chance.

First: *if I believed that being single would be painful but survivable — and would
not diminish my worth — what would the evidence in this relationship lead me to do?*

Then:
- Based on the evidence, which response is wisest: continue, slow down or end?
- What evidence supports that response?
- What part of me resists it, and why?
- Which lesson should I now revisit — 17, 18 or 19?
- Who will help me act on what I have concluded?

### Part E — What I believe

Mark each *I believe this / I understand it but struggle to feel it / I am not yet
convinced*.

- God has not promised me a spouse, and his goodness to me does not depend on one.
- A single life can be a whole life before God, not a waiting room.
- I am allowed to want marriage and to grieve if it does not come.
- An unsafe or deeply unsuitable marriage is not a safer choice merely because the
  alternative may be singleness.
- One person's no is not the world's verdict on me.
- My worth is not decided by whether I am chosen.

> Take anything in the second or third column to someone who will not simply reassure
> you, but will help you think it through.

### Part F — The life I am building now

- The friendships I am investing in:
- The community where I am known, not just present:
- The work or service that matters to me:
- The home I am making, as mine and not as a holding pattern:
- The hospitality I am offering:
- The expectation I may need to grieve, and with whom:

*Before you finish:* pray Psalm 13:1 without editing it — *how long, O LORD?* Then
keep reading. The psalm does not tell us the circumstances changed before its final
verses; it shows a prayer moving from anguish towards remembered trust. Ask God not
for artificial certainty, but for the grace to live faithfully while the future is
unknown — and for the fear to be his to carry, yours to live beside, and no longer
the one making your decisions.

## Next faithful step

If you have a safe, trustworthy person available, say: *I am afraid I will never find
anyone, and I think it may be affecting my decisions.* Choose someone who can listen
without dismissing the fear with quick promises.

If no such person is available, write the sentence privately or take it to a suitably
qualified counsellor or wise pastoral adviser. You are naming the fear, not asking
someone to predict your future.

## Journal reflection

1. When did this fear begin, or become strong? Is there a specific age, event or
   comparison that feeds it?
2. What would you begin building now if you stopped treating your present life as a
   waiting room?

## Need support?

Sometimes this fear is not really about dating. It may be part of a wider anxiety, an
old wound, or a way of thinking about yourself that has been there for years.

If it is constant, if it is affecting your sleep or your work or your other
relationships, if it makes you feel worthless without a partner, or if this lesson
has not touched it — talk to a suitably qualified counsellor. That is not a sign
something is wrong with you. It is what wise people do with a burden too heavy to
carry alone.

If the fear has become hopelessness — if you are thinking about harming yourself or
not wanting to live — seek crisis support now. If you believe you may act on those
thoughts, contact emergency or urgent mental-health services immediately. If it is
safe and possible, tell someone trustworthy and do not remain alone. Do not wait for
a routine appointment. **Finding Help Where You Live** lists places to begin by
country.

## Continue

You have reached the end of the teaching. You now have language and tools for
examining motives, attraction, character, compatibility, safety and fear. You have
considered what continuing wisely, slowing down and ending a relationship may
require. The course has not made the decision for you or guaranteed that every
distinction will be clear. It has helped you bring what you know — and what you still
do not know — into the light.

None of that depends on marriage coming. It depends on continuing to practise honest
discernment and treating yourself and others with truth, freedom and care.
