# Lesson 18 — When to Slow Down

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to give the answer "not yet"
deliberately — with a stated reason, an agreed shape, and a review point — rather
than drifting, waiting for the question to answer itself, or placing the other
person under scrutiny.

**Key Scripture**
"Desire without knowledge is not good, and whoever makes haste with his feet misses
his way." — Proverbs 19:2 (ESV)

A proverb about life generally rather than courtship, but the principle transfers
cleanly. Wanting something is not the same as knowing it, and speed is not a virtue
when the road is unclear.

## Core teaching

The second honest answer to *should we continue?* is *not yet*.

It is the least discussed of the three options and the most misused. Some readers
treat it as a way of avoiding the other two — neither committing nor ending, holding
a relationship in suspension until the decision makes itself. Others treat it as a
punishment, a probation imposed by one person on another. Neither is what this
lesson means. **A wise "not yet" is as deliberate as a yes or a no.** It says: there
is something I do not know that I need to know before I can say more, and I am going
to find it out rather than guessing or waiting for the question to go away.

**What slowing down is.** Reducing the pace at which commitment deepens, for a
defined period and a stated reason, so that something specific can become clearer.
It is not usually about seeing each other less, though sometimes that helps. It is
about not taking the next step — exclusivity, meeting families as a prospective
in-law, moving towards engagement, merging finances or plans — until a particular
concern has had time to show itself for what it is.

Three things make it a decision rather than a drift.

*It has a reason.* Something named: a difficulty that has not yet produced evidence,
a pace that has outrun discernment, a concern from counsel, an external strain
distorting everything. *I just need more time* is not a reason. *I need to see
whether the pattern with money changes now that we have both named it* is.

*It has a shape.* Both people know what is paused and what is not. Are you still
exclusive? Still seeing each other's families? Still talking about the future? These
need not be answered the same way by every couple, but they have to be answered —
otherwise the slowdown becomes a vagueness in which one person thinks they are still
moving forward and the other thinks they have stopped.

*It has an end.* Not necessarily a date on which everything resolves, but a point at
which you will look at what has been learned and decide again. Without that, *not
yet* quietly becomes *never*, and the person waiting is being kept rather than dealt
with truthfully.

**When "not yet" is the right answer.**

*The evidence is missing.* A difficulty is named and mutually accepted, but too new
to have shown change. The absence of evidence in a newly identified problem is not
evidence of unwillingness — and it is also not evidence of change.

*The pace has outrun the knowledge.* Some relationships move very fast, and the two
people know each other's hopes far better than each other's habits. If you have
observed little but are already discussing a date, slowing down is not a lack of
faith. It is catching up.

*External strain is distorting the picture.* Grief, illness, a job crisis, a visa
decision. Name the strain before treating its effects as a verdict — but keep both
possibilities open. Pressure does not explain everything away. It may temporarily
distort someone's behaviour, and it may also reveal how they respond to pressure,
which is worth knowing. Set a review point and ask whether you have gained enough
clarity, not merely whether the circumstances have ended, because illness, grief and
family responsibilities can last years.

*Counsel is concerned and you are not sure they are wrong.* If several independent
people have raised the same concrete concern and you cannot confidently say they are
mistaken, look at what they are seeing before committing further.

*Readiness is unequal.* One of you is ready to move towards engagement and the other
is not, and the reluctance is a real difference in where each person is rather than
a verdict on the relationship. Slowing down protects both: the one who is not ready
is not pressured, and the one who is ready is not left guessing.

*You are not sure what you feel.* If you cannot tell whether what you feel is love,
comfort, fear of being alone or habit, ordinary time is the only way to find out.
But be truthful about the horizon. **A person cannot wait indefinitely for you to
discover whether you want them.**

**When "not yet" is the wrong answer.**

*When you already know.* Some readers reach this lesson having recognised their
relationship in Lesson 16 and are looking for a way to postpone acting on it.
Slowing down will not make a directional incompatibility resolve; it will only make
the ending later and more costly.

*When it is imposed as a probation.* *We are slowing down until you prove yourself*
is not a decision made together; it is a sentence imposed by one person. One-sided
probation tends to produce short-term compliance rather than trustworthy change. The
question is never whether someone behaves well while being watched.

*When it is a way of keeping someone.* Some people slow a relationship down because
they are not sure they want the other person but are sure they do not want to lose
them. That is not fair. They are being held in reserve while you decide, and they
cannot consent to that because they do not know it is happening. If you are unsure
whether you want them, say so, and accept that they are free to decide what to do
with that.

*When the relationship keeps cycling through pauses and resumptions.* Repeated pauses
are cumulative evidence. Ask what each one actually clarified, whether the question
has narrowed, and whether the same uncertainty keeps returning unchanged. If it
does, the answer is probably not more time.

*When the relationship is unsafe.* **Do not slow down an unsafe relationship.**
Slowing down assumes the problem is a question to be answered and that both people
can speak and act freely while it is answered. Where there is fear, coercion,
control, intimidation or violence, there is no question to answer and no safe way to
wait for one.

**Slowing down well.**

*Say it, and say why.* A pause one person has decided privately is not a pause; it
is withdrawal, and the other person will feel it as rejection without being told
what it is. If you cannot explain the reason truthfully, examine whether it is a
reason or an excuse. If saying it directly feels unsafe, that is a different matter
entirely — do not arrange a private confrontation; seek support and make safety the
priority.

*Agree the shape together, or recognise that you cannot.* You do not need permission
to decline the next level of commitment; that is yours. But if the relationship is
to continue during the slowdown, both of you must freely agree what that continuation
means. If you cannot agree on a shape, the honest result may be an ending rather than
an undefined pause — and that is better than a vagueness in which each of you is
living in a different relationship.

*Name what you are looking for.* Not *whether things get better*, but the specific
thing — and make it observable. You cannot see a motive, and a slowdown that waits
for the other person to demonstrate what is in their heart will never end.

*Keep living ordinary life.* The purpose is to see the relationship as it actually
is, and that requires ordinary conditions: time with each other's friends,
unglamorous errands, disagreements about small things, how each of you behaves when
tired. A slowdown spent only on intense conversations about the relationship will
not show you what you need to see. And let evidence emerge naturally — do not
provoke, monitor or read the other person's messages to speed it up. Evidence
gathered that way tells you more about the gatherer than the gathered.

*Respect the other person's freedom.* A slowdown is your decision; the response is
theirs. They may agree. They may say they cannot wait, or do not want to, and **that
is a legitimate answer even if it is not the one you hoped for.** A person asked to
slow down is not obliged to stay, and if they leave, they have not done something
wrong.

*Do not get engaged.* Engagement is a public promise, and it is not a way to resolve
a private uncertainty. If a partner, a parent or a church is pressing for engagement
during a period you agreed to use for finding out, that pressure is itself something
to look at.

**A word about ninety days.** The review that follows is called a 90-Day Review.
**Ninety days is a practical checkpoint, not an evidence-based test or a universally
appropriate period.** It gives the review a clear horizon, but it may be too long for
a simple question, too short for a longstanding pattern, and entirely inappropriate
where a decision has already been reached or safety is involved.

**Ninety days is a period of discernment, not a promise to stay for ninety days
regardless of what happens.** Either of you may reconsider at any point, and new
information does not have to wait for a date in a diary. Set the period to fit the
concern and say what it is — a recent change in how you handle conflict may be
visible in six weeks; recovery, financial repair or the working-through of a long
personal pattern may need far longer and may need professional judgement about what
long enough means.

When the period ends, do the review — separately first, then together where you can.
And be clear how the deciding works. **Continuing requires two freely given yeses.
Walking away requires only one person's no, and nobody must persuade the other to
remain.**

## Practical tool — 90-Day Relationship Review

Two parts. Part One is completed at the start of a slowdown and sets what the period
is intended to clarify. Part Two is completed at the review point. Both separately
first, then compared together if you can speak freely with each other. Nothing here
is scored.

> **For relationships in which both people can speak freely and neither is afraid.**
> Do not complete or compare this worksheet with a partner you fear. Do not disclose
> private plans, passwords, financial information or sources of support to someone
> who may use them to control you. If fear, coercion, control, intimidation or
> violence is present, do not slow down — Lesson 6 and Lesson 19's safety guidance
> are the right routes.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

### Part One — At the start

**1. The reason**
- The specific concern I need to see more clearly:
- Which it is (tick all that apply): a newly named difficulty without evidence yet ·
  a pace that has outrun knowledge · external strain · a concern from counsel ·
  unequal readiness · my own uncertain feelings
- What evidence do I already have, rather than merely fear or suspect?
- What information is missing?
- Is this something time can clarify, or does it require a decision now?
- What I am *not* slowing down for (check truthfully): to avoid a decision I have
  already made · to impose a probation or punish · to keep the other person while I
  decide

**2. The shape**
- What continues:
- What pauses:
- Are we exclusive during this period?
- How often will we communicate and see each other?
- What will we tell family, friends or church members?
- What privacy will each of us retain?
- What neither of us will do before the review — engagement, moving in, merging
  finances, telling families we are serious:
- What happens if one of us no longer wishes to continue?
- Have we agreed this together, in words?

**3. What the period is intended to clarify**
- I will know the concern is resolving if I see:
- I will know it is not resolving if I see:
- I will know the question was the wrong question if:
- Is what I am looking for observable, or am I expecting the other person to
  demonstrate an inner motive?
- Can this evidence emerge naturally, without provoking, monitoring or invading
  privacy?

**4. The period**
- The review date:
- Why this period fits the concern:
- If the concern involves addiction, substantial financial instability, a
  longstanding destructive pattern, or the practical effects of a health condition
  that materially affect the proposed shared life: what appropriately qualified help
  has the person freely chosen to seek? What information can a professional
  appropriately provide about realistic expectations, without being asked to decide
  the future of the relationship or disclose private medical information?

**5. Support**
- Who knows we are doing this:
- What they may know, and what remains private:
- What help, if any, either of us will seek during the period:
- Have I retained at least one source of counsel independent of the relationship?

**6. Freedom**
- I understand the other person may decide not to wait, and that this is a
  legitimate answer.
- I understand either of us may reconsider before the review date if new
  information, an incompatibility or a safety concern arises. The date is a prompt
  to look honestly, not a commitment to remain until it arrives.

### Part Two — At the review

Complete separately before discussing.

**1. What I saw.** Return to Part One, question 3.
- What I saw that suggested the concern is resolving:
- What I saw that suggested it is not:
- Was the period spent in ordinary conditions, or mainly in conversations about the
  relationship?
- What happened when we disagreed during this period?
- What happened when one of us was tired, ill, disappointed or under pressure?
- Did anything new come up that Part One did not anticipate?

**2. The quality of any change**
- Did words and behaviour increasingly agree?
- Did the change depend on reminders, monitoring or fear of losing the
  relationship?
- Did it continue when the immediate conversation had passed?
- Was responsibility accepted without blame, concealment or resentment?
- Were setbacks admitted or hidden?
- Has the change become visible across more than one situation?
- Did either of us feel continually examined rather than known?

**3. What I now know**
- What I know now that I did not know at the start:
- Which of Lesson 17's four kinds of difficulty this has turned out to be:
- If a personal pattern: whose, and has that person taken up the help it needs?
- If external strain: has enough clarity emerged, whether or not the strain itself
  has lifted?
- Did the slowdown produce clarity, or merely temporary relief from making a
  decision?

**4. The whole relationship.** Mark each *Yes / Not sure / No*.
- Our shared spiritual foundation remains intact.
- No safety concern has arisen.
- Our core life direction remains compatible.
- Attraction and freely chosen desire are present — in both of us.
- I can say no to this person without being punished for it.
- I have remained connected to independent people.
- The relationship has contained enjoyment and friendship during this period, not
  only scrutiny.
- I have taken the original concern back to wise, independent counsel and considered
  their observations without evasion. What do they believe has changed, remained
  unresolved, or become clearer?

**5. Checking myself**
- Has the same question come back unanswered from a previous pause? What did each
  earlier pause clarify?
- Is the question now narrower than it was, or exactly the same size?

**6. Where this leaves me.** Describe it in your own words first.
- What the concern was, and what I now know about it:
- What the whole relationship looks like alongside that:
- What counsel has contributed:

Then choose one:

- **Continue**, using Lesson 17's agreement.
- **Slow down again, with a narrower question.** The new question is ______ and the
  new review date is ______. If this is not the first pause, write what each earlier
  pause clarified and take that to counsel before agreeing another.
- **Walk away.** *(Lesson 19)*
- **Seek specialist support before deciding anything.**
- **I am not ready to choose yet.**

> No combination of answers produces this decision. Continuing requires two freely
> given yeses; walking away requires only one person's no, and neither of you has to
> persuade the other to stay. A slowdown that ends in a clear, kind no has done
> exactly what it was for.

*Before you finish:* whatever you have decided, thank God for the truthfulness of
the last weeks. Ask him for the courage to act on it — to go forward without
pretending the concern never existed, or to end without pretending the relationship
had no good in it. "Let your yes be yes and your no be no" (James 5:12) is about
truthful speech rather than dating, but it is what a slowdown is for: to make it
possible for both of you to say one or the other, and mean it.

## Next faithful step

**If you are beginning a slowdown:** where it is safe, explain the reason and propose
the shape clearly. The other person remains free to accept that arrangement, propose
a different one or decline to continue.

**If you have completed the review:** communicate the path you have freely chosen —
continue, slow again, seek support or end — without treating the worksheet as the
authority for your decision. If the other person chooses not to continue, receive
that answer.

If either conversation would be unsafe, do not arrange it privately to discover how
they will react. Seek individual guidance first.

## Journal reflection

1. If you were to continue, would it be because the concern resolved — or because
   the review date arrived and you do not want another hard conversation?
2. If you were to end it, what would you be responding to — the original concern,
   what the review revealed, the continuing uncertainty, or your freely reached
   decision that you no longer want to continue? None requires you to prove the
   other person wrong.

## Need support?

Repair and patience are available to you where goodwill and freedom exist. Neither
is ever the price you must pay before being permitted to leave. You do not owe this
relationship a waiting period, and no review date obliges you to stay until it
arrives.

If what you are facing is fear rather than uncertainty, a slowdown is the wrong tool
and it can be a dangerous one: it keeps you in place while you observe someone you
are afraid of. Begin with a specialist abuse service for individual guidance, and do
not announce a pause to someone whose reaction you fear.

## Continue

The review has done its work if it has made the situation clearer — not only if the
relationship continues. You may have seen encouraging movement, identified a
narrower unanswered question, recognised that specialist help is needed, or decided
not to continue. None means the period was wasted.

If the evidence suggests meaningful and sustained improvement appropriate to the
concern, go forward gladly. Act on what you now know without pretending that ninety
days proved more than they could.
