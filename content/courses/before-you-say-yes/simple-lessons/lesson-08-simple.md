## Key Scripture

## Four words that are often confused

| Word | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Boundary** | A limit I set and an action I can take | “I will end the call if I am being shouted at.” |
| **Request** | Something I ask another person to consider | “Would you put your phone away while we eat?” |
| **Agreement** | Something we both freely choose | “We agree not to post photos of each other without asking.” |
| **Control** | Pressure, monitoring, or punishment used to take away another person’s freedom | “Give me your passwords so I can check who you talk to.” |

## Audio script and transcript

Welcome to Lesson 8.

The word “boundaries” makes some Christians uncomfortable. It can sound selfish, as if rules are replacing love. Other people have seen the word misused. Someone says, “I’m setting a boundary,” just to end every conversation. Both reactions are understandable. But both miss what a boundary really is.

Let me make this simple. A boundary names your limit and what you will do to care for it. It may describe what you will take part in, what access you will give, or what action you will take if the limit is crossed. A boundary does not control the other person. It describes a choice that is yours to make.

People often use one word, “boundary,” for four different things. The lesson page shows them in a table. Here they are.

First, a boundary. This names my limit and an action I can take. For example: “I will end the call if I am being shouted at.”

Second, a request. This is something I ask you to consider. You are free to say yes or no. For example: “Would you put your phone away while we have dinner?”

Third, an agreement. This is something we both freely choose. For example: “We agree not to post photos of each other without asking.”

Fourth, control. This is when one person takes away the other person’s fair freedom through pressure, watching, or punishment. For example: “Give me your passwords so I can check who you talk to.”

Requests and agreements are normal in healthy relationships. Ask: Can both people speak honestly, disagree, or say no without pressure or punishment? A disagreement may also show that the relationship is not a good fit.

Here are three things a boundary is not.

A boundary is not a wall. A wall keeps everyone out. A boundary lets the right things in, at the right pace, so closeness can grow safely. But if a relationship is unsafe, distance may be exactly what is needed.

A boundary is not a secret test. You do not invent a limit just to see what someone will do. You name a real limit because it matters.

And a boundary is not a punishment. Ending a phone call when someone starts shouting protects you. Refusing to speak to someone for days to make them give in is punishment.

Now, why should Christians not feel guilty about boundaries?

Some Christians believe that a truly loving person has no limits. But look at Jesus. The Bible says he often withdrew from the crowds to pray. He did not do everything people asked. He let people walk away. Nobody would call him unloving.

Jesus said, “Let what you say be simply ‘Yes’ or ‘No.’” He was teaching people not to hide dishonesty behind impressive promises. His words remind us to speak truthfully and clearly. When your yes and no are honest, people can know where you stand.

Paul says, “Bear one another’s burdens.” A few verses later he says, “Each will have to bear his own load.” So we help each other with what is too heavy. But we do not take over what belongs to someone else.

Here is the main point: clear limits help love stay honest and freely given. If you cannot safely refuse something, your yes is not freely given. Healthy love does not depend on fear, guilt, or pressure.

Boundaries also go both ways. The person you are dating may have limits too. That does not mean every limit they name is wise. But if only one person in a relationship is allowed to say “this far and no further,” that is not a relationship with boundaries. That is a relationship with a ruler.

The optional workbook will help you think about six areas: emotions, faith, phones and online life, money, time, and other relationships.

Let me say a quick word about money and phones, because they matter so much. While dating, do not share bank accounts, sign for each other’s debts, or share passwords. And be very careful about anyone you have not met in person who asks for money.

So how do you state a boundary? Keep it plain. Do not turn it into an accusation or bury it under a long apology. “I will not lend money while we are dating” is enough. If the limit is crossed and it is safe to act, do what you said you would do.

Some limits you will only discover after something has happened. That is okay. You are still allowed to name a boundary afterwards.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean that if you struggle to keep a boundary, it is your fault. Fear, money worries, past trauma, and pressure can all make it hard. If you cannot keep a boundary because you are afraid of how someone will react, better boundaries are not the answer. Please get outside help.

And remember what we learned in Lesson 6. Boundaries are not a way to make an unsafe person behave better. If a line crossed is one of the serious things, like violence, threats, or sexual pressure, this is no longer a boundary conversation. It is a safety concern.

Think of one area of your life that needs a clearer limit. Which area is it?

Lord, help me speak honestly and clearly. Free me from guilt about having fair limits and from fear about keeping them. Help me respect other people’s limits too. Where a line has been crossed, give me wisdom, courage, and safe support. In Jesus’ name, amen.

## Part 1 — My boundaries

### Screen 1 — Six areas to think about

*Read these ideas first. You do not need to write anything yet.*

- **Emotional** — How much of your inner life you share, and how fast. You can care about someone’s feelings without being responsible for fixing every one of them. Emotional closeness can move faster than commitment.
- **Spiritual** — Your walk with God stays yours. Praying together can be good, but a dating partner does not become your spiritual boss. Be careful if someone claims to speak for God so that you must obey them.
- **Phone and online** — Passwords, location sharing, how fast you reply, posting photos. A dating partner is not entitled to your accounts. Never pressure anyone to send intimate pictures, and never share someone’s pictures or messages without permission.
- **Money** — Who pays, lending, shared purchases. While dating, the wise answer to shared accounts and debts is usually “not yet.” Gifts never buy affection, access, or commitment.
- **Time** — How much of your week the relationship takes, and what it pushes out: sleep, work, church, friends, rest.
- **Other relationships** — Your friendships, your family, and contact with former partners. Privacy is healthy. Secrecy that affects the other person’s safety or choices is not.

> **If you think your phone or accounts are being watched,** do not suddenly change passwords or turn off location sharing. This can alert a controlling person. Get advice from a safe device first. **Find help where you live →**

### Screen 2 — Write one boundary

*Repeat this screen for each area you want to work on. Be specific. “I will be careful with money” is not a boundary. “I will not lend or borrow money while we are dating” is.*

1. **Area:** (Emotional · Spiritual · Phone and online · Money · Time · Other relationships)
2. What does this boundary protect?
3. I will... / I will not...
4. How will I say it, simply and without apologising?
5. If it is crossed, what can I realistically and safely do? Who could support me?

### Screen 3 — Check my boundaries

For each boundary I wrote:

1. Is this about what **I** will do, a **request** I will make, or an attempt to **control** someone else?
2. Could I say this plainly, without apologising?
3. Is the action I wrote really in my control, and am I ready to take it?

## Part 2 — Talking together (optional)

### Screen 4 — Conversation guide for two

> **Only have this conversation if both of you can speak freely and safely.** If you are afraid to disagree, say “not yet,” or ask others for advice, do not use this conversation to test the relationship. Get individual guidance first.

*Nothing on this screen is saved. You do not need to show each other your private answers from Screens 1–3. Each person chooses which boundaries to share.*

Talk through these questions, in person if possible and without hurrying:

1. What boundaries does each of us want to share?
2. What requests does either of us want to make?
3. What do we both freely agree to?
4. Where do we still disagree — and does that show a difference we can live with, or a deeper mismatch?
5. How would each of us like to be told if a line is crossed?

*Do not let the more confident person’s preference become “our agreement” without real discussion.*

*Physical boundaries come in Lesson 9. A written or spoken agreement is never ongoing consent to sexual activity. Consent applies to each moment and can always be withdrawn.*

### Screen 5 — After the conversation (private)

*This is for you alone.*

1. Did I feel free to say what I really think?
2. How did they respond when I named a boundary?
3. Is there anything I agreed to that I am not comfortable with?

*Acceptance or respectful questions are a green flag. Pressure, sulking, punishment, or spiritual arguments against your limit are not.*

## Part 3 — When a line is crossed

### Screen 6 — Thinking it through

1. What happened? *(Keep it short.)*
2. Was it a misunderstanding, a one-time slip, or a pattern?
3. How serious was it?
4. If I raised it safely, did they listen, apologise, and change?
5. What did I say I would do — and can I safely do it?

*Name it calmly and specifically: “We agreed no messages after eleven, and last night you called at one” — not “You never respect me.”*

> **If the line crossed involved violence, threats, sexual pressure, stalking, being watched, or fear of how they will react,** do not announce a new boundary and wait to see what happens. This is a safety concern, not a boundary conversation. Get individual specialist guidance first. **Find help where you live →**

*You are always free to seek confidential advice or specialist support without anyone’s permission. No agreement removes that freedom. And either person can change a personal boundary at any time.*

### Screen 7 — Private journal

1. Guilt can attach to boundaries in three ways: feeling guilty for having them, being made to feel guilty for keeping them, and feeling guilty for not keeping one. Which do I recognise? Where did it come from?
2. Is there a line I would have wanted that has already been crossed? What would it take to draw it again from where I am now?

*Ask God to help you give an honest yes, an honest no, and the courage to seek help when you do not feel free.*
