## One truth to remember

You do not marry a family, but you marry into one, and you marry someone who was shaped by one.

## Key Scripture

"Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh." — Genesis 2:24 (ESV)

Cultures understand "leaving" in different ways. In many places, it does not mean cutting off family. It means the new household comes first in loyalty and decisions, while the wider family is still loved and honoured.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About eight minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 10.

You do not marry a family. But you marry into one. And you marry someone who was shaped by one. Both of those things will be part of your life for many years. It is better to think about them now, while you are still deciding, than to discover them after the wedding.

This lesson is for people from many different cultures. In some cultures, families are involved from the very first conversation. In others, families are hardly involved at all. The Bible was written in cultures where families were deeply involved in marriage. But the people marrying must still be willing. Family involvement should never replace their free choice.

Let's start with what families teach us.

All of us learned how relationships work before we were old enough to choose. We learned how conflict was handled: shouting, silence, talking it through, or never at all. We learned how love was shown, how money was talked about, who made decisions, and what faith looked like at home, not just at church.

These lessons often become our automatic reactions. But they are not our destiny. God, good friends, counselling, and practice can all help us change.

So the question is not, "Was it a good family?" A warm family does not automatically produce a healthy partner. A painful family does not automatically produce an unhealthy one. The real question is: Do they know what their family taught them? And what have they decided to do with it?

Being able to describe a family pattern is not the same as changing it. Someone may understand the pattern clearly and still repeat it. Look for real change, not only good insight.

Families also pass on strengths. You may see hospitality, generosity, hard work, shared faith, respect for elders, or the ability to make peace after conflict. Notice what is healthy as well as what needs to change.

And of course, all of this applies to you as well.

Now let's think about loyalty. The Bible tells us to honour our father and mother. That honour lasts all our lives. But for adults, honour does not always mean obedience. It does not mean giving parents control over your money, or staying close to someone who is abusive.

In many parts of the world, honouring parents includes real duties: supporting them financially, caring for them in old age, or paying for a younger brother or sister's school. These responsibilities are good. But a future spouse should know about them before the wedding, not discover them afterwards.

Remember, dating is not marriage. A boyfriend or girlfriend should not demand the loyalty, money, or authority that belongs to a spouse. At this stage, you are asking a simpler question: Could this person form a new, healthy household if you married?

When you meet their family, notice how the person you are dating treats their parents and siblings. And notice how they are treated. But be fair. One visit shows you one day. Cultures show warmth in different ways. And many adults slip back into old family roles when they are at home. The question is whether they still keep their own judgement and their own voice.

Now, what if a family objects to the relationship? Let me make this simple. Family objections usually fall into five kinds. One objection may include more than one.

First, a concern about behaviour or safety. They have seen something specific that worries them. Listen carefully. Families are sometimes wrong. But sometimes they see a pattern that attraction has hidden from you.

Second, a practical concern. Money, distance, caring for relatives, immigration, education, or health. These are about facts, and facts can be looked at together.

Third, a concern about faith or conscience. A difference in church background is not automatically prejudice. It may be about real differences in belief, worship, or how to raise children. Look at what is actually being said.

Fourth, prejudice. An objection based on ethnicity, tribe, nationality, class, disability, or family background, with no concern about character. Prejudice does not prove the relationship is wrong. But it is a real cost. And you do not have to spend years under hostility just to prove the prejudice is unfair.

Fifth, control. The family wants to make an adult's decision for them. Adults can honour their parents by listening, showing gratitude, and speaking respectfully, without giving them the final say over who to marry.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean families should be ignored. Their wisdom can be a gift. It also does not mean every family custom must be obeyed.

And please hear this clearly. Customs such as bride price, dowry, or seeking a family's blessing can be meaningful. But no payment makes a person someone else's property, and no family can say yes on another person's behalf. Everyone keeps the right to say no. An arranged marriage, where both people are genuinely free to accept or refuse, is different from a forced marriage. If someone is being pushed into marriage through threats, violence, confinement, severe shame, money or immigration pressure, or having their passport taken, that is not family honour. It is coercion. Please get specialist help privately, and do not start with a family meeting.

Pause for a moment.

What did you learn from your family about handling disagreement? Do you still do the same thing today?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

Whatever your answer, it is worth knowing.

Here is your one step for today. If you are dating, choose one question to ask the person you are dating at a calm, private moment: "What would your family expect of the person you marry?" If you are not dating, answer that question about your own family.

Let's pray.

Father, thank you for the families that shaped us, with all their strengths and all their wounds. Help me honour what is good in my family, and change what is not. Give me wisdom to understand the family of the person I may marry. Protect anyone who is being pressured or controlled. And help me build a home that receives the best from both families, without being ruled by either. In Jesus' name, amen.

That is enough for today. You can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

## Need support?

For some people, family is where they learned that love and harm came from the same people.

If the person you are dating comes from an abusive family, your role is to support them, not to fix their family or push them to reconcile. If their family is hostile or cruel to you, it is not your job to win them over. A partner who expects you to accept mistreatment "for the sake of peace" is asking too much.

No family can consent to a relationship for you, and no family can force you into one. If threats, violence, being kept somewhere against your will, being watched, severe shaming, or money or immigration pressure are being used, contact a specialist forced-marriage or domestic-abuse service privately, using a safe device. **Do not start with a family meeting or community mediation.**

**If travel is being planned and you fear being taken somewhere against your will, get specialist advice before you travel.** Warning signs include your passport or documents being taken, pressure to travel without a clear reason, threats against brothers or sisters, being watched by family or community members, and being taken out of work or school.

In the UK, contact the Forced Marriage Unit on +44 (0)20 7008 0151 or at fmu@fcdo.gov.uk. Check the current contact details and availability on GOV.UK. You can also contact a 24-hour domestic-abuse service or, if there is immediate risk, the police or emergency services.

If danger is immediate, contact the emergency service where you are. **Find help where you live →**

> **This is not a file on the other person's family.** Do not question their relatives behind their back, or write down private family information that is not yours to keep.

> **Not dating right now?** Screens 1–3 are the work, and they are some of the most useful preparation you can do.

## Part 1 — My own family (private)

### Screen 1 — What my family taught me

1. How was conflict handled in my home? What do I do now when there is conflict?
2. How was love shown? What do I now expect from a partner?
3. How was money talked about? What did I absorb?
4. Who made decisions? What do I assume about decisions in marriage?

### Screen 2 — Strengths and changes

1. What did faith look like at home? What do I expect in my own household?
2. What gifts and strengths did I receive from my family?
3. Which traditions do I hope to keep?
4. Which patterns do I want to change — and what evidence is there that I am already doing something different?

### Screen 3 — My obligations and expectations

1. What do I owe my family now, or expect to owe later? (Money, care, brothers or sisters.)
2. What does my family expect of the person I marry? How much weight do I give that?
3. Is my family's role in my decisions advice, or approval?

## Part 2 — What I have seen of theirs (private)

*For each, choose* ***Seen / Not seen yet / Mixed / Concern****, with a short example.*

### Screen 4 — How they relate to their family

1. They can talk about their family honestly and with some self-awareness.
2. They know what they took from their family and what they rejected — and I have seen real change, not just insight.
3. Their family's role in big decisions is clear, freely accepted, and could fit the kind of marriage we would both choose.
4. They relate to their family with the honour, honesty, responsibility, or protective distance their situation needs.
5. Around their family, they still keep their own judgement and voice.

### Screen 5 — Distance and responses

1. If they are distant from their family, have they shared what is appropriate for now, and have I respected their right not to share painful details before they are ready?
2. Do I understand enough to know whether the situation could affect a shared future?
3. How has their family responded to me?
4. How has the person I am dating responded to their family's response?

*A person who has left an abusive home owes you no reconciliation with it. "I'm not ready to share details, but the distance is necessary" can be an honest and enough answer for now.*

## Part 3 — Questions to ask each other over time (conversation guide)

> **Only have these conversations if you can both speak freely and safely.** Nothing on Screens 6–8 is saved. Ask a few at a time, not all at once, and not as an interview.

### Screen 6 — Family life

1. What did your family teach you about handling disagreement?
2. What do you owe your family, now and in the future?
3. What would your family expect of the person you marry?
4. How involved would you expect your family to be in our decisions if we married?
5. How much of a marriage's private life should be shared with family?

### Screen 7 — Home and money

1. Where would you expect us to live, and near whom? Is either family expecting to live with us?
2. Are there debts or regular money commitments to family?
3. Who would make decisions if a parent needed long-term care?
4. Are there relatives who would expect access to our home, money, or children?
5. Are there legal, immigration, or inheritance matters that could affect us?

### Screen 8 — Customs and children

1. What customs would your family expect at engagement or marriage? How do you feel about them?
2. Are there family customs about men's and women's roles in the home?
3. If we had children, which languages, customs, and traditions would we pass on? Where would we worship? How would grandparents be involved?
4. What about our relationship would stay private?
5. Is there anything about your family you would want me to know before we go further? *(Share only as much as you are ready to.)*

*Before engagement, you need real agreement about children, where you will live, money owed to family, family involvement, and how final decisions will be made. "We'll work it out later" is not enough when expectations are already opposite.*

## Part 4 — Sorting expectations (private)

### Screen 9 — Four groups

List what either family expects — customs, involvement, support, living arrangements. Put each into one group:

1. **Required by my conscience** — I could not honour God and refuse this.
2. **A cultural expectation I would gladly honour.**
3. **Negotiable** — could change with honest conversation.
4. **I would not accept this** — and is my reason conviction, cost, or fear?

*For anything in group 1, ask: What is the Bible basis? Is this truly a Christian duty, or a cultural expectation I have made spiritual? Does it respect both people's freedom and dignity? Have wise people tested it with me?*

## Part 5 — When families object (private)

### Screen 10 — Sorting an objection

1. What exactly was said, and by whom?
2. Which kinds apply — behaviour or safety, practical, faith or conscience, prejudice, or control? More than one?
3. What part might be fair, even if another part is prejudiced or controlling? What information would help us judge it?
4. Are we dismissing wise concern because we dislike how the family said it? Or calling prejudice "wisdom" because facing it would be costly?
5. If it is control: can either of us make a decision our parents disagree with? What help do we need?

## Part 6 — Safety

### Screen 11 — Pressure or immediate danger (part 1)

*Answer Yes or No. Your answers on Screens 11 and 12 are never saved.*

1. Either of us is being forced or pressured through threats, violence, being kept somewhere, or serious money or immigration pressure that makes saying no unsafe.
2. Either of us fears being taken abroad, kept somewhere, or stopped from returning.
3. Passports, money, phones, or identity documents have been taken or controlled.
4. Saying no may put brothers, sisters, or other relatives at risk.

### Screen 12 — Pressure or immediate danger (part 2)

1. Family or community members are watching our movements or messages.
2. Either person is under the legal age for marriage.
3. We have been told to hide the pressure from safe professionals.

> **If any answer on Screen 11 or 12 is Yes, do not arrange a family meeting or mediation, and do not tell the relatives involved that you are seeking help.** Use a safer device to contact a specialist forced-marriage, safeguarding, or domestic-abuse service. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services where you are. **Find help where you live →**

### Screen 13 — Serious family-boundary concerns

*Your answers on this screen are never saved.*

1. Their family is hostile or cruel to me, and I am expected to accept it for the sake of peace.
2. "My family expects" is used again and again to end discussion or override my voice.
3. Either of us cannot disagree with our family without being punished or losing essential support.
4. Family prejudice is being played down rather than addressed.

> These concerns need honest thought and independent advice. If threats, being watched, being kept somewhere, or fear are also present, treat it like Screen 11 and get specialist help before involving the family.

### Screen 14 — Private journal

1. What did I take from my family that I have never really examined? A marriage will eventually ask this question, whether or not anyone asks it now.
2. What did I reject — and have I replaced it with something good, or only with its absence?

*Ruth told Naomi, "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). It was her free choice, not a command for anyone to abandon their own people. But her story shows that belonging across families and cultures can be chosen. Pray that both families would be honoured where that is possible, that harmful expectations would be resisted, and that any future home would receive what is good from both.*

*If this lesson touched something painful in your own family story, be gentle with yourself. You do not need a family story that looks like anyone else's.*
