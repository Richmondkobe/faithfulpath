## One truth to remember

Christian words can make a warning sign sound holy, but they do not change what the behaviour is.

## Key Scripture

“The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for it.” — Proverbs 22:3 (ESV)

This is practical wisdom. A prudent person notices danger and responds. Seeing danger and explaining it away is not an act of greater faith. The Bible does not treat seeking safety as a failure of love, forgiveness, or trust in God.

## Seven warning signs and their disguises

| What is happening | How it may be disguised |
|---|---|
| Using God to win arguments | “God told me.” “If you trusted God, you would not question me.” |
| Control | “I am just protecting you.” “I am guarding your heart.” |
| Rushing commitment | “It is God’s timing.” “When you know, you know.” |
| Secrecy | “It is just between us and the Lord.” |
| Pushing past your “no” | “You are being legalistic.” |
| Refusing outside advice | “I answer to God, not people.” |
| Never admitting they are wrong | “I am just being honest.” “You are too sensitive.” |

## Audio script and transcript

*(About nine minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the full transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 6.

Before we begin, please hear this. If you are afraid of the person you are dating, you do not need to finish this lesson. You can go straight to Find Help Where You Live.

It is usually easier to see warning signs in someone else's relationship than in our own. A friend tells you her boyfriend checks her phone. Or that his girlfriend has cut him off from his family. From the outside, you can see the problem clearly.

In our own relationships, it is harder. We have feelings, hopes, and fears. And in Christian relationships, there is another difficulty. Christian words can make a warning sign sound like a good thing.

Let me give you some examples. Rushing into engagement becomes "God's timing." Control becomes "spiritual leadership." Cutting you off from your friends becomes "guarding what God has given us." Keeping the relationship hidden becomes "keeping it between us and the Lord." Jealousy becomes "protection."

Here is the important point. Those words do not change what the behaviour is. Whether someone means to or not, spiritual words can make it harder for you to question, object, or slow down.

Let me make this simple. A red flag is not the same as an ordinary flaw. Everyone has weaknesses and makes mistakes. Ordinary problems may be addressed through honest conversation, responsibility, and change—where it is safe to do so. A red flag is behaviour that may involve danger, dishonesty, control, coercion, or pressure against your judgement. Some red flags appear as patterns. Others are serious the first time. They call for outside support and may require distance or ending the relationship.

The lesson page shows seven warning signs. Let us walk through them now.

First, using God to win arguments. "God told me you are my wife." "If you really trusted God, you wouldn't question me." "The devil is trying to separate us." Notice what these do. They take a normal question, like "Is this too fast?", and turn it into a spiritual test you cannot win. But the Bible tells believers to "test everything." And another person's claim that God spoke to them does not give them authority over your conscience.

Some people also use the word “submit” to end a disagreement while dating. Christians understand marriage roles in different ways. But the biblical teaching about submission in marriage does not give a dating partner authority over your conscience, and it never permits pressure or control. Husbands are told to love their wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her.

Second, control that calls itself care. Wanting to know where you are all the time. Checking your phone. Objecting to your friends or your clothes. Real care respects your freedom. Control makes your life smaller. If you now have fewer friends, fewer places, and fewer freedoms than before the relationship, that is not protection.

Third, rushing you. Saying "I love you" within days. Pushing for engagement long before you know each other well. Getting upset when you ask for time. Be fair. Some cultures have shorter courtships, and that is not automatically a problem. The warning sign is pressure, and punishment when you ask for more time.

Fourth, pushing past your "no." You set a limit. They push. They apologise. Then they push again. An apology without changed behaviour does not make you safe.

Fifth, cutting you off from others. They resent it when you ask for advice. Anyone who raises a concern gets pushed away. Step by step, the relationship moves away from everyone who could see it clearly. That movement is the warning sign.

The sixth warning sign is secrecy, and the seventh is never admitting they are wrong. Privacy is healthy. Everyone deserves personal space, and you do not have to give anyone your passwords. But if someone insists that the relationship must be hidden from safe people who care about you, ask why. A person may honestly disagree with you, but repeated blame-shifting, false apologies, or making you responsible for every problem prevents accountability and change.

Now I need to say something very important. Some things are serious the very first time. You do not need to wait for a pattern.

These include physical violence, especially choking or strangling; blocking you from leaving; threats; forced or pressured sexual activity; stalking or tracking; gaining access to your accounts; and serious deception that affects your safety or freedom to choose.

If you have been choked or strangled, seek urgent medical help, even if you have no visible injuries or feel well. Strangulation can cause serious hidden injuries.

These things are not problems to work on together. If you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services where you are. If not, contact a specialist abuse service. Do not confront the person alone, and do not start with couples counselling.

If someone threatens suicide or self-harm to stop you from leaving, take the threat seriously. Contact a crisis service, or emergency services if the danger appears immediate. Do not promise to keep it secret or try to manage it alone. You are not responsible for preventing suicide by remaining in the relationship.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean every flaw is a red flag. It does not mean you should become suspicious of everyone. And it does not mean forgiveness is wrong. Forgiveness is commanded. But forgiving someone does not mean you must trust them again or give them another chance to hurt you.

Here is one simple tool. When someone uses spiritual words to make a point, take the spiritual words away. What is left? "If you trusted God, you wouldn't question me" becomes "Don't question me." "God told me you're my wife" becomes "Commit to me now, before you have time to decide." In plain words, you can usually see it.

Pause for a moment.

In a safe relationship, you can say, "This bothered me," and be heard. They may disagree. They may need time. But you will not be frightened, punished, or spiritually pressured. Ask yourself: Can I raise a concern without fear?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

If you feel afraid to raise a concern, that fear is telling you something important.

Here is your one step for today. If something in this lesson worried you, choose one trusted person or a specialist service, and talk to them. If nothing worried you, be thankful, and keep going with ordinary wisdom, not suspicion.

Let's pray.

Lord, give me the courage to see things as they really are. Where I have been explaining things away, help me to see clearly. Give me courage to speak to one safe person who can help me. Where I have been told that seeing clearly is doubting you, show me that it is not. If I am in danger, lead me to help, and go with me. Amen.

Unless you are in immediate danger, you do not need to decide the future of the whole relationship today. Take the next safe step: seek support, continue only if it is safe, or stop here and return later.

## Part 2 — The seven warning signs

*For each sentence, choose **Seen / Not seen / Mixed / Not sure**. For “Seen” or “Mixed,” write no more than one short line. Do not record detailed or potentially dangerous information here.*

### Screen 3 — Using God to win arguments

1. They use God, the Bible, prophecy, or a leader's authority to end disagreements or override my judgement.
2. Questioning them is treated as doubting God, disobeying, or siding with the devil.
3. They have claimed God confirmed something in order to pressure a decision.

### Screen 4 — Control that calls itself care

1. They want to know where I am and who I am with, and are unhappy when I go somewhere without them.
2. They object to my friends, family time, or how I dress.
3. My life has become smaller — fewer people, places, or freedoms — since the relationship began.

### Screen 5 — Rushing commitment

1. They pushed for being exclusive, engagement, or marriage long before we really knew each other.
2. Asking for time led to frustration, hurt, or pressure.
3. I have made commitments bigger than what I really know about them.

### Screen 6 — Secrecy

1. They have hidden information that affects my safety or my freedom to choose.
2. Their identity, relationship status, or important life stories have turned out to be false or keep changing.
3. They want me to hide the relationship from safe people, without a fair reason we both agreed on.

*Privacy is not secrecy. Refusing to share passwords is not proof of lying — and demanding someone's passwords can itself be controlling.*

### Screen 7 — Pushing past my "no"

1. I have set a clear limit, and it was pushed, apologised for, and pushed again.
2. They treat my "no" as temporary, or something I must keep defending.
3. My limits are called legalism, coldness, or a lack of love.

### Screen 8 — Refusing outside advice

1. They discourage or resent me asking others for advice about the relationship.
2. They have cut off everyone who raised a concern about them.
3. The relationship is being moved, step by step, away from people who could see it.

*Be fair: moving house, disability, social anxiety, persecution, or a harmful family can explain having few long friendships. The warning is pushing away honest feedback and separating you from support.*

### Screen 9 — Never admitting they are wrong

1. They are never wrong about the specific thing I raised.
2. Their apologies are not followed by change.
3. I am the one who always ends up apologising, or I have stopped raising things.

*Disagreeing is not the same as refusing correction. Someone may hear you and still honestly see it differently.*

## Part 4 — Truths that get twisted

### Screen 13 — True teaching, wrongly used

*Each teaching is true. The way it is twisted is not.*

- **Forgiveness.** Jesus commands forgiveness. But forgiveness does not automatically restore trust, remove consequences, or require you to go back.
- **“Do not judge.”** Jesus also taught, “Thus you will recognise them by their fruits” (Matthew 7:20, ESV). You can recognise behaviour without claiming to judge someone’s soul.
- **“Do not gossip.”** Asking a wise, trustworthy person for advice about your own life is not gossip. “In an abundance of counsellors there is safety” (Proverbs 11:14, ESV). Choose someone discreet who understands abuse.
- **Suffering.** God can use hardship to shape us. He does not ask you to accept mistreatment. Paul warned Timothy, "Beware of him," about a man who had harmed him (2 Timothy 4:14–15).
- **Grace.** Grace does not give anyone another chance to harm you. Real change needs safety, time, and accountability. Sometimes getting back together is neither wise nor safe.

### Screen 14 — Boundaries are not a technique

Lessons 8 and 9 teach about boundaries. But boundaries are for two healthy people who respect each other. They are not a method for making an unsafe person behave better.

If every limit becomes a fight and you are exhausted from defending lines that should not need repeated defence, the answer is not simply a better boundary script. The next step may be individual specialist support, greater distance, or ending the relationship safely.

*Answering this does not make you responsible for anyone else's control or abuse. Look honestly at your own behaviour, without using your own faults to excuse what has been done to you.*

1. Have I used any of these behaviours in this relationship or another?
2. What would real change look like for me — not just admitting it, but accepting consequences, respecting others' distance, getting help where needed, and changing over time?
