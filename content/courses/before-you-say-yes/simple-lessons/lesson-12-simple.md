# Lesson 12 — Understand Their Past Wisely

## Today's question

What do I need to know about someone's past, and what is not mine to know?

## One truth to remember

You can believe God has forgiven someone and still decide, without condemning them, that you should not marry them.

## Key Scripture

"As far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us." — Psalm 103:12 (ESV)

This psalm describes how completely God forgives a repentant person. It does not say that every consequence disappears, that trust returns automatically, or that anyone is owed a marriage. This lesson holds both of those truths together.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About nine minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the full transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 12.

Everyone you might marry has a past. Some of it will be ordinary. Some of it might include a broken engagement, a divorce, a child, an addiction, a time away from God, a criminal record, or harm that was done to them.

You will need to know some of it. You are not entitled to all of it. And how you respond to what you learn says as much about you as it does about them.

Christians often get this wrong in two opposite ways. Some treat the past as destiny. "They were divorced, so they are disqualified." "They struggled with addiction, so they always will." Others treat the past as if it does not matter at all. "Who they were is not important. Only who they are now." Neither of these is true, and neither is truly Christian.

Let's begin with what the past cannot tell you.

It cannot tell you whether a person is forgiven. That is between them and God. If God has forgiven a repentant person, you must never treat them as a lesser Christian or as permanently unclean.

But here is the most important sentence in this lesson. Forgiveness does not mean you must marry someone. You can believe God has forgiven a person, and still decide, without condemning them, that you should not marry them. Saying no to a relationship is not the same as denying forgiveness. Please hold on to that. It protects you from being pressured in the name of grace.

So what can the past tell you? Let me make this simple. Ask four questions.

First, truth. Are they honest about the important facts? Does what they say remain reasonably consistent over time? Honest disclosure about a hard past can show courage and integrity. A clean-looking record, by itself, does not prove good character.

This question is about facts they are responsible for sharing. It is not a test of how neatly someone tells a trauma story. A person may not be ready or able to give a complete account, and that alone is not dishonesty.

Second, responsibility. Where they caused harm, can they say so, without making excuses or blaming everyone else? But be careful. If someone was harmed, never pressure them to take blame for what was done to them.

Third, response. What did they actually do about the harm they caused or the pattern they needed to change? Did they get appropriate help, make things right where possible and safe, and change their behaviour? "I've dealt with that" is a claim. Changed behaviour over time is evidence.

Fourth, present effect. What from the past is still active today? An unhealthy attachment to a former partner, unfinished legal matters, children and custody, debts, or old patterns repeating.

Let me say a word about a few common situations.

If someone has children, remember that a child is a person, not baggage. Be honest with yourself about whether you could love and commit to a child who is not yours. And a wise parent will take time before introducing a new partner to their child.

If someone has struggled with addiction, recovery is a journey, and it is not always smooth. Ask whether an active addiction is being hidden, whether appropriate help is being refused, and whether the situation affects present safety or stability. Remember, you cannot be their treatment plan.

If someone was divorced, Christian churches teach different things about remarriage. Talk with your pastor early.

And if someone's past includes violence towards a partner or a child, or sexual offences, please hear this clearly. Time and sincere repentance may matter to God, but they do not guarantee safety. You cannot decide this by prayer and observation alone. Get advice from a qualified safeguarding professional.

Now, something very important about trauma.

What was done to a person is not a "past" to evaluate. It is a wound. It does not reveal anything wrong in their character.

Survivors decide when to share and how much of their story to share. If someone says, "There are things in my past I'm not ready to talk about," that can be an honest and complete answer for now. Please do not push for details. As a relationship moves towards commitment, they may choose to explain any present needs or effects that matter to a shared life without telling the story of what happened.

And if someone tells you about harm done to them, here is what helps. Say something like: "Thank you for trusting me. I am sorry this happened. It was not your fault. You do not have to tell me details. What would help you feel respected and safe?" Avoid questions that sound like blame, such as, "Why didn't you leave?" Do not ask them to prove that they are healed.

If you are the survivor, you owe no one your story before you are ready. The timing is yours. The details are yours. And a partner who cannot receive what you share with care is showing you something about them, not about you.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean you should secretly investigate someone's past. Do not try to obtain private records, contact former partners, or ask friends to find things out. If a serious safety or legal concern requires a lawful check, first get qualified advice and use the proper process. You are never entitled to details of someone's trauma or a list of their past sexual partners.

Also remember, what applies to them applies to you. Share honestly at the right time, to the same standard you expect from them.

And do not assume a clean record means good character. Someone who has never had to admit being wrong and make things right may simply be untested. The apostle Paul called himself a former "blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent." But he "received mercy." Grace can reach anyone.

Pause for a moment.

Which way do you tend to lean? Do you treat a person's past as their destiny? Or do you treat it as if it does not matter?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

Knowing your tendency will help you be both wise and gracious.

Here is your one step for today. Ask yourself: Is there something in my own past that someone I date will one day need to know? If so, think about the right stage to share it, and what support you might need. You do not need to write down any details.

Let's pray.

Father, thank you that you remove our sins as far as the east is from the west. Help me to see my own past as forgiven. Help me to treat other people's pasts with grace and honesty. Give me wisdom to see what still matters today, without holding against anyone what you have forgiven. And where harm has been done to me or to someone I love, bring healing and safe help. In Jesus' name, amen.

That is enough for today. You can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

## Need support?

**If someone's past includes violence towards a partner, child, or vulnerable person, or sexual offences,** the four questions are not enough. Time, treatment, and repentance may matter, but none of them guarantees safety. Do not try to assess the risk yourself. Get qualified safeguarding advice before moving towards commitment.

If a child or vulnerable adult may be in danger now, do not confront the person privately and do not contact a possible victim to investigate. Do not copy, forward, download, or continue opening illegal or intimate material as "evidence." If material is already on your device, get safeguarding or legal advice before deleting, moving, or changing it. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services. Do not promise to keep the concern secret.

If you have been asked to keep quiet about something that affects other people's safety, you do not have to work out what to do alone. Get confidential safeguarding advice.

If this lesson brought up harm done to you, a trauma-informed counsellor can help. If it involved control, abuse, stalking, or sexual violence, begin with a specialist service. A trusted pastor can support you spiritually alongside that help. **Find help where you live →**

## Part 1 — My own past (private)

### Screen 1 — The four questions for me

*Do not write details. Short answers only.*

1. **Truth:** Is there important information I will need to share before a bigger commitment?
2. **Responsibility:** Where I contributed to what went wrong in past relationships, can I say so without collapsing or making excuses?
3. **Response:** What real steps have I taken about the things that needed dealing with?
4. **Present effect:** Is anything from my past still affecting my life now — an attachment, an obligation, a pattern, or something unfinished?
5. If I have been harmed: when, and with what support, would I want a partner to know? *(No details.)*

## Part 2 — What I know about theirs (private)

*Choose* ***Seen / Not seen yet / Mixed / Concern****. Leave blank anything your relationship has not reached yet.*

### Screen 2 — The four questions for them

1. **Truth:** What they have told me about things they were responsible for has been honest and consistent. *(This question is not about how someone shares trauma. Trauma memories can be delayed or fragmented, and that is not lying.)*
2. **Responsibility:** Where they caused harm, they can say so.
3. **Response:** Where their own behaviour needed to change, they can describe real steps. Where they were harmed, I have not demanded proof of healing.
4. **Present effect:** I understand any important ways the past still affects their obligations, safety, or our relationship.

### Screen 3 — Patterns and respect

1. If there is a pattern across their past relationships, have I noticed it — and raised it as a question, not a verdict?
2. If they have said "I'm not ready to talk about that," have I respected their privacy without pretending that I must continue towards commitment while an important question remains unanswered?

## Part 3 — What needs to be known, and when (private)

*Choose* ***Known / Not yet known / Does not apply****, for the stage you have reached.*

### Screen 4 — Before becoming exclusive (part 1)

1. Any current marriage or legal partnership.
2. Children and other dependants.
3. Any current relationship with someone else.
4. Anything creating an immediate safety risk.

### Screen 5 — Before becoming exclusive (part 2)

1. Active addiction that seriously affects daily life or safety.
2. Legal restrictions affecting contact or travel.
3. Any history of violence or sexual offending that matters for present safety.

### Screen 6 — Before engagement (part 1)

1. Previous marriages and ongoing obligations.
2. Children, custody, and support arrangements.
3. Significant debt or legal problems.
4. Addiction or recovery matters that affect shared life.
5. Criminal history that affects safety, legal duties, or our future plans.

### Screen 7 — Before engagement (part 2)

1. Health information that affects the other person's medical decisions or our practical shared life — shared at a suitable level, without handing over private records.
2. Ongoing contact or obligations involving former partners.
3. Immigration and residence matters.
4. Any other fact that, if hidden, would stop the other person making a free and informed choice about marriage.
5. **Have I shared to the same standard?**

*You are never entitled to the details of someone's trauma, a list of past sexual partners, a full account of every past relationship, or anything that is another person's secret to keep.*

## Part 4 — Sharing and receiving (private)

### Screen 8 — Giving and receiving hard news

1. Is there something important I need to share? At what stage, where, and with what support?
2. When they shared something hard, how did I respond — with thanks, with time, without shaming, and keeping it private?
3. Have I ever used something they told me in trust against them?
4. If they responded to me with shaming, revenge, or pressure, what did that show me?

*When receiving hard news: thank them first. Take time to think if you need it, and say so. Never use what was shared in trust as a weapon later. You are still free to slow down, seek advice, or end the relationship. A thoughtful decision is not a punishment.*

*When sharing: choose the right stage, speak plainly, and say what happened, what you did about it, and where you are now. Choose a setting where you feel safe and can leave if you need to.*

## Part 5 — Safety

### Screen 9 — Safety check

*Answer Yes or No. Your answers on this screen are never saved.*

1. Their past includes violence towards a partner, a child, or a vulnerable person, or sexual offences.
2. I am being asked to accept "that was the old me" without real evidence of change — such as professional treatment, following legal or safeguarding requirements, and stability over time.
3. Their behaviour now — anger, control, contempt — matches the past.
4. I have been asked to keep something quiet that affects other people's safety.

> **Do not try to obtain private records, contact former partners or possible victims, or ask friends to investigate. If a lawful check may be needed, get qualified safeguarding or legal advice first.**

**If you answered Yes, which fits best?**

- **Immediate danger** → Contact emergency or specialist services.
- **A current safeguarding concern** → Get qualified safeguarding advice soon. Do not investigate or confront alone.
- **A past concern that needs assessment** → Do not move towards engagement until you have specialist advice and enough information.

**Find help where you live →**

### Screen 10 — Private journal

1. Which way do I lean — treating a past as destiny, or as if it does not matter? What has that cost me or someone else?
2. Is there a decision I have been avoiding because it would feel like condemning someone? Remember: saying no is not condemning. What changes if I separate the two?

*Pray Psalm 103:12 for yourself first — that you would know your own past is forgiven. Then pray for the person you are considering. Ask for wisdom to see clearly both what happened and what has followed, without holding against anyone what God has forgiven, and without ignoring what is still plainly present.*
