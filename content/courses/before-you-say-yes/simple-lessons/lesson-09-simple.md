## One truth to remember

A conviction is much easier to keep with a plan made in advance than with willpower in the moment.

## Key Scripture

"But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires." — Romans 13:14 (ESV)

Paul is writing about sinful desire in general, not dating. But his words give practical wisdom: do not keep putting yourself in situations where keeping your conviction becomes unnecessarily hard. Wise choices are often made before the moment of temptation, not during it.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About nine minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 9.

Most single Christians have heard the same short message about sex many times: "Wait until marriage." What they hear much less often is anything practical. How do two people who are attracted to each other, who want to honour God, and who spend a lot of time together, actually keep that conviction?

That is what this lesson is about.

This course holds the historic Christian conviction that sexual intercourse and deliberately sexual activity belong within marriage. Christians draw different practical lines around things like kissing and physical closeness. Those differences make honest planning important. But they do not remove the call to sexual integrity.

Let me begin with what the Bible does not say, because much harm has been done here.

The Bible does not say sex is dirty. The same Bible contains the Song of Songs, which celebrates love and desire. The problem was never desire. Sex is a good gift, meant for the covenant of marriage.

The Bible does not say your worth depends on your sexual past. A person who has sinned sexually and turned to God is fully forgiven. They are not a lesser Christian, and they are not "damaged goods." If you were taught otherwise, you were taught wrongly.

The Bible does not make one person responsible for managing another person's desire. Each person is responsible for their own choices.

And the Bible does not say that someone who was forced or pressured has sinned. If sexual activity happened to you without your free consent, that was not your sin. It was a wrong done to you.

So why do so many sincere Christian couples struggle? Partly because desire is strong. God made it that way. But mostly because couples rely on willpower in the moment instead of wisdom beforehand. They have a rule, but no plan. They are alone, late at night, tired, and affectionate. And they discover that a conviction is very hard to keep in that moment.

Remember Joseph in the book of Genesis. When his master's wife pressured him, he ran out of the house. His situation was different from dating. He was under pressure from someone with power over him. But his choice shows us something practical. Sometimes wisdom means leaving the room, not staying to prove how strong you are.

Let me make this simple. A good plan has four parts.

First, your surroundings. Where and when are you most likely to cross your line? For many couples, it is late at night, alone, tired, or after drinking. So plan around it. Say goodnight earlier. Spend time where other people are around.

Second, your pace. Affection should grow slowly and together. If one person is always pushing, and the other is always holding back, name it. That is not fair, and it will not last.

Third, your phone. Sexual messages, videos, and intimate pictures are part of your sexual choices, and they bring serious privacy and legal risks. Never pressure anyone to create or send them. Never share another person's intimate image. Never ask for, create, send, forward, or save a sexual image of anyone under eighteen. If one is sent to you, do not forward it; seek confidential legal or child-protection guidance.

Fourth, support. Consider choosing one wise, trustworthy person outside the relationship who knows the limits you are trying to keep. They are not there to control or shame you. They can pray for you, encourage you, and help you think clearly.

Now, one of the most important things in this lesson. Consent.

Consent means a clear, free, and informed yes. It is not simply the absence of a no. Silence is not consent. Freezing is not consent. Giving in after repeated pressure is not consent. Consent applies to each moment, and it can be withdrawn at any time. An engagement ring is not consent. Saying yes last week is not consent. A plan written down together is not consent.

If someone freezes, goes quiet, pulls away, or stops taking part, stop. Check only when they are able to answer freely. Someone who is asleep, unconscious, or too affected by alcohol or drugs to understand and choose cannot consent.

It is fine for two people to talk honestly about different convictions. But if someone keeps pushing after you have said no, or uses guilt, sulking, threats, or spiritual arguments to get their way, that is not a boundary problem. That is pressure, and it needs outside help.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean that your body's automatic reactions are sin. Bodies respond. The question is what you choose to seek or continue.

And if you have crossed a line in the past, it does not mean you are ruined. If both of you freely crossed a line you had agreed on, confess honestly to God. He forgives. Then do not fall into despair, and do not give up either. Instead, ask what went wrong, and change your plan. And do not rush into marriage just to solve temptation. That can trade a short-term problem for a lifelong one.

If you were pressured, it was not a shared failure. You do not need to confess anything to the person who pressured you. Please get support.

And if pregnancy, infection, injury, or assault may be involved, please see a doctor or sexual health service soon. Some medical help is time-sensitive. Getting medical help is not a sign of weak faith.

Pause for a moment.

When and where are you most likely to find your line hard to keep?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

Knowing that is the beginning of wisdom.

Here is your one step for today. Choose one situation that makes your line harder to keep. Then write one specific change. Not "we will be more careful." Something clear, like "we will not stay at the flat after ten o'clock."

Let's pray.

Father, thank you that you made desire, and that you call it good. Help me honour you with my body and honour the person I care about. Give me wisdom to plan ahead, and courage to leave when I need to. Where I have failed, thank you for your forgiveness. Where I have been hurt, bring healing and safe help. In Jesus' name, amen.

That is enough for today. You can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

## Need support?

If you read about consent and pressure and recognised something that happened to you, put the plan aside. It is not what you need. Begin with a specialist sexual-violence or abuse service. If pregnancy, infection, injury, or assault may be involved, medical help can be time-sensitive. Getting medical care does not by itself mean you have decided to report to the police, although some reporting rules may apply depending on where you live and your situation. Where you can, ask the service about confidentiality before sharing details.

A trusted pastor can support you spiritually alongside that help, as long as they do not push you towards confronting the person, getting back together, or couples counselling. If you fear the person's reaction, get support before raising anything with them. **Find help where you live →**

> **Not dating right now?** Screens 1 and 2 are the work, and they are worth doing now.

## Part 1 — My conviction and my line (private)

### Screen 1 — What I believe

1. In my own words, what do I believe the Bible asks of me sexually before marriage?
2. For kissing and physical closeness, where am I drawing my line? Be specific.
3. When and where am I most likely to cross my own line? (Late, alone, tired, after drinking?)
4. *Optional and private:* Is there anything about physical closeness I would want a future partner to understand? *You do not need to write any details of past harm here.*

### Screen 2 — Checking my line

Ask these about any kind of physical affection:

1. Can I do this with a clear conscience before God?
2. Is this deliberately trying to go further, or making wise choices harder?
3. Can either of us stop without pressure, guilt, or punishment?
4. Does this respect what we have both honestly said we believe?
5. Would I want to hide this because it goes against what I said I believe?

*An automatic physical reaction is not a moral failure. The question is what you choose to seek or continue.*

## Part 2 — Talking together (optional)

> **Only have these conversations if both of you can speak freely and safely.** Choose a time when you are both sober, rested, and not late at night. Nothing on Screens 3–5 is saved. Neither of you needs to show the other your private answers.

### Screen 3 — Our line

Talk through:

1. What parts of our private answers does each of us want to share?
2. What is our line, in plain words? What will we do, and what will we not do?
3. Where do we differ? When our limits are different, we stop at the earlier limit. Neither person has to adopt the other's conviction as their own. Either person may decide the difference means the relationship is not a good fit, but neither may pressure, negotiate in the moment, or punish.
4. What have we agreed about messages, photos, and video?

### Screen 4 — Our plan

Talk through:

1. When and where are we most at risk? What will we change? (Leave earlier, meet where others are, change direction while it is still easy.)
2. How do we want affection to grow — together, and not one pushing while the other holds back?
3. What words or signals will mean "pause" or "stop"? How will we respond straight away and respectfully?
4. Can each of us leave safely and on our own if we need to?
5. Has either of us felt rushed, or left holding the line alone? What will we change?

### Screen 5 — Wise support

Talk through:

1. Is there a wise person outside the relationship whom each of us would feel safe talking to?
2. Are they discreet and safe? Would they respond with wisdom rather than shame? Do they understand the difference between a shared failure and pressure?
3. What are we both happy for them to know, and what stays private?
4. Either of us may also seek individual support at any time, without the other's permission.

## Part 3 — Afterwards

### Screen 6 — After the conversation (private)

*This is for you alone.*

1. Did I feel free to say what I really believe?
2. Did I feel pressured to accept a line I was not comfortable with?
3. How did they respond when I named my line?

### Screen 7 — If we crossed our line (conversation guide)

> **Only if what happened was freely chosen by both of you.** If either person was pressured, afraid, or too affected by alcohol or drugs to understand and choose freely, skip this and go to Screen 8. If you are unsure whether it was fully free, stop and get confidential individual guidance.

*Nothing on this screen is saved. Do not write down details.*

Talk through:

1. Are we both sure that what happened was freely chosen? Does either of us feel blamed, pressured, or afraid?
2. Does either of us need medical or sexual-health advice?
3. What made crossing the line more likely?
4. What in our plan needs to change?
5. What support would help, and what is each of us free to share with others?

*If this keeps happening despite real effort, or if either of you feels unable to hold a limit freely, get help now. Do not wait for it to happen a certain number of times.*

### Screen 8 — What always stays true

*Read this whenever you need it. There is nothing to fill in.*

- Consent is a clear, free, and informed yes. It applies to each moment, and it can be withdrawn at any time. A past yes, an engagement, living together, or a written plan is never consent.
- Using sulking, guilt, repeated requests, threats, or money, housing, immigration, or spiritual pressure to get sexual activity can be coercion. Coercion is not a boundary problem to be solved with a better plan.
- Silence, freezing, pulling away, or no longer taking part is not consent. Stop. Check only when the person is able to answer freely.
- Someone who is asleep, unconscious, or too affected by alcohol or drugs to understand and choose cannot consent.
- Anything that happened to me without my free consent was not my sin.
- If pregnancy, infection, injury, or assault may be involved, medical help may be time-sensitive.
- Either of us may seek confidential outside help at any time, without the other's permission.

### Screen 9 — Private journal

1. What have I been taught about sex, or about my own worth, that this lesson says is not in the Bible? What do I want to unlearn?
2. If shame has shaped how I think about this, whose voice is it? What would I say to a friend in the same place?

*Pray 1 John 1:9 honestly, whether you are making a plan for the first time or starting again after a failure. If what happened was not freely chosen, it was not your failure to confess. Ask God for the practical wisdom of Romans 13:14 — to plan ahead, so your conviction can be kept in ordinary rooms and ordinary hours.*
