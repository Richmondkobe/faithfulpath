# Lesson 15 — Can You Build a Life Together?

## Today's question

We may share the same faith, but do the lives we want actually fit together?

## One truth to remember

A shared faith is the foundation, but you still need to know whether you can build the same house on it.

## Key Scripture

"By wisdom a house is built, and by understanding it is established." — Proverbs 24:3 (ESV)

This proverb is about households in general, not choosing a spouse. But notice the order. Wisdom builds. Understanding makes it firm. Neither word describes a feeling. Both describe knowing what you are working with before you start building.

## Three groups for your differences

| Group | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Liveable** | Can be part of everyday life together. Needs patience and adjustment, not complete agreement. | Temperament, ways of resting, showing affection |
| **Needing agreement before engagement** | Must be mostly settled before you commit. "We'll work it out later" is not enough. | Children, where to live, household roles, family obligations, money, life direction |
| **Incompatible** | Two lives that cannot both be lived in one shared future, and neither person can freely change now. | Opposite certainties about children, or a country one person cannot leave and the other cannot live in |

## Audio script and transcript

*(About eight minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the full transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 15.

In Lesson 2, you asked whether the two of you share the same foundation of faith. This lesson asks a different question. Could you build a house on that foundation?

Christians sometimes mix these two up. "We are both believers. We pray together. Our pastor approves." That is a foundation. But it is not yet a house. Two people can share a real faith in Christ and still want very different lives. Different countries. Different family plans. Different ways of handling money, conflict, and time.

Let me make one thing clear. Being compatible does not mean being the same. In Genesis, the woman is described as "a helper fit for him"—a true partner, not a copy. Ecclesiastes says, "Two are better than one," because each can help the other. A strong partnership does not require identical personalities, gifts, or preferences.

So the question is not, "Are we alike?" The question is, "Can we build one life out of two, with the differences we actually have?"

That has two parts. What are the differences? And can you handle differences well?

Here is why the second part matters so much. Two people may differ on many things and still build well if they can talk honestly, repair conflict, and make room for each other. Two people may agree on almost everything and still struggle if disagreement leads to punishment, contempt, or pressure. Every relationship eventually meets a real difference.

But good communication cannot fix everything. It cannot make two opposite futures fit together. Sometimes honest conversation simply makes it clear that two lives do not fit. That clarity is still a gift.

So what areas should you think about? The workbook covers seven: your personality, how you handle conflict, your calling, where you will live, what you expect marriage to look like, children, and your daily lifestyle.

Some of these may sound small at first. But where you live can become a source of deep, long-term unhappiness when it is assumed rather than discussed. "I always thought we'd end up back home" is a sentence that should be said before engagement, not after.

Once you see your differences clearly, sort them. Let me make this simple. There are three groups.

First, liveable. These differences can become part of the ordinary texture of your marriage. One of you is a planner and the other is spontaneous. One needs more quiet time and the other enjoys more company. These may need patience, adjustment, and humour rather than a final solution. Over time, you may even come to value them.

Second, needing agreement before engagement. These should not be left to assumptions. They must be mostly settled before you commit: whether to have children, where you will live, how you understand roles and responsibilities at home, obligations to family, expectations about money, and the direction of your lives. "We'll work it out later" is not enough when your present expectations are already opposite.

Third, incompatible. These are differences where two lives cannot both be lived in one shared future, and neither person can honestly and freely change right now. For example, one person is certain they want children, and the other is certain they do not. Do not move something out of this group just because you hope someone might change later.

This third group is the hardest to admit, because it can exist between two good people who love each other. We will talk about that in Lesson 16. For now, remember this: an incompatibility is not a failure of love. It is honest information about what a shared life would cost.

Be careful as you sort. Attraction wants to put everything in the first group. Fear wants to put everything in the third. Neither is honest.

Now, Jesus once asked, which of you, wanting to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost? He was talking about following him, not about marriage. But the picture fits. Before you build, sit down and count the cost.

Every marriage requires choices and sacrifices. A person may move to another country, change a career plan, or take on some responsibility towards a partner's family. Many people choose such costs gladly. The danger is a cost that was never named or freely chosen. One person assumes the other has agreed to it, and years later the hidden cost becomes resentment.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

A difference is not a red flag, and a red flag is not a difference. Control, lies, or threats do not belong in any of these three groups. If you have seen the warning signs from Lesson 6, this sorting is the wrong tool. Safety comes first.

A marriage should never require one person to give up their conscience, safety, dignity, or essential responsibilities. Change and sacrifice may be part of marriage, but they must not erase one person's voice or freedom.

This lesson also does not promise a happy marriage. Compatibility helps you judge whether a shared life is workable. Faithfulness, forgiveness, patience, and grace are still needed to live that life day after day.

Pause for a moment.

Is there something you have been assuming about the future — where you will live, children, or roles at home — that you have never actually said out loud?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

Here is your one step for today. Write down one important assumption about your future that you have never said out loud. If you can both speak freely and safely, plan a good time to talk about it. Do not bring it up casually, or at the end of an already difficult conversation.

Let's pray.

Lord, you are the builder of every good house. Give us wisdom to see our differences honestly, and humility to sort them truthfully. Help us count the cost before we build. Where our lives can fit together, give us joy and patience. Where they cannot, give us courage and kindness. In Jesus' name, amen.

That is enough for today. You can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

## Need support?

This lesson assumes two people who can each say what they want from life and be heard. If naming a difference brings threats, repeated pressure, guilt used to make you give in, sulking used as punishment, or other retaliation, the problem is not compatibility, and this is not the right tool. Lesson 6 describes that pattern. The answer is individual guidance, not a better conversation.

If this lesson brought up something you have known for a long time but have not been able to say — a country you cannot leave, a conviction you cannot set aside, a certainty about children — you may need help saying it more than help deciding it. A qualified counsellor or a wise, independent person can help. **Find help where you live →**

> **Early in a relationship?** This workbook is for later. Read the seven areas, notice them as ordinary life shows them to you, and come back when there is something real to sort.

## Part 1 — Seven areas (private)

*For each area, write what you know about yourself, what you have seen in them, and any difference.*

### Screen 1 — Personality and conflict

1. **Personality:** My temperament, and theirs. What is the difference?
2. Over time, does the way we spend time together feel sustainable for both of us?
3. **Conflict:** How do I raise concerns, listen, and make peace? How do they?
4. Have I seen us disagree and make peace? What happened?

### Screen 2 — Calling and location

1. **Calling:** What do I believe my life is for? What do they believe theirs is for?
2. Could both be lived in one home? Would I be sharing their calling, or supporting it — and could I do that gladly for decades?
3. **Location:** Where do I expect to live, and near whom? Where do they?
4. Is there an assumption about location I have not said out loud?

### Screen 3 — Marriage and children

1. **Marriage:** My picture of roles, money, decisions, and time together and apart. Theirs.
2. Where we differ, is it a preference or a conviction?
3. **Children:** Does each of us hope to have children? What do we believe about raising them?
4. How would we respond to infertility, pregnancy loss, an unexpected pregnancy, or raising a child with a disability or additional needs? Does either of us already have children or responsibilities for children?

### Screen 4 — Daily life

1. **Lifestyle:** My pace, rest, company, solitude, food, health, and weekends. Theirs.
2. What is the one daily difference I would find hardest to live with?

## Part 2 — Talking together (optional)

### Screen 5 — Conversation guide for two

> **Only have this conversation if you can both speak freely and safely.** If you are afraid to disagree, say "not yet," or seek advice, do not use this conversation to see what will happen. Get individual guidance first. If safety is in question, Lesson 6 comes first.

*Nothing on this screen is saved. Each person chooses what to share from their private answers.*

Go through the seven areas, one at a time. For each, talk about:

1. Where do we agree?
2. Where do we differ?
3. Is the difference a preference or a conviction?
4. Has either of us been assuming the other had accepted something that was never said?

*As you talk, notice whether you can disagree and stay connected. That is important evidence. But it does not solve the difference itself.*

## Part 3 — Sorting (private)

### Screen 6 — Three groups

Place each difference into one group:

1. **Liveable** — part of everyday life; needs patience, not a solution:
2. **Needing agreement before engagement** — must be mostly settled, not postponed:
3. **Incompatible** — cannot both be lived in one future without one person giving up something central, and no freely chosen solution exists now:
4. What would a wise outside voice say about how I sorted these? *(Lesson 14's circle is the place to ask.)*

*Do not move something out of "Incompatible" just because someone might change later.*

### Screen 7 — Counting the cost

1. What would I freely be choosing to give up, change, or carry?
2. What do *they* say they would freely choose — not what I assume for them?
3. Is each choice truly free, or is either of us agreeing mainly out of fear of losing the relationship?
4. Could I make this choice without treating the other person as if they owe me forever?
5. What could make this cost too heavy to carry, and what support would we need?

## Part 4 — Where this leaves me (private)

### Screen 8 — My summary

1. Differences we both see as liveable:
2. Things that need real agreement before engagement:
3. Anything one or both of us see as incompatible:
4. What is still unclear, and what has wise counsel added?

**The next step I am choosing:**

- ☐ Keep getting to know each other, without moving towards engagement
- ☐ Have one important conversation
- ☐ Seek independent advice
- ☐ Slow down and let more become clear
- ☐ Admit a possible incompatibility, and continue to Lesson 16
- ☐ I am not ready to choose yet

> **No number of items decides the outcome.** One unresolved difference about children, location, or calling may matter more than several ordinary lifestyle differences. This workbook helps you name the life being proposed and what it would require. It does not make the decision for you.

### Screen 9 — Private journal

1. Did I put anything in "Liveable" because I could not bear for it to be anywhere else? Or in "Incompatible" out of fear rather than evidence?
2. Is the life we would build one that both of us truly want — or one that one of us has quietly agreed to for the sake of the other?

*Pray for the wisdom of Proverbs 24:3 — not for your differences to disappear, but for honesty to see them, humility to sort them truthfully, and discernment to know which house can be built and which cannot.*
