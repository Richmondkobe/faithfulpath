## Audio script and transcript

*(About four minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the transcript” section.)*

You have reached the second check-in in this course.

In the last four lessons, you learned to look carefully at another person.

In Lesson 4, you saw that attraction is not the same as wisdom. In Lesson 5, you looked at character, not just charm. In Lesson 6, you learned about warning signs that Christians sometimes excuse. In Lesson 7, you learned to notice the quiet signs of healthy character.

This check-in has one simple purpose: to help you separate what you genuinely know from what you have hoped, assumed, or not yet had time to see.

Here is why this matters. What we believe about someone often comes from four places.

First, there is what the person told you—their account of their faith, past, family, circumstances, and plans. Honest sharing matters. What someone tells you is real information, and healthy relationships require trust. But important claims may still need time, context, or appropriate confirmation, especially when they affect your safety, freedom, finances, or future.

Second, there is what you hoped or assumed. When we do not know something, our minds naturally fill in the gaps. That is normal. It becomes a problem when we begin treating a hope or assumption as an established fact.

Third, there is what other people told you. Friends, family members, pastors, or church members may know something you do not. Their observations may be helpful, but ask sensible questions. Did they see it themselves? Are they sharing evidence or an opinion? Could loyalty, prejudice, jealousy, family pressure, or a desire for control be affecting their view?

Fourth, there is what you have observed yourself—what you personally saw or heard and the circumstances in which it happened. Repeated behaviour in different settings can provide useful evidence. But even direct observation needs humility. You may not know the whole context, and one moment does not always reveal a pattern.

These four sources can all be useful, but they are not the same.

Let us make this simple. When you believe something important about a person, ask:

How do I know this?

Did they tell me? Did someone else tell me? Did I assume it? Or have I observed it over time?

Pause for a moment.

Think of one important thing you believe about this person. Where did that belief come from?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

If you discover that you know less than you thought, that is not failure. It is valuable clarity. It means you need more time before making a larger decision.

Remember the other side too. If you have been hurt before, you may watch so carefully for danger that you overlook genuine goodness. Notice healthy patterns as honestly as you notice concerns.

The next module is different. Until now, you have mostly been observing. In Module 3, you will begin having important conversations about boundaries, physical intimacy, family, money, and the past.

Those conversations require a relationship in which you can speak freely.

If saying no brings punishment, if raising a concern leads to intimidation, or if you carefully manage every word to prevent a frightening reaction, better communication is not the solution. Go first to **Find Help Where You Live** and seek individual guidance.

Let us pray.

Lord, thank you for helping me see more clearly. Show me the difference between what I know, what I have been told, and what I have hoped or assumed. Where I have overlooked danger, open my eyes. Where I have overlooked goodness, help me see that too. Give me patience when I need more time, humility when I may be mistaken, and courage when I need to act. In Jesus’ name, amen.

## What still needs clarity?

Something unclear may require:

* more time in ordinary settings;
* a safe and honest conversation;
* appropriate confirmation of an important fact; or
* advice from someone wise and independent.

Appropriate confirmation may be sensible when information affects your safety or freedom to choose—for example, identity, marital status, significant financial obligations, or another major fact about the person’s circumstances.

Do not investigate private accounts, secretly monitor someone, ask others for confidential information, or create tests to provoke a reaction.

Do not begin a difficult conversation if you are afraid of how the person may react.

Time is useful when there is no serious concern. But do not use “I need more time” to postpone responding to something that is already serious. Violence, sexual pressure, stalking, threats, coercive control, and serious deception affecting your safety or freedom matter from the first incident.
