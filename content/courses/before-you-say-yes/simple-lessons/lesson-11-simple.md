## One truth to remember

How someone handles money often reveals more than how much they earn.

## Key Scripture

"If then you have not been faithful in the unrighteous wealth, who will entrust to you the true riches?" — Luke 16:11 (ESV)

This verse comes just after the one we studied in Lesson 5. Jesus was teaching about stewardship, not marriage. But the principle fits: the way we handle money can show whether we are honest, responsible, and trustworthy.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About eight minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 11.

When people think about a future spouse and money, they often ask one question: "How much do they earn?" That question is not useless. A steady income matters when you think about housing, children, or supporting family. But income tells you much less about character than people think.

Two people can earn exactly the same amount and be completely different. One is careful, honest, generous, and content. The other is reckless, secretive, and never satisfied.

Here is why money matters so much. Almost everyone says they value honesty, generosity, and responsibility. Money is where you find out if it is true.

Let me be clear about what this lesson is not about. It is not about how much money someone has. Being poor is not a character flaw. Being rich is not a virtue. A student with loans, someone in a low-paid ministry job, or someone supporting their parents on a small income should not be judged by the amount in their account. The book of James warns Christians not to honour the rich and look down on the poor.

This lesson is also not about checking someone's bank account. While you are dating, financial privacy is normal and healthy. You are not entitled to see their statements or passwords. What you are doing is watching character, and learning the big facts that would affect a shared future.

So what can you notice?

You can notice how they spend. Not how much, but how. Do they understand what they can afford? When money is tight, do they adjust honestly, or hide it and borrow more?

You can notice how they handle debt. Many people have debt, sometimes for education, illness, housing, business, or family needs. Debt alone does not tell you someone's character. Ask whether they understand it, tell the truth about it, and have a realistic plan. If they need help, are they willing to ask for it?

You can notice generosity. The Bible says, "God loves a cheerful giver." Real generosity helps others without trying to buy praise or create an obligation.

And you can notice honesty. Privacy about details is fine. But hiding big facts is not. Big facts include serious debts, children or other dependants, regular support for family, gambling losses, or legal problems.

Now let's talk about money between the two of you. Who pays for dates is different in different cultures. What matters is that nobody keeps score, and nobody uses money as pressure. A gift never buys affection, access, or commitment. And watch how they treat your money. Someone careful with their own money but careless with yours is showing you something.

And please remember this safeguard. While you are dating, do not share bank accounts, do not buy property together, and do not co-sign anyone's loan. If you co-sign, you may become responsible for the whole debt.

So how much do you need to know, and when? Let me make this simple. There are four stages.

First, early dating. Notice attitudes and habits. You mostly see these, rather than ask about them.

Second, before becoming exclusive. You need to know any major fact that affects your safety or whether the relationship is genuine. For example, are they already married? Do they have children or other dependants? Have they been honest about who they are and what they do?

Third, before engagement. You both need to share honestly about debts, dependants, family obligations, income, and legal problems.

Fourth, before marriage. You plan together in detail. Independent legal or financial advice may be wise if there is serious debt, property, a business, inheritance concerns, or a previous marriage.

Now I must warn you about something serious. Some money problems are not just warning signs. They are harm.

Romance scams are one example. If someone you know only online asks you for money, gift cards, cryptocurrency, account access, or help moving money, treat it as a serious warning, whatever their story is. Do not send money while you try to decide whether they are telling the truth. People caught by scams are not foolish. They are often kind, and someone chose to exploit that kindness. Be careful of anyone who directs you to an investment or trading website. And remember, even a video call does not prove every claim someone has made about their identity.

Financial abuse is another. This may include taking your money or cards, pressuring you to sign documents, opening accounts or debts in your name, interfering with your work or education, controlling your access to essentials, or deliberately making it difficult for you to leave.

These are not problems to discuss calmly over dinner. They need outside help.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean you should judge someone for being poor or trust someone because they are wealthy. One person earning less, studying, or freely agreeing to stay home is not financial abuse. The question is whether both people retain honest information, meaningful access, and the freedom to make choices.

And remember, this lesson also looks at you. Do you over-give? Do you find it hard to say no to requests for money? Do you hide your own debt?

## Need support?

If you recognised exploitation rather than a warning sign — a request for money from someone you have never met, your money slowly becoming theirs, or a dependence you did not choose — put the conversation aside and get help. You are not foolish. You offered trust, and someone chose to exploit it.

**If you have already sent money or shared account details, first think about your safety.** Does this person have physical access to you, your phone, or your money? If this may be part of control, being watched, or a relationship where you fear their reaction, use a safer device and get confidential specialist advice **before** making changes they might notice.

**If there is no immediate safety concern,** stop any further payments. Contact your bank, card provider, transfer service, gift-card company, or cryptocurrency platform quickly and ask whether the payment can be stopped or traced. Getting money back is not guaranteed. Keep copies of messages and receipts somewhere safe, report the account to the platform, and report the fraud through the right service in your country. Change passwords from a safe device. Be careful of anyone who promises to recover your money for another payment.

**If money control is part of a wider pattern of control,** suddenly freezing accounts, moving money, or confronting the person may increase the danger. Get confidential domestic-abuse advice alongside financial advice first. **Find help where you live →**

## Part 1 — My own money (private)

### Screen 1 — Me and money

1. What do I believe money is for?
2. How do I usually spend, save, and plan — and how does that change when I am stressed?
3. What debt do I have, why, and what is my plan?
4. What do I give, to whom, and how do I feel about it?
5. What do I owe my family now, or expect to owe later?

## Part 2 — What I have seen (private)

*For each, choose* ***Seen / Not seen yet / Mixed / Concern****, with a short example.*

### Screen 2 — Spending and debt

1. They understand what they can afford, and adjust honestly when money is tight.
2. What they spend on over time matches what they say they value.
3. I know the honest outline of any big debt — what kind, why, and the plan — as much as fits our stage.
4. They are honest about debt instead of hiding it or making it sound smaller.

### Screen 3 — Generosity and honesty

1. They are generous within their means, without using it to win praise, obligation, or control.
2. They can say no to requests, including from family or church, when that is wise.
3. They can talk about money without avoiding it, as much as fits our stage.
4. What they tell me about money has stayed consistent.
5. They have not pretended to be richer or poorer than they are.

### Screen 4 — Ambition and responsibility

1. Their money goals fit their values, and do not depend on dishonesty or reckless risk.
2. They are not drawn to "fast money" — get-rich schemes, chasing losses, or investments they cannot explain.
3. I have some sense of what they would, and would not, sacrifice for money.
4. They take debts and responsibilities seriously, and speak honestly when problems come up.
5. They plan where they can, and ask for help when things are beyond them.

*Be fair. Late payments can come from poverty, delayed wages, or illness, not bad character. The question is whether they are honest and responsible about it.*

## Part 3 — Warning signs (private)

*Choose* ***Seen / Not seen / Not sure****.*

### Screen 5 — Warning signs (part 1)

1. Borrowing again and again, with entitlement, lies, or broken promises.
2. Pressure on me to lend money, co-sign, or guarantee a loan.
3. Hidden gambling, chasing losses, or gambling with money needed for essentials.
4. Debts or obligations I discovered rather than was told about.

### Screen 6 — Warning signs (part 2)

1. Spending that keeps contradicting what they say they value.
2. Gifts or money used to create pressure or control.
3. Treating my money as theirs, or my money decisions as theirs to approve.
4. Refusing to talk about money at all, when our relationship is serious enough that we should.

> One serious thing — pressure to co-sign, hidden gambling debt, or trying to misuse your identity — may need action and advice straight away. Several smaller concerns may show a pattern and a reason to slow down (Lesson 18). If raising a concern could lead to threats, revenge, or more financial harm, get individual advice instead of confronting them.

## Part 4 — Exploitation

*Answer Yes or No. Your answers on Screens 7 and 8 are never saved.*

### Screen 7 — Scam warning signs

> **If any answer is Yes, do not send money. Get advice before doing anything else.**

1. A romantic interest I know only online has asked me for money, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or access to my accounts.
2. They have urged me to use an investment website or adviser they chose.
3. The opportunity involves urgency, secrecy, or "guaranteed" profits.
4. I cannot independently check who this person, company, or investment really is.
5. They become angry, extra romantic, or threatening when I hesitate.

### Screen 8 — Financial abuse

1. They have taken or used my money, cards, or documents without my free agreement.
2. I have been pressured to sign financial documents, or accounts or debts have been opened in my name.
3. My access to my own income, essentials, work, or education is controlled or interfered with.
4. They hold or control my identity documents.
5. I have become financially dependent on them in ways I did not choose, and that would make leaving hard.

> **If any answer is Yes, get confidential individual advice.** Do not move or freeze money, change access, or confront the person until you have thought about whether that could increase danger. **Find help where you live →**

## Part 5 — Money between us (private)

### Screen 9 — Money and us

1. Who pays for what? Does it create obligation or score-keeping?
2. How do they treat my money? How do I treat theirs?
3. Have gifts, in either direction, created pressure or obligation?
4. Is there any lending, co-signing, or shared spending between us that should not exist yet?

### Screen 10 — Looking ahead to engagement

*Nothing to fill in. This is so you know what is coming.*

Before engagement, you will both need to share honestly about debts and plans, family obligations, income and how steady it is, legal or business problems, and what you each expect about earning, managing, giving, and supporting.

Checking important facts belongs at that stage too — but it must be **mutual**, not one person auditing the other. It does not mean sharing passwords or full account access. It may mean looking at relevant documents together, or getting independent advice, with both people given the same opportunity.

### Screen 11 — Private journal

1. Is there something about my money I would find hard to tell a future spouse? What makes it hard — shame, fear of judgement, or something I have not dealt with yet?
2. Do I use money — giving, paying, lending — to win affection or approval? Where did that start?

*Pray Proverbs 30:8–9 for yourself and for this person — that neither of you would love money, fear its absence, or be controlled by it, and that whatever you might one day share would be handled faithfully, in little and in much.*
