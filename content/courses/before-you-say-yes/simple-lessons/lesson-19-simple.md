## Today's question

If it is time to end this relationship, how do I do it honestly, kindly, and safely?

## One truth to remember

A freely given no is enough. You do not have to prove a case to end a dating relationship.

## Key Scripture

"The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit." — Psalm 34:18 (ESV)

David wrote this about God's people in trouble, not about dating. But the promise belongs to all who are hurting. Ending something that had to end is still a loss, and God does not ask you to pretend otherwise.

## Two very different endings

|  | **An ordinary ending** | **An ending where you are afraid** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What it is**                                             | Two people of goodwill. The relationship is simply not right. | The other person's reaction may itself be dangerous.                                  |

## Audio script and transcript

*(About twelve minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the full transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 19.

Before anything else, I need to ask you one question. Are you afraid of how this person will react if you end the relationship?

If the answer is yes, or if you are not sure, please listen carefully. The ordinary advice about ending a relationship is not for you, and following it could put you at risk. Please contact a specialist service before making plans or telling the person. If you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services or go somewhere safer now. You can also go straight to Find Help Where You Live. I will talk about your situation again later in this lesson.

Now, let's begin.

The third honest answer to "Should we continue?" is no. This lesson is about giving that answer well.

Many people know a relationship should end, sometimes for months or years, but they stay. Ending a relationship is one of the hardest ordinary things a person does. And Christians are often given very little help with it. We are taught how to start relationships and how to keep marriages strong. We are rarely taught how to end something well.

First, let me say this clearly. You do not need certainty. You do not need a villain. You do not need the other person's agreement, your family's permission, or a special word from God. Discernment is allowed to end in no. A freely given no is enough. You do not have to prove a case.

And the size of the decision should match the relationship. If you have been on three dates, you do not need a worksheet to decide it is not for you. You never owe a dating relationship a long repair programme. And you never owe an unsafe relationship more time.

How do you know it is time? Perhaps you want different lives, and time has not changed that. Perhaps a problem was named, time was given, and the change did not come. Perhaps help was refused. Perhaps something essential is now missing, like your shared faith or your freedom to say no. Or perhaps there is harm. If there is harm, you do not need to have tried everything first. You need to be safe.

Be honest about what an ending is not. It is not a threat to make someone change. It is not a test to see if they will fight for you. If you are using it that way, stop and say what you really want. But if what you want is for the relationship to be over, that is reason enough.

Now let's talk about an ordinary ending — two people of goodwill, where you are not afraid.

Let me make this simple. Here are five things to do.

First, be clear. Do not say, "I need some space," or "Let's take a break." If you have decided, say so plainly: "I have decided to end our relationship. I am not asking you to change my mind." It may feel harsh. But it is kinder than leaving someone to spend weeks trying to reopen a door you have already closed.

Second, do it directly, where it is safe. If the relationship was serious, a face-to-face conversation is usually most respectful. Choose a neutral place, with some privacy but other people nearby, and your own way home. Not at church, not at a family event, and not in front of friends. If the relationship was short or mostly online, a phone call or a clear message is fine. And do not just go quiet and disappear. Slowly fading away forces the other person to do the ending for you.

Third, give a brief, true reason, without an attack. One or two honest sentences. Then stop. Do not soften it until it is no longer true, like "It's not you, it's me." And do not turn it into a long list of their faults. A reason is not a court case.

Fourth, do not negotiate. They may offer promises, counselling, or a different pace. If you had wanted those, Lessons 17 and 18 were the place for them. And do not offer an immediate friendship to soften the blow. Friendship may be possible later, but not as a consolation prize.

Fifth, step back. Tell them clearly what contact you want now. Return anything that needs returning. Do not message a week later to check how they are. That is for their friends and family. Their grief is real, but it is not proof that you were wrong. You do not have to be the one who helps them through it.

One more thing. Do not blame God for your decision. You may have prayed and sought advice. That is good. But saying "God told me to end it" or "I don't have peace" can be a way of avoiding your real reason. And it makes it impossible for the other person to respond. Just say, "I have decided," and own it.

What if they will not accept it? They keep calling. They turn up. They say God showed them you belong together. They get family or a pastor involved. You have said no. You do not owe them more conversations. Repeat your decision once, in the same words, and then stop responding. If contact becomes frightening or constant, get specialist or legal advice.

If they threaten to harm themselves, take it seriously and contact a crisis or emergency service. But do not go to them alone, do not promise to keep it secret, and do not take back your decision. Whether the threat comes from real distress, an attempt to pressure you, or both, it needs professional help. You are not responsible for keeping someone alive by staying with them.

Now, if you are afraid.

Please hear this very clearly. The time around separation can be especially dangerous with a controlling or violent partner. So the rules are different.

Do not arrange a private meeting to end it. Wanting to do it face to face is a good instinct in an ordinary ending, but it can be dangerous here. A phone call or a message is not cowardice. It may be the safest way.

Do not announce your plans before getting safety advice. For example, do not say, "We need to talk this weekend." A controlling person may use that warning to increase pressure or control.

Contact a specialist service first. They can help you plan what to do, and in what order. That is what they are there for, and it is not overreacting.

If you think your phone or accounts are being watched, do not suddenly delete apps or change passwords on that device. It may alert the person. Use a safer device to contact a specialist, and let them help you decide what to do.

If your family or community is the pressure, do not use family meetings or joint church counselling. Contact a service that specialises in forced marriage or family pressure first.

And if you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services now, or go somewhere safer first. Planning must never delay urgent protection.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean ending a relationship should not hurt. Whatever kind of ending it is, there will be grief. And grief is allowed. Grief is the cost of having cared. It is not evidence that you should have stayed.

If you were the one told no, receive it as final. Grieve it as a real loss. And if it has woken a fear that no one else will come, Lesson 20 was written for you.

And if the ending was an unsafe one, be gentle with yourself about how long it took. People stay for reasons that make sense from the inside: hope, love, fear, faith, children, money, or shame. Leaving is often a process, not a single moment. Every step you have taken towards safety matters.

Pause for a moment.

If you already know it is time, how long have you known? And what has kept you from acting?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

You do not need to act today. But your answer matters.

Here is your one step for today. If you are afraid, your next step is not a conversation. It is contacting a specialist service. If you are not afraid and you have decided, write two sentences: your decision, and your reason. Read them aloud until you can say them without softening them.

Let's pray.

Lord, I have been carrying this for a long time, and I am tired. Give me courage to be honest and show me the safest way to take the next step. Keep me from taking back a wise decision simply because it is hard. I trust the other person to you, without making myself responsible for their choices. And when it is done and the room is quiet, be near. You have promised to be near the brokenhearted, and I will need you. In Jesus' name, amen.

Unless you are in immediate danger, you do not need to decide or act today. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services or go somewhere safer now. Otherwise, you can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

## Need support?

If you are afraid of what will happen when you end this, a specialist abuse service is the right first call — **before** a conversation, before a pastor, and before any change to your accounts or devices. They will help you plan the timing, and timing is what people most often get wrong alone.

> **Digital safety.** If you think your phone, computer, or accounts are being watched, do not immediately remove apps, turn off access, or change settings on that device. Sudden changes may alert the person and may destroy evidence you will want later. Use a safer device — one they have never used — to contact a specialist, and let them help you decide what to keep, what to change, and when.

**Keep a record.** If there have been threats, unwanted contact, or frightening incidents, write them down with dates. Do not keep the only copy on a device or account the other person can access. Ask an advocate how to keep evidence safely. Do not copy, forward, or keep illegal or intimate material as "evidence."

**If you live together, share money, or there is a child or a pregnancy,** you will also need legal advice from a qualified adviser. But safety comes first, and paperwork second.

**If forced marriage or family pressure is involved,** contact a specialist service and do not raise your plans at home. Do not travel abroad at a family's request while this is unresolved. In the UK, contact the Forced Marriage Unit on +44 (0)20 7008 0151 or at fmu@fcdo.gov.uk. Check the current contact details and availability on GOV.UK. You can also contact a 24-hour domestic-abuse service or, if there is immediate risk, the police or emergency services.

**Afterwards,** follow the contact plan you made with the specialist. That may mean no contact at all, or contact only through an advocate or lawyer. Do not respond to apologies, gifts, or spiritual appeals. If the person keeps contacting you, follows you, or turns up, get specialist or legal advice quickly. You do not have to wait for things to get worse before asking for help.

**If the grief after an ending is more than you can carry** — if you cannot function, or the loss has taken you somewhere dark — please talk to a qualified professional now, rather than waiting to see if it lifts.

If danger is immediate, contact the emergency service where you are. **Find help where you live →**

### Screen 4 — What may be influencing me

*Think about which of these are true. Bring them into the light, so that fear, guilt, or pressure does not quietly decide for you.*

**Am I staying because…**

- ending it would mean a conversation I do not want to have?
- of what it has already cost me?
- they have promised to change?
- I am afraid there will be no one else? *(Lesson 20)*
- I do not want to disappoint my family, church, or friends?
- I would feel guilty?
- I believe God brought us together, so ending it would be disobedience? *(Lesson 13)*

**Or am I ending it…**

- to see whether they will fight for me?
- because I want to be talked out of it?
- as a threat, to make them change?

*None of these has to be solved before you decide. But if you are using an ending as a test or a threat, stop and say what you really want. You are still free to end a dating relationship without perfect reasons.*

### Screen 6 — Preparing an ordinary ending

*Only for an ordinary ending where you are not afraid. Think these through, or write them on paper you keep safely. There are no answer boxes here.*

- How will I tell them — in person, by phone, or in writing — and why does that fit this relationship?
- Where and when? *(Somewhere neutral, with other people nearby, a clear way to leave, and my own transport.)*
- Which one or two people will I tell beforehand, for support?
- My decision, in words that cannot be heard as a question.
- My reason, in one or two true sentences, without an attack.

### Screen 7 — What I will and will not do

*Think these through. There are no answer boxes.*

**I will not:** negotiate · offer an immediate friendship · give a reason that is not true · say God made the decision instead of owning it · let others know before I have told them.

**I will think about:**

- What I will say to others, keeping both truth and appropriate privacy.
- What contact I want afterwards, including any practical contact that cannot be avoided.
- What needs to be returned.
- Who I will talk to afterwards, and what I want to learn.

## Part 4 — If you are afraid

### Screen 8 — Ending safely

> **Do not write or save your safety plan in this course.** The list below shows what a specialist may help you think about. It is not something to fill in here. A real safety plan may contain information that would be dangerous if discovered. Make it and keep it only in the way your specialist advises.

A specialist will usually help you think through:

- Which service to contact, and their advice about timing.
- If you are in immediate danger: the emergency service or safer place to go first.
- At least one trustworthy person or advocate who knows what you are doing, and when.
- The safest place you can go.
- How and when to secure documents, medication, money, and essentials — without making changes the person might notice too early.
- How you will tell them — **not** in a private meeting.
- What you will say, briefly.
- A digital-safety plan: a safer device, what to keep as evidence, when to change passwords, location sharing, and shared accounts.
- A record of threats or incidents, kept where they cannot find it.
- If they threaten self-harm: which crisis or emergency service to contact.
- If family or community pressure is involved: which specialist service to contact, and what to say at home, and when.
- If you live together, share money, or there is a child or pregnancy: legal advice, once safety is secured.
- Afterwards: what contact to allow, who to ask not to pass on information, where to report continued contact, and who will support you.

### Screen 9 — Afterwards

*Read this whenever you need it.*

Whatever kind of ending it was, grief will follow, and grief is allowed. **Grief is the cost of having cared, not proof that you should have stayed.**

Do not rush to fill the space with someone new. The weeks after an ending are when Lesson 1's question — why do I want a relationship? — is easiest to answer badly.

Take what happened back to the wise people who walked with you. Not to be told you were right, but to learn.

And if the ending was an unsafe one, be gentle with yourself about how long it took. Leaving is often a process, and every step towards safety matters.

*Ending a relationship, even one that had to end, is a loss. You do not have to pretend otherwise before God. If it is helpful and safe, you may pray for the other person from a distance, without contacting them. Then pray for yourself. You have done a hard thing. Let God be near.*
