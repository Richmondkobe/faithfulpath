## Audio script and transcript

*(About five minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the full transcript” section.)*

You have reached the third check-in in this course.

In Module 2, you mostly watched and noticed. In Module 3, you began to take part. You thought about boundaries and physical limits. You looked at family expectations, money, and the past. You may have spoken about some of these things with the person you are dating.

That gives you a different kind of knowledge. Watching someone shows you what they do. Speaking honestly shows you what happens when your needs, limits, and story become part of the relationship—especially when they are different from what the other person wants.

This check-in is not a test. Nothing is scored, and nothing here will make a decision for you.

Here is the most important question in this module: What happened when you spoke up?

Perhaps you said no to something. You named a limit. You asked a difficult question. You shared an expectation or something important from your past. Now think about how the other person responded.

Do not only ask whether you got your way. A healthy person may disagree with you, refuse a request, or realise that your limits and theirs do not fit together. Disagreement is not automatically disrespect.

Instead, ask: Were you free to speak honestly?

Were you listened to? Could you disagree without being frightened, punished, or pressured? Could the other person ask questions without shaming you?

If saying no brought repeated pressure, punishment, threats, spiritual arguments, or sulking used to make you give in, that is more than an ordinary disagreement. If you have started staying quiet because speaking honestly feels unsafe, notice that too.

Now think about patterns.

One event may be a misunderstanding or a poor response that the person recognises and changes. But sometimes the same attitude appears in different parts of the relationship.

A boundary is pushed. A family expectation is used to silence you. Your money is treated as if it belongs to them. Something you shared privately is later used against you.

These may be separate events. Or they may reveal one larger pattern: your voice matters only when you agree.

Look at what happens afterwards too. When the person realises they have hurt you, do they listen? Do they take responsibility without blaming you? Do they make a real change that lasts? An apology matters, but repeated behaviour tells you more.

Some things, however, are serious even once. Violence, sexual pressure, stalking, threats, misuse of your identity or money, and serious deception affecting your safety or freedom to choose do not need to happen again before they matter.

Remember to notice healthy behaviour too. Respect, patience, honest disagreement, and changed behaviour are real, and they matter.

But healthy moments do not cancel harm. Green flags are not points that make up for danger.

Pause for a moment.

When you spoke honestly in this relationship, did you feel free—or did you feel that you had to be careful?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

Whatever your answer, it is important information.

The next module is about making wise decisions. You will look at what to do when someone says, “God told me,” whose advice to trust, whether your lives can fit together, and whether two sincere Christians can still be wrong for each other.

If this check-in shows you that you know less than you thought, that is useful—not a failure. You may simply need more ordinary time and honest conversation.

But if the conversation you need to have frightens you, do not force yourself to have it. Fear of the other person’s reaction is already important information. Please go to Find Help Where You Live before confronting them.

Let us pray.

Lord, thank you for giving me a voice. Help me see clearly what happens when I use it. Where I have been heard and respected, help me to be thankful. Where I have been pressured, punished, or silenced, give me wisdom and courage to seek help. Keep me from ignoring danger, and keep me from overlooking what is healthy and good. Lead me as I make wise decisions. In Jesus’ name, amen.

## What is still unknown?

Some questions may still have no answer. Think about why:

* **We have not reached that stage yet.**
  That is normal. Wait for the right time.

* **It is part of their private story.**
  Respect their privacy. But you do not have to move towards commitment while an important question remains unanswered.

* **Asking would not feel safe.**
  Do not force the conversation. This is serious. **Find help where you live →**

* **I avoided asking because I did not want the answer.**
  Be honest with yourself. If it is safe to ask, what support would help you begin the conversation?

Checking important facts must be lawful, fair, mutual, and suitable for your stage. It does not mean secretly accessing someone’s accounts, contacting former partners or relatives behind their back, or trying to obtain confidential records.

If a serious safeguarding or legal concern may require a lawful check, get qualified advice and follow the proper process. Do not set up secret tests, and do not start a conversation if you fear the person’s reaction.
