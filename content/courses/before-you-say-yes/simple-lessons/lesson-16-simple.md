# Lesson 16 — Can Two Good Christians Be Wrong for Each Other?

## Today's question

What if we are both good people, and it still may not be right?

## One truth to remember

A person's goodness is a reason to respect them, but it is not, by itself, a reason to marry them.

## Key Scripture

"Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts!" — Psalm 139:23 (ESV)

David asks God to examine him, not to approve him. That is the attitude this lesson needs. The prayer is not "Show me I'm right" or "Show me I'm wrong." It is "Look at me honestly, because you already know."

## Audio script and transcript

*(About ten minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the full transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 16.

Most of this course has helped you recognise problems: wrong motives, poor character, and harmful patterns. But some relationships have no obvious wrongdoing or serious warning signs.

Two sincere believers. Good character on both sides. No known safety concerns. Families who approve. A church that is already imagining the wedding. And yet, the relationship may still not be the right fit—not because either person is bad, but because goodness alone does not make two lives compatible.

Few people prepare you for this. When someone has done something wrong, the reason to end a relationship may be easier to name. When nobody has done anything wrong, even the reason can feel difficult to explain. Some people stay mainly because they cannot find anyone to blame. But serious incompatibility that is ignored before marriage does not disappear after the wedding.

Let me be clear about one thing. "Wrong for each other" does not mean God made one perfect match for you, and this person is not it. We set that idea aside in Lesson 13. It simply means this relationship, as it really is, cannot support a marriage that both people can enter freely and faithfully.

Here is the hardest thing to hear. A person's goodness is not, by itself, a reason to marry them. Goodness is a reason to respect them, to be glad you know them, and to wish them well. Good character is essential in marriage. We spent three lessons on it. But there are many good people in the world, and you will not marry most of them. The fact that this good person is in front of you, wants you, and has been kind to you does not create an obligation.

You cannot marry someone out of gratitude, or pity, or because saying no would hurt someone who does not deserve to be hurt. A marriage built on those reasons will not honour their goodness. Over time, it will wear it down.

The Bible sets boundaries for marriage, including Paul's instruction to marry "only in the Lord." In that same verse, Paul says a widow is free to marry "whom she wishes." This does not make every choice equally wise, but it does show that personal willingness matters. Your own desire—or lack of it—is not automatically a spiritual weakness. It is part of what you are allowed to weigh.

So what might it look like when two good people are wrong for each other? Let me make this simple. Here are five signs. None of them is a verdict on its own.

First, you want different lives. One of you needs a calling, a country, or a family shape that the other cannot honestly give. This is Lesson 15's "Incompatible" group.

Second, the same person is nearly always the one giving way. All couples adapt to each other. But if one person repeatedly gives up convictions, healthy friendships, or important parts of their life while the other rarely adjusts, notice that.

Third, over time, the relationship itself leaves you regularly drained, guarded, or unable to recover. Do not judge this from one difficult week. Work, illness, grief, or family pressure can make any relationship feel heavy. Ask where the tiredness is actually coming from.

Fourth, you are less yourself. Some part of you has gone quiet. Ask honestly: Is this healthy growth, or am I slowly disappearing?

Fifth, a doubt that honest time has not answered. You have prayed, observed the relationship in ordinary life, and tried to understand the concern, but the hesitation remains. A feeling is not automatically God's warning. But neither should you dismiss it simply because the other person is good. Serious premarital doubt should be explored and understood, not shamed or argued away.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean every doubt is a sign to leave. Every serious relationship has moments of "What if I'm wrong?" That is the weight of a big decision, not a verdict. It does not mean you should leave whenever a relationship starts to require real commitment. If you have left good relationships at that point more than once, look at that pattern first. And it does not mean waiting for a perfect match. Nobody will fit you perfectly in every area.

Some relationships simply need work. If there is a specific problem that you both name, you are both willing to work on, and your honest effort is producing change, that is worth the work. Lesson 17 is about relationships like that.

Now, here is something important. Dating is not marriage. Before the vows, you are two people discerning. And discernment is allowed to end in no. That is what it is for.

A clear, kind no may be painful. But causing pain by telling the truth is not the same as being cruel. Continuing towards marriage mainly because you are afraid to disappoint someone can create deeper harm for both of you later.

What if they are sure it is right, and you are not? You are not obliged to agree. Their certainty is theirs. Your no does not need a competing spiritual message, and you do not need to prove that they are a bad person. A freely given no is enough. Once you have truly decided, do not say "not now" merely to soften a final no. False hope can make the pain last longer. If you fear their reaction, seek individual specialist advice and make a safety plan first. You do not have to end the relationship in private or in person.

What if you are sure it is right, and they are not? This is the harder side, and many people are on it. They are not rejecting God. They are using the freedom this lesson describes. Please do not argue that their no is disobedience. Accept their decision, unless they clearly choose to reopen it themselves. Do not stay in contact secretly hoping to change their mind. Grieve it. It is a real loss. But do not turn it into a spiritual contest.

Two good people, and one says no. That is allowed. It hurts. And God's purposes for you are not smaller because of it.

Pause for a moment.

If you were not afraid of hurting them, what would you say is true?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

You do not need to act on your answer today. But it is worth knowing.

Here is your one step for today. Unless your safety requires immediate action, do not force yourself to make a big decision while you are emotionally stirred by this lesson. Write down one thing you believe is true about the relationship, and take it to one wise person who will not push you towards either answer.

Let's pray.

Lord, I keep looking for someone to blame, and there is no one. They are good, and I am not sure this can be built. Take away the idea that loving you means ignoring what I can plainly see. If I have reached a decision, give me courage to say it honestly and safely. Hold them, whatever I decide. And if I am wrong about all of this, show me that instead. In Jesus' name, amen.

That is enough for today. You can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

## Need support?

This lesson describes a particular kind of grief: grief with no one to blame. If you recognised your relationship here, you are not ungrateful, disloyal, or faithless. You are seeing clearly, which is what this course has asked of you all along.

This kind of grief is worth sharing with someone, especially if it is seriously affecting your sleep, work, studies, or daily life. A qualified counsellor may help, and so may a wise friend who will not rush you towards either decision.

If you have been told no, and it has woken a fear that no one else will ever come, Lesson 20 speaks to that directly. You do not have to wait until you reach it.

## Part 1 — What I am seeing

*For each sign, choose **True / Partly true / Not true**, then answer the questions.*

### Screen 1 — Different lives, and who adapts

1. **Different lives:** We want lives that would require one of us to live a life that is not truly ours.
2. Is this about a central conviction, an unavoidable responsibility, or a settled life direction — or a preference we have not really explored?
3. **Who adapts:** What decisions, friendships, or parts of who I am (or they are) keep being given up?
4. Is this freely chosen, caused by circumstances, or expected?
5. What happens when the one who adapts asks for change?

### Screen 2 — Drained, and lasting differences

1. **Drained:** Over months, the relationship itself has left me drained, guarded, or unable to recover.
2. What outside pressures might be affecting both of us?
3. **Lasting differences:** What exact beliefs, needs, or future plans are in conflict?
4. Is there an arrangement we could both freely accept? What would each of us have to give up, and could we do it without lasting resentment?

### Screen 3 — Less myself

1. **Less myself:** Which part of me has become quieter?
2. Did they ask for that?
3. Is this healthy growth, ordinary adjustment, fear, or slowly disappearing?
4. Can I safely express that part of me, and what happens when I do?

### Screen 4 — Counsel and lasting doubt

1. **Counsel:** Have independent, wise people named the same specific concern? What is it?
2. **Lasting doubt:** I have given honest time in ordinary life, and a hesitation remains.
3. What is the feeling attached to? What evidence supports it?
4. Does it change when specific concerns are addressed?

## Part 2 — Other explanations

### Screen 5 — Could it be something else?

For each concern above:

1. How long has it been there, and in what settings?
2. Could it be ordinary doubt, a habit of leaving when commitment grows, perfectionism, outside stress, or a personal struggle of my own?
3. Have I talked about it? What happened when I did?
4. Does the same pattern show up in my other close relationships?

## Part 3 — What we have tried

### Screen 6 — Something specific we tried

1. The concern we named, and the change we agreed to try:
2. How long did we try it?
3. What changed, and what stayed the same?
4. Was the effort shared by both of us?
5. Did it require either of us to go against our conscience?

> **If nothing specific has been tried,** ask whether you both freely want to try something, and whether that would be safe. Trying something may help clarify a problem that can be worked on. **But it is not required before either person may say no.** Do not use an "experiment" to delay a decision already made, or to test an unsafe relationship.

## Part 4 — What I believe

*Mark each **I believe this** or **I struggle to believe this**.*

### Screen 7 — Freedom to decide (part 1)

1. Dating is not marriage, and discernment is allowed to end in no.
2. Respecting someone's goodness does not require a romantic commitment.
3. Ending a relationship does not require proving that either person is wrong.
4. I cannot marry someone out of gratitude, pity, or to avoid hurting them.

### Screen 8 — Freedom to decide (part 2)

1. I am allowed to say no, and I am responsible for saying it honestly.
2. They are allowed to say no, even without an explanation I find good enough.
3. Neither of us should use God, advice, or past sacrifices to overturn the other's decision.

*Take anything you struggle to believe to a wise pastor, mentor, or qualified counsellor who will not push you towards either answer.*
