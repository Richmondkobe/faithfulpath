# Lesson 14 — Whose Advice Should You Trust?

## One truth to remember

A wise voice is worth hearing, but a voice is not a vote, and it is not a veto.

## Key Scripture

"Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed." — Proverbs 15:22 (ESV)

Proverbs praises good advice many times. Scripture also gives examples of advice that was foolish, biased, or self-serving. The value is not simply in how many people you ask, but in how wise, honest, and well-informed they are.

## What makes a good adviser

- **Wise** — humble, with sound judgement, and honest about what they do not know.
- **Honest** — willing to tell you what you do not want to hear.
- **Independent enough** — able to separate their own interests from your decision.
- **Well-informed** — they know you and, ideally, have seen something of the relationship for themselves.
- **Discreet** — careful with private information.
- **Safe** — they know the difference between ordinary disagreement and control, and they refer you to specialists when needed.

Welcome to Lesson 14.

There are two common mistakes when it comes to getting advice about a relationship.

The first mistake is telling no one. The relationship feels private, or new, or complicated. Or maybe you already suspect what people would say. So you make one of the biggest decisions of your life alone, inside the relationship.

The second mistake is telling everyone. Parents, friends, your small group, the person next to you at church, and hundreds of people online. You collect many opinions, but you do not weigh them. The advice conflicts. The relationship becomes a public event. And you end up defending a decision you have not even made yet.

So why do you need other voices at all?

Here is why. You cannot fully see your own relationship from the inside, just as you cannot see your own face without a mirror. Attraction, fear, and hope can all affect what you notice. Other people may see what you are missing.

The Bible says, "The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to advice." That is not an insult. It simply means that everyone's view is limited, even a good one.

And advice is not only for warnings. A friend who says, "I've never seen you this settled and happy," is also giving you useful information. If you only look for people who will find fault, that is not wisdom either.

So who should you listen to? Let me make this simple. Build a small circle—perhaps three or four people—rather than asking everyone. Try to include these four kinds of voices.

First, someone who knows your history. This might be a safe parent, a sibling, or an old friend. They may notice patterns you have repeated before.

Second, someone who sees your life now. A friend who spends regular time with you can tell you what has changed since this relationship began.

Third, someone with mature Christian judgement. This could be a wise pastor, mentor, or mature Christian whose life shows humility and good judgement.

Fourth, when needed, a qualified professional. A counsellor, a financial adviser, or a specialist service, especially if safety is in question.

One person may fill more than one role. The lesson page lists the qualities to look for: wise, honest, independent, knowing, discreet, and safe.

Be careful with each kind of voice. Family members may know you well, but some families are controlling or prejudiced. Being family does not automatically make someone wise. Pastors can offer valuable biblical wisdom, but they are not automatically experts in abuse, addiction, money, or the law. And a pastor has no authority to make you marry anyone. Friends see you often, but some are so loyal they will not challenge you.

The Bible tells a sad story about a king named Rehoboam. Experienced advisers urged him to answer the people gently. He rejected their counsel and chose the harsher advice of the young men who had grown up with him. His decision helped divide the kingdom. Asking only people who support what you already want—and calling that advice—is a very old mistake.

So how do you ask for advice well? Ask before your decision feels fixed. Advice requested only after you have decided can easily become a search for approval. Ask specific questions, such as, "What have you actually seen?" or "Is your concern about safety, character, practical fit, or personal preference?" Do not tell only the good parts of the story. If you find yourself leaving things out so that people will approve, you have already learned something.

But also protect the other person's privacy. What they told you in confidence is not yours to share. You can talk about your own experience without sharing their private details. But if someone may be in danger, do not promise to keep it secret. Get specialist advice.

How do you weigh what you hear? Something a person has actually seen usually carries more weight than a vague feeling. "He stops speaking to you whenever you disagree, and I have seen it happen several times" is more useful than, "I just don't have a good feeling." A vague concern can still be worth exploring, but ask what it is based on.

Also ask whether the voices are truly independent. Three friends who talked it over together are really one voice, not three. But when several wise people who have not compared notes name the same specific concern, take it very seriously.

And never outvote a safety concern. If one trustworthy person says they fear for your safety, do not ignore it just because others are relaxed. Get specialist advice.

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean other people should make your decision. Advice can inform you, warn you, and encourage you. But it cannot decide for you, and it cannot consent for you. In the end, you must weigh what you hear and make your own decision before God.

It also does not mean everyone deserves a voice. Some things are never real counsel: posting relationship arguments online, asking followers to judge your partner, or sharing private messages.

And if you notice that you are avoiding certain people, ask why. Is it because they are unsafe, controlling, prejudiced, or unkind? Or is it because you suspect they might name a concern you do not want to hear? If it is the second, consider speaking with one safe, fair person who will be honest with you.

## Need support?

If the person you are dating asks for reasonable privacy, you can discuss together what should remain private. But if they repeatedly **discourage** you from seeking any wise advice or try to cut you off from trusted people, notice the pattern. If they **forbid or punish** you for seeking advice, that may be control, and the right first step is individual help—not another conversation with them.

If a **church leader** is pressuring your decision, seek advice from outside that leader's influence — such as an independent safeguarding professional or a qualified counsellor not connected to the church. Someone more senior in the same structure is not automatically independent.

If **forced marriage or family pressure** may be involved, do not arrange a family meeting. If **violence, stalking, or immediate danger** is present, follow the safety guidance in Lesson 6.

## Part 1 — My circle

### Screen 1 — Each person who has a voice

*Repeat this screen for each person.*

1. Initials and role (parent, pastor, friend, mentor, professional):
2. What have they seen for themselves, and what do they only know from me?
3. Could they have a personal interest or bias?
4. What question do I want to ask this person?
5. Would they tell me something I did not want to hear?

### Screen 2 — Gaps in my circle

1. Which of these people are wise, honest, independent, well-informed, discreet, and safe? Where does anyone fall short?
2. Do I have someone who knows my history, someone who sees my life now, someone with mature Christian judgement, and — if needed — a qualified professional?
3. Have I only chosen people who will approve — or only people who will not?
4. Is anyone missing who should be here? Why?

## Part 2 — What I have heard

### Screen 3 — What was actually said

*Repeat for each concern or encouragement.*

1. Who said it (initials):
2. What did they actually see, and what did they only hear from me?
3. What conclusion did they draw? What might change their mind?
4. Could prejudice, control, or their own interests be shaping it?
5. Is it about safety, or about preference?

### Screen 4 — Weighing it

1. Do independent voices agree on a specific concern? What is it?
2. Do they agree on any encouragement?
3. Could their agreement come from a shared culture, prejudice, or interest, rather than accuracy?
4. Where one person disagrees with everyone else, do they have specific evidence, relevant knowledge, and no personal interest?
5. Have I left out or softened anything when asking for advice?

## Part 3 — Too many or too few

### Screen 5 — Getting the balance right

1. How many people know about this relationship? How many have I actually asked for advice?
2. Is the relationship being talked about more than it is being lived?
3. Have I avoided someone I would normally ask? Is that because I fear what they might say, or because they have not proved safe or fair?
4. If I am isolated, who could become part of my circle? Who will I contact first?

## Part 4 — My decision

### Screen 6 — Advice and responsibility

1. What has advice helped me understand?
2. Do I understand that advice informs my decision, but does not make it for me?
3. Do I understand that the other person is also free to decide differently?
4. Am I putting off a decision by continuing to collect more advice?
5. Has anyone attached pressure, punishment, or a threat to the decision I make? Or are they calmly explaining a boundary about what they can and cannot continue to offer?

## Part 5 — Safety

*Answer Yes or No. Your answers on Screens 7 and 8 are never saved.*

### Screen 7 — Safety check (part 1)

1. I am afraid to tell anyone about this relationship.
2. The person I am dating repeatedly discourages me from seeking wise advice or tries to cut me off from trusted people.
3. The person I am dating forbids or punishes me for seeking advice.

### Screen 8 — Safety check (part 2)

1. A trustworthy person has told me a specific reason they fear for my safety.
2. A leader's advice has become pressure.
3. Family pressure has become coercion.

> **If any answer on Screen 7 or 8 is Yes, think about these privately:**
> - What exactly am I afraid will happen?
> - Is it safe to contact an adviser from my usual phone or computer?
> - Do I need a domestic-abuse, forced-marriage, or safeguarding specialist?
> - Should I avoid telling the partner or leader until I have received advice?
> - Is anyone in immediate danger?
>

### Screen 9 — Private journal

1. If my view is different from everyone else's, what am I seeing that they are not? What might they be seeing that I have ignored?
2. Am I gathering advice to help me decide — or to avoid deciding? No adviser can carry a responsibility that is mine.
