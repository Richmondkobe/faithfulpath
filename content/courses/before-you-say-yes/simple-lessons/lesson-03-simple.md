## One truth to remember

Knowing yourself can help you recognise confusion, pressure, and control more clearly.

## Key Scripture

"Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life." — Proverbs 4:23 (ESV)

In the Bible, the "heart" is more than feelings. It is the place where you think, choose, and decide. This verse asks you to watch over the place where your decisions are made.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About seven minutes. Read slowly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed "Read the full transcript" section.)*

Welcome to Lesson 3.

Ask most single Christians what they want in a husband or wife, and they will answer quickly. Kind. Godly. Funny. Good with money.

Now ask a different question: What would a husband or wife be getting in you? Often the room goes quiet.

That is understandable. When we date, we look outward. We study the other person. But every relationship has two people in it, and you are one of them.

The person you marry will live with your faith, your habits, your family, your money, and your past. If you do not know these things about yourself, your spouse will discover them for you. Usually after the wedding. Usually the hard way.

Here is why this matters most. When you are unsure of your beliefs, boundaries, and values, it can be harder to recognise when another person is taking too much control.

Imagine someone who is not sure what they believe, what they expect, or what they will not accept. Then they meet someone who is very sure about all three. It can be easy for that confident person to start deciding for them. Knowing yourself is not only wise. It can help protect you.

So what should you know about yourself? Your faith. What you value most. The lines you will not cross. What you expect marriage to look like. How close you expect your family to be. How you handle money. And how you handle conflict. Can you say sorry without defending yourself? Many people understand this part of themselves least, but a spouse may experience it most strongly.

You do not have to answer all of that today. The workbook will help you, one small piece at a time.

It also helps to notice your patterns. What kind of person are you usually drawn to? What have you put up with that you should not have? If you have not dated much, think about your friendships and your family. This is not about blaming yourself. It is about seeing clearly. A pattern you can see is a pattern you can change. A pattern you do not recognise is more likely to be repeated.

Now let's talk about your list. Most people have a list of what they want in a partner. The problem is that most lists mix four very different things together. Let me make this simple.

First, there are must-haves for everyone. These are needed for any safe, faithful, healthy marriage. A shared, genuine faith in Christ. Honesty. Respect. And freedom from violence, threats, control, and ongoing lies. Let me be very clear. Violence, threats, and control are never just a difference between two people. They break the basic safety that every relationship needs.

Second, there are your personal must-haves. These come from your own life and calling. For example, whether you want children, where you need to live, or a family member you must care for. These are real. But they should be clear, and you should have thought them through carefully.

Third, there are nice-to-haves. Height. A sense of humour. Loving the same music. You are allowed to want these things. But if you treat a nice-to-have as a must-have, you may turn away a good person for a small reason.

Fourth, there are rules that come from old hurts. For example: "They must never have dated anyone before." Or, "They must earn more than me." Some of these rules contain real wisdom. If someone lied to you before, it is reasonable to want a partner who is very honest. But a rule made quickly after pain can become too rigid. It may keep out good people and still fail to protect you. Real safety comes from a person's character, not from avoiding whatever hurt you last time.

So for every item on your list, ask two questions. Where did this come from? And what am I trying to prevent?

Let me say what this lesson does not mean.

It does not mean you must have your whole life figured out before you date. Nobody does. And it does not mean you should examine yourself until you feel ashamed. Self-examination in the Bible always ends by turning back to God. He knows you better than your list does, and he loves you more than your fears allow.

Pause for a moment.

If you had to describe yourself honestly to someone who might marry you, what is one thing they would need to be patient with?

*[Pause for five seconds.]*

Do not be discouraged by your answer. Everyone brings something that needs patience. Knowing yours is a strength.

Here is your one step for today. Write down the five things you most want in a partner. Choose one and ask: "Where did this come from, and what am I trying to prevent?"

Let's pray.

Lord, you know me better than I know myself. Search my heart and show me who I really am. Help me see my strengths with gratitude and my weaknesses with honesty. Give me clear convictions and a humble spirit. Where my fears have made my rules, bring your wisdom and your peace. In Jesus' name, amen.

That is enough for today. You can go on to the next lesson, open the workbook, or stop here and come back later.

3. Three things that matter most in how I live my life:
4. The last thing that made me very angry or sad. What might it show about what I value? *(Anger can also come from fear or misunderstanding, so think about it carefully.)*

### Screen 2 — Lines I will not cross

1. Biblical convictions I believe are essential for a faithful Christian relationship:
2. Matters of conscience where sincere Christians may disagree (Romans 14):
3. Which convictions have I carefully examined through Scripture, prayer, and wise counsel—and which do I simply feel strongly about?

### Screen 3 — Marriage and family

1. My picture of marriage: who works, who manages money, how decisions are made, how conflict is handled, how children are raised.
2. Where did that picture come from? (Parents, church, culture, or reacting against something?)
3. How involved do I expect my parents and wider family to be in my marriage?
4. What responsibilities do I carry for my family now, or expect to carry later?
5. What does my family expect of the person I marry, and how much weight will I give that?

### Screen 4 — Calling and daily life

1. What I believe God is asking of my life — or "not sure yet":
2. How have I tested that? (The Bible, prayer, advice from wise people, time.)
3. Do I need a spouse to share this calling, or to support it?
4. How I like to live: city or countryside, busy or quiet, lots of people or few, how I rest:
5. The one difference in daily life I would find hardest to live with:

### Screen 5 — Money

1. Am I more of a spender or a saver? What debt do I have, and how do I feel about it?
2. Who do I give to, or support? What do I believe money is for?

### Screen 6 — How I relate to people

1. How do I tell someone what I need?
2. How do I react when someone corrects me?
3. How do I handle disappointment?
4. When was the last time I said sorry without defending myself?

## Part 3 — Sorting my list

### Screen 8 — Write everything down

Write everything you want in a partner. Do not edit yet. Include serious needs, small preferences, and things you feel a little embarrassed to want.

### Screen 9 — Ask about each item

Go through your list one item at a time:

1. Where did this come from?
2. What problem do I think it would prevent?
3. Is it needed for safety, faithfulness, or a workable life together — or could the same need be met another way?
4. Has the Bible, experience, or wise advice made this stronger, or challenged it?
5. Would I apply the same standard to myself?

### Screen 10 — Sort into four boxes

Put each item into one box. *The course does not do this for you, and nothing is counted.*

- **Must-haves for everyone** — needed for any safe, faithful, healthy marriage: shared faith, honesty, respect, and freedom from violence, threats, control, and ongoing lies.
- **My personal must-haves** — connected to my conscience, children, family responsibilities, health, where I live, or a calling I have tested carefully.
- **Nice-to-haves** — things I truly want, but can be flexible about.
- **Rules from old hurts** — made partly because of fear or past pain. They may contain wisdom, but I need to look at them carefully.

### Screen 11 — Check your boxes

1. Can I clearly explain why each item in the first two boxes is necessary?
2. Are any of my "must-haves" really nice-to-haves that I am afraid to let go of?
3. For each rule from an old hurt: what is the real danger, and what would truly protect me from it?
4. Is there a wiser boundary that keeps the wisdom but is less rigid?
5. If I am dating now: has anything in my first two boxes already been bent?

*The length of your list is not what matters. What matters is whether you understand where each item came from and why it belongs in that category.*

> **If something in your first box has been crossed** — violence, threats, control, stalking, sexual pressure, or fear for your safety — do not begin by confronting the person privately or in a couples conversation. Get individual support and make a safety plan first. **Find help where you live →**
>
> For ordinary differences, a bent line does not mean the relationship must end today. It does mean you should look at it honestly — with God, with someone wise, and with the person you are dating, where that is safe.

### Screen 12 — What a spouse would get

1. Three strengths I would bring to a marriage:
2. Three things a spouse would need to be patient with in me:
3. One area I am working on right now:
4. Someone who would tell me the truth about these answers:

### Screen 13 — Ask one wise person

Choose one wise person who knows you well, will be honest, and will not use what you share against you. It does not need to be the person you are dating.

Ask them one question:

> Is there anything important about how I treat people that I might be missing?

You do not need to show them this workbook. Listen for one clear example, not a judgement of your whole character.

### Screen 14 — Private journal

1. What am I asking a spouse to accept about me that I have not fully accepted myself? Am I quietly hoping marriage will fix a responsibility that is mine?
2. Which of my values is hardest to see in how I actually live right now?
