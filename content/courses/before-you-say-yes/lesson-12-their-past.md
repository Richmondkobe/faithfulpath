# Lesson 12 — Their Past Matters, But Not the Way You Think

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to apply four questions to any part
of someone's history — truth, responsibility, response, present effect — know what
needs to be disclosed at which stage, and recognise what is never yours to ask
for.

**Key Scripture**
"As far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions
from us." — Psalm 103:12 (ESV)

The psalm is about what God does with a repentant person's sin, and it is
absolute. What it does not say is that consequences disappear, that trust is
automatically restored, or that anyone is thereby owed a marriage. Holding both
halves is the whole work of this lesson.

## Core teaching

Everyone you might marry has a past. Some of it will be ordinary. Some may include
a broken engagement, a divorce, a child, an addiction, a season away from God, a
criminal record, a sexual history, or harm that was done to them. You will need to
know some of it. You are not entitled to all of it. And what you do with what you
learn will say as much about you as about them.

Christians tend to get this wrong in two opposite directions. Some treat the past
as destiny: a divorced person is disqualified, someone who struggled with
addiction always will, a person with a sexual history is damaged, and no amount of
grace changes the verdict. Others treat it as irrelevant — *who they were doesn't
matter, only who they are now* — as though the present could be seen without
reference to how it was arrived at. Neither is true, and neither is Christian.

**What the past cannot tell you.** It cannot tell you whether a person is forgiven.
That is between them and God, and if God has forgiven a repentant person you must
not call them spiritually inferior or permanently unclean. But forgiveness does
not erase consequences, restore trust automatically, or create entitlement to
marriage. **You may recognise God's grace in someone's life and still conclude,
without condemnation, that you should not marry them. Declining a relationship is
not the same as denying forgiveness.** Hold on to that sentence; it is the one that
keeps grace from becoming pressure.

It cannot tell you their worth — a repentant person is not a lesser Christian or a
second-class spouse. And it cannot, on its own, tell you whether they would be a
good husband or wife. Some of the most faithful spouses alive are people whose
twenties would have alarmed you. Some of the most damaging are people with nothing
in their history that would have warned anyone.

**What the past can tell you** — through four questions.

**Truth.** Are the material facts disclosed honestly and appropriately? Is the
account substantially consistent over months? Do they correct a good impression
that turns out to be inaccurate? Someone honest about a difficult history is
showing you something more valuable than a clean one.

**Responsibility.** Where they caused harm, have they owned it without minimising
or blame-shifting? Not every past involves fault, and a person who was harmed
should never be pressed into false equivalence. But where a part was theirs, a
person of character can name it without collapsing into shame and without
deflecting onto everyone else.

**Response.** What concrete steps followed — restitution, treatment, changed
behaviour, accountability, legal compliance? *I've dealt with that* is a claim.
Evidence can include behaviour, stability, professional support and appropriate
boundaries. It does not have to be an impressive recovery narrative; some people
struggle to describe complex inner work, and what they have done matters more than
how well they explain it.

**Present effect.** What obligations, risks, patterns or consequences remain
active? An unresolved attachment to a former partner. Contact that is not
appropriate. Patterns visibly repeating. Legal, financial or parental matters
unfinished. A past does not have to have disappeared to have stopped governing
someone destructively — but you need to understand what of it is still in the
room.

**Some particular pasts**, each taking the same four questions.

*Previous serious relationships.* What ended, and why, and what did they learn?
Several relationships ending through similar dynamics deserve careful exploration:
repetition may reveal something about their choices, their behaviour, the kind of
relationship they enter, or circumstances they have not yet understood. It raises
a question. It does not supply the answer.

*Divorce.* Christian traditions differ on when remarriage is permitted, and this
course does not settle that for you. Know your own church's teaching and theirs,
and take the question to your pastor before the relationship goes far. What this
lesson can ask: is the divorce legally final? How do they speak of their former
spouse — with discretion, or contempt? Can they talk about the former marriage
with enough steadiness to make room for a new relationship, or are grief, anger or
legal conflict still dominating daily life? Where strong feelings remain — and
after betrayal or abuse they legitimately may — are they being addressed
responsibly rather than transferred onto you? Do not require a survivor to
manufacture equal responsibility for abuse. Before engagement you will both need
clarity about children and custody, support obligations, property, unresolved
litigation, necessary contact with the former spouse, any protective orders, and
whether remarriage aligns with both your convictions.

*Children.* A child is a person, not baggage. Two things must be true at once. You
must be honest with yourself about whether you could love and commit to a child
who is not yours, and to the arrangements that come with them — pretending that is
small will hurt everyone, including the child. And the child's wellbeing comes
before your convenience: a thoughtful parent weighs the child's age, attachments,
safety and the seriousness of the relationship before arranging an introduction.
Dating a parent does not make you a parent. Do not seek emotional authority,
discipline the child, or ask a child to keep relationship secrets.

*Addiction.* Recovery is a process rather than a flawless record, and it is not
always linear. The relevant questions concern present use, safety, stability,
honesty, treatment or support, relapse planning, and the effect on daily
responsibilities. Someone in genuine recovery can usually describe the structure
holding it up. The non-negotiable from Lesson 3 stands: not whether someone has
ever struggled, but whether there is an active, unaddressed addiction accompanied
by deception or refusal of help. A relapse may require immediate reassessment and
stronger treatment; it is not automatic proof that all recovery was false.
Concealment, refusal of help, dangerous conduct and pressure on you to manage the
addiction are the separate and serious concerns. And be clear about your role:
supporting someone in recovery does not make you their therapist, their monitor,
or their reason for staying sober. **A partner cannot be the treatment plan.**

*Sexual history.* Three different matters need separating. *Details of past sexual
history* — a partner is not entitled to these, and demanding a list is a warning
sign about you. *Current sexual behaviour or commitments* — a present struggle
deserves honesty and may affect compatibility. *Sexual health* — information that
may affect informed medical decisions. Mutual sexual-health responsibility focuses
on present health, appropriate testing and informed decisions, not on using
results to infer moral worth or reconstruct someone's history. Screening needs
vary, so discuss them confidentially with a healthcare professional rather than
relying on a checklist. A chronic condition may require treatment; it does not
make someone unsuitable or unclean.

*Criminal history.* Some conduct affects safety, and that is not a matter for grace
alone. A conviction is not the only relevant fact — a person may have caused
serious harm without one, and a conviction may involve complex circumstances.
Weigh the nature of the conduct, its seriousness, recency, legal consequences,
evidence of accountability, and any continuing risk. **Where violence or sexual
offending is involved, a long period without reoffending and sincere repentance
may matter morally, but neither guarantees safety. Obtain specialist safeguarding
advice rather than deciding through prayer and observation alone. Dating
discernment cannot substitute for a professional risk assessment.**

*Faith history.* A season away from God, a previous religion, a church left badly.
The important questions are what they now believe, how long that direction has
been visible, and whether their present life increasingly aligns with it.

**Trauma is not a past to evaluate.** What was done to a person is not their
history in the sense this lesson has been using. It is not something they did that
reveals character. It is a wound, and the only questions it raises about character
concern the person who inflicted it.

Survivors decide when, how much, and whether to disclose. Someone who says early
on *there are things in my past I'm not ready to talk about* has given you a
complete and honest answer for that stage. Do not press. Do not treat a survivor
as fragile, unstable or damaged — many of the most whole people you will meet are
people who have done hard work with hard things. As trust grows, both people may
need honest conversation about present boundaries, support needs and effects on a
shared life, without the survivor disclosing details of what happened. And a
prospective spouse is allowed to consider honestly whether they have the maturity
to honour another person's needs.

Be careful about consistency here. Trauma disclosure can be delayed for years, can
develop over time, and can contain fragmented memories. Inconsistency is not
automatically deception, and incomplete or developing disclosure is not proof of
lying. If safety decisions need professional understanding, seek trauma-informed
help rather than investigating the survivor.

When someone tells you, what helps is simple, and it is not a set of questions.
*Thank you for trusting me. I am sorry this happened. It was not your fault. You
do not have to give me details. What would help you feel respected and safe now?*
What does not help is *why* questions, *are you healed?* or *how will this affect
marriage?* — not on that day.

And if you are the survivor: you owe no one your story before you are ready. A
serious relationship will eventually need enough truth to understand how what
happened might shape a shared life, but the timing is yours, the detail is yours,
and a partner who cannot receive it with care is showing you something about them,
not about you.

**What needs to be known, and when.** The test is not *what would they feel
deceived not to know*, but: any fact a reasonable person would need in order to
make an informed decision about safety, legal status, children, finances, health,
faith commitments, or the practical life being proposed.

*Early dating:* nothing detailed. Attitudes, honesty in small things, how they
speak about their life. *Before exclusivity:* a current marriage or legal
partnership; children and dependants; a current relationship with someone else;
any conduct or condition creating an immediate safety risk; active addiction
seriously affecting functioning or safety; legal restrictions affecting contact or
travel; a history of violence or sexual offending relevant to present safety.
*Before engagement:* previous marriages and continuing obligations; children,
custody and support; significant debt or legal liabilities; addiction or recovery
matters materially affecting shared life; relevant criminal history; known health
information materially affecting the other person's informed medical decisions or
the practical shared life being proposed, disclosed at an appropriate level
without surrendering private records; ongoing obligations involving former
partners; immigration and residence consequences; and any fact whose concealment
would prevent informed consent to marriage.

A diagnosis alone is not evidence of poor character or automatic incompatibility.

**What you are not entitled to, at any stage:** the details of someone's trauma; a
sexual history; an exhaustive account of every past relationship; anything that is
another person's confidence to keep. And what applies to them applies to you.
Disclosure is mutual, to the same standard, on the same timetable.

*Receiving a disclosure:* with thanks for the honesty first. Without humiliation,
retaliation or weaponising — what was shared in trust must never come back as
ammunition. With time; you are allowed to need to think, and to say so. And you
remain free to recognise incompatibility, seek counsel, slow down or end the
relationship. A thoughtful decision is not punishment. Treat what you were told as
confidential, unless someone is in danger, a child or vulnerable person may be at
risk, or law or safeguarding responsibility requires action.

*Giving one:* at the right stage, plainly, without minimising or performing. Say
what happened, what you did about it, and where you are now. Choose a setting that
allows privacy, emotional safety and an independent way to leave — in-person is
often right, but remote disclosure can be safer where retaliation is possible.
Then let them respond. If they need time, give it. If they cannot receive it, that
is painful, and it is also information.

**Neither record decides it.** To the reader drawn to someone with a difficult past
and wondering whether they are settling: a difficult past does not prevent someone
from becoming a faithful spouse. Paul described himself as "formerly a blasphemer,
persecutor, and insolent opponent" who "received mercy" (1 Timothy 1:13, 16). His
history shows the reach of grace — though it does not imply that every repentant
person is suitable for every relationship. And to the reader whose record is the
cleaner one: an uncomplicated past does not guarantee character. Someone who has
never had to be wrong, apologise and repair may be untested rather than proven.

## Practical tool — Past Relationship Reflection

Part A is about you. Part B is what you know about them, at the level appropriate
to where you are. Part C is what must be known before exclusivity and engagement.
Part D is giving and receiving disclosure. Part E is safety. Nothing here is
scored.

> **This is not a dossier.** Do not investigate, do not record what was told in
> confidence, and do not write down details of anyone's trauma — including your
> own. Record only what you need for your own discernment.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you or
> anyone else at risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

> **If you are not currently dating**, Part A is the work. Ask the four questions
> of yourself before you ask them of anyone else.

### Part A — My own past

Apply the four questions to yourself. Do not record details here.

- *Truth:* Is there material information I will need to disclose before a greater
  commitment?
- *Responsibility:* Where I contributed to what went wrong in past relationships,
  can I say so without collapsing or deflecting?
- *Response:* What concrete steps have I taken about the things that needed
  dealing with?
- *Present effect:* Is anything in my past still affecting my present — an
  attachment, an obligation, a pattern, an unfinished matter?
- If I have been harmed: when, and with what support, would I want a partner to
  know? *(No details here.)*

### Part B — What I know about theirs

Answer *Observed / Not yet observed / Mixed evidence / Concern observed*, with
evidence. Leave blank what the relationship has not yet reached.

- *Truth:* Their account of matters for which they were responsible has been
  substantially honest, without deliberate contradiction or concealment.
  *(Developing or fragmented trauma disclosure is not evaluated by this
  question.)*
- *Responsibility:* Where they caused harm, they can say so.
- *Response:* Where their own conduct required change, they can identify concrete
  steps taken. Where they were harmed, I have not demanded proof of healing.
- *Present effect:* I understand any significant ways the past presently affects
  obligations, safety, boundaries or the relationship.
- If there is a pattern across past relationships, I have noticed it and raised it
  as a question, not a verdict:
- If they have said *I'm not ready to talk about that*, I have respected it.

### Part C — What needs to be known

Answer *Known / Not yet known / Not applicable*, for the stage you have reached.

**Before exclusivity**
- Any current marriage or legal partnership.
- Children and dependants.
- Any current relationship with another person.
- Any conduct or condition creating an immediate safety risk.
- Active addiction seriously affecting functioning or safety.
- Legal restrictions affecting contact or travel.
- Any history of violence or sexual offending relevant to present safety.

**Before engagement**
- Previous marriages and continuing obligations.
- Children, custody and support arrangements.
- Significant debt or legal liabilities.
- Addiction or recovery matters materially affecting shared life.
- Criminal history materially affecting safety, legal obligations or our proposed
  future.
- Known health information materially affecting the other person's informed
  medical decisions or the practical shared life being proposed, disclosed at an
  appropriate level without surrendering private records.
- Ongoing contact or obligations involving former partners.
- Immigration and residence consequences.
- Any other fact whose concealment would prevent informed consent to marriage.

- Have I disclosed to the same standard?

### Part D — Giving and receiving

- Is there material information I need to disclose? At what stage, in what setting,
  and with what support?
- When they disclosed something difficult, how did I respond — with thanks, with
  time, without humiliation or weaponising, in confidence?
- Have I ever used something they told me in trust as ammunition?
- If they responded with humiliation, retaliation, coercion or weaponising, what
  did that show me?

### Part E — Safety

Answer *Yes / No*.

- Their past includes violence towards a partner, a child or a vulnerable person,
  or sexual offences.
- I am being asked to accept *that was the old me* without sustained evidence of
  accountability and change beyond the person's own assurances, including
  appropriate professional treatment, compliance with legal or safeguarding
  requirements, and independently verifiable stability where such information is
  lawfully and ethically available.
- Present behaviour — anger, control, contempt — matches the past account.
- I have been asked to keep something quiet that affects other people's safety.

> Do not seek confidential records, contact former partners or potential victims,
> or ask friends to conduct an informal investigation.

Sort any *Yes* into one of three:

- **Immediate safety concern** — contact emergency or specialist services.
- **Current safeguarding concern** — seek qualified safeguarding advice promptly.
  Do not investigate or confront alone.
- **Historical concern requiring assessment** — do not move towards engagement
  until specialist advice and sufficient information are available.

*Before you finish:* pray Psalm 103:12 for yourself first — that you would know
your own past forgiven — and then for the person you are considering. Then ask for
the harder gift: wisdom to see clearly both what happened and what has followed,
without holding against anyone what God has released, and without ignoring what is
still plainly in the room.

## Next faithful step

Look at the first line of Part D. If material information should reasonably be
disclosed at your present stage, decide when, where and what support you need
before speaking. If it belongs to a later stage, identify that stage without
disclosing prematurely or recording sensitive details here. Where retaliation,
humiliation, coercion or unsafe exposure is possible, seek individual advice
before arranging the conversation.

## Journal reflection

1. Which direction do you lean — treating a past as destiny, or as irrelevant?
   What has that cost you or someone else?
2. Is there a decision you have been avoiding because making it would feel like
   condemning someone? Declining is not condemning. What changes if you separate
   the two?

## Need support?

Before anything else: **a history of violence towards a partner, child or
vulnerable person is not adequately evaluated through the four questions alone. It
remains relevant to present safety, even when the conduct is historical.
Accountability, treatment and time may provide important information, but none
guarantees safety. Do not attempt your own risk assessment; seek qualified
safeguarding advice before moving towards commitment.**

Where current danger to a child or vulnerable adult may exist, do not confront the
person privately and do not contact a potential victim to investigate. Keep only
information already lawfully in your possession and necessary for obtaining
advice, and store it safely. Do not copy, forward or retain illegal or intimate
material in order to create evidence. Ask a safeguarding, legal or specialist
service what should be preserved and how before taking further steps where
possible. Follow the reporting requirements where you live, and contact emergency
services if danger is immediate. Do not promise secrecy.

If you have been asked to keep quiet about something affecting other people's
safety, you are not obliged to work out what to do alone — seek confidential
safeguarding advice promptly, so you can act without investigating or increasing
danger.

If this lesson has surfaced harm done to you, a suitably qualified trauma-informed
counsellor may be one helpful source of support. If it involved coercion, control,
abuse, stalking or sexual violence, begin with a specialist service. A trusted
pastor may support you spiritually alongside that help.

## Continue

You do not need to decide today.

If you learn something unsettling, you do not have to decide in the moment.
Consider truth, responsibility, response and present effect; clarify only what is
appropriate to the relationship stage; and seek independent counsel. If the
history involves violence, sexual offending, danger to a child or vulnerable
person, or another safeguarding concern, do not investigate or rely on ordinary
pastoral discernment alone — seek qualified specialist advice.

And if their past, or yours, includes harm that was done rather than done to, be
gentle and get help. That is not a lesson's work. It is a season's.
