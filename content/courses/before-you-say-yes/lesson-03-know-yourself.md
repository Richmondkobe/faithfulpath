# Lesson 3 — Know Yourself Before You Choose Someone Else

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to describe what a spouse would be
getting in you, and to sort your requirements for a spouse into four kinds
instead of one undifferentiated list.

**Key Scripture**
"Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life." —
Proverbs 4:23 (ESV)

The Hebrew for *heart* covers far more than feeling — it is the centre from which
thinking, willing and deciding come. Proverbs is not telling you to guard your
emotions from injury. It is telling you to keep watch over the place your
decisions are made. Paul asks for the same thing when he tells the Romans to
think of themselves "with sober judgment" (Romans 12:3): not too highly, not too
low, but accurately.

## Core teaching

Ask most single Christians what they are looking for in a spouse and they will
have an answer ready. Ask them what a spouse would be getting, and the room goes
quiet.

That is understandable. Dating turns our attention outward. We study the other
person and look for signs. But every relationship has two people in it, and you
are one of them. Whoever you marry will have to live with your values, habits,
family, money, convictions and history. If you do not know those things about
yourself, you are asking someone else to discover them for you — usually after
the wedding, usually the hard way.

Four practical reasons to do this before you go further. **You cannot assess fit
against something you have not defined** — undefined, you will end up evaluating
people on whatever is most visible: looks, charm, spiritual vocabulary, how they
make you feel. **You will confuse preferences with principles**, producing either a
list nobody could meet or one so vague you cannot tell when a real line has been
crossed. **You may repeat patterns you have not noticed** — self-knowledge is how a
pattern becomes a choice rather than a fate. And most importantly for this
course: **a person who does not know themselves is easier to define.** Someone who
arrives unclear about what they believe, expect and will not accept is vulnerable
to a partner who is very clear about all three. Several of the red flags in
Lesson 6 depend on exactly that — one person supplying the identity the other has
not yet worked out. Knowing yourself is not only wise. It is protective.

Nine areas are worth understanding. This is a map, not an evening's checklist.

**Faith** — not only whether you believe but how you practise, and what you would
not change about your spiritual life for another person. **Values** — the
priorities that shape how you organise your life. Everyone has them; not everyone
knows what they are until they are violated. What made you most angry or grieved
this year may point to one, though anger can also come from fear or
misunderstanding, so examine it rather than simply trusting it. **Convictions** —
lines you believe you should not cross. Some are shared by all Christians; others
are matters of conscience where sincere believers differ, and Paul asks each
person there to be "fully convinced in his own mind" (Romans 14:5). Know which is
which, and test your convictions against Scripture rather than treating them as
unquestionable because they feel strong. **Expectations of marriage** — who works,
who manages money, how decisions are made, how conflict is handled, how children
are raised. Most people carry a picture absorbed from parents, church or culture
and have never examined it. You do not have to justify yours yet. You do have to
know what it is and where it came from. **Family** — Genesis 2:24 describes leaving
father and mother to hold fast to a spouse. In many cultures that leaving is not
cutting ties but forming a new primary bond while the extended family stays
close. Know what you expect, what obligations you carry, and how much weight you
will give your family's expectations. **Calling and direction** — hold it with
conviction and humility, tested by Scripture, prayer, counsel and time. Note the
difference between sharing a calling and supporting one. **Lifestyle** — city or
countryside, fast or slow, how you rest, how much order you need. These sound
small. They are what a marriage is made of hour by hour. **Finances** — not your
income but your habits and attitude. **Relational capacity** — how you communicate
a need, receive correction, repair conflict. Can you apologise without defending
yourself? This is the area most people know least about themselves, and the one a
spouse will feel most.

Alongside those is something less tidy: your history, and what it shows. Who have
you been drawn to, and why? What did you tolerate that you should not have? What
did you demand that was unfair? If you have not dated much, ask the same of your
friendships and family. None of this is self-condemnation; it is information. A
pattern you can see is one you can pray about and choose to interrupt. A pattern
you cannot see will simply happen again.

Now the list. What do you require in a spouse? Most people's lists mix four very
different kinds of item, and the mixture is where the trouble starts.

**Foundational requirements** are necessary for any safe, faithful, healthy
marriage. They are not personal — they are the floor beneath everyone's list. A
shared and genuine faith. Basic honesty. Respect. Freedom from violence, threats,
coercion, controlling behaviour and ongoing deception. Be clear about that last
one: violence, threats, coercion and control are not compatibility differences.
They violate the basic safety and dignity a relationship requires.

**Personal non-negotiables** arise from your circumstances, conscience and calling
rather than universal principles: children, an established responsibility you
cannot walk away from, health, location, a well-tested direction — or an active,
unaddressed addiction accompanied by deception or refusal of help. Specific,
examined, open to testing.

**Preferences** are qualities you genuinely value but can hold flexibly. Height,
humour, education, a shared love of music. Legitimate — you are allowed to want
what you want — but a preference treated as a non-negotiable will keep you from
good people for reasons that do not survive prayer.

**Protective rules to examine** are the difficult fourth kind: requirements formed
partly in response to fear, pain or previous harm. *They must never have been in a
serious relationship. They must earn more than me. They must have no close
friends of the opposite sex.* Some contain real wisdom — someone betrayed may have
a legitimate need for strong transparency. The existence of fear does not
automatically invalidate a boundary. But a rule built quickly after an injury can
be expressed too rigidly, keeping out good people along with dangerous ones, and
some protective rules do not deliver the safety they promise, because safety comes
from a person's character rather than from the absence of whatever frightened you
last time. The aim is not to discard every boundary connected to pain. It is to
identify the actual danger and choose a boundary that answers it wisely.

To tell the categories apart, ask of each item: where did this come from, and
what problem do I believe it would prevent?

Two warnings. A list that is too long is usually preferences and protective rules
dressed as principles, and often functions as a way of never having to risk
anything. A list that is too short, or does not exist, usually means the work has
not been done — and the non-negotiable will be discovered only once it has
already been crossed.

## Practical tool — Personal Values and Non-Negotiables Sheet

A written reflection, best done over several sittings. Keep what you write: you
will use it in Lesson 7 before exclusivity, and again in Lesson 15 when you
consider whether you and another person can build a life together. Nobody grades
this, and nothing is scored.

### Part A — Who I am

A few sentences under each heading. Where a question does not apply, say so and
move on.

**Faith**
- My spiritual practices, week to week (church, prayer, Scripture, giving,
  serving):
- What I would not be willing to change about my spiritual life for another
  person:

**Values**
- Three qualities or priorities that shape how I organise my life:
- The last thing that made me deeply angry or grieved, and what I think it
  reveals — a value, or something else:

**Convictions**
- Lines I believe I should not cross that all Christians would share:
- Convictions of conscience where I know sincere Christians differ (Romans 14):
- Which of these I have actually tested against Scripture, and which I have
  simply felt strongly:

**Expectations of marriage**
- The picture of marriage I carry — who works, who manages money, how decisions
  are made, how conflict is handled, how children are raised:
- Where that picture came from (parents, church, culture, reaction against
  something):

**Family**
- How involved I expect my parents and extended family to be in my marriage:
- Obligations I carry now or expect to carry (financial support, care of
  relatives, responsibility for siblings):
- What my family expects of the person I marry, and how much weight I will give
  that:

**Calling and direction**
- What I believe God is asking of my life, or "not yet sure":
- How I have tested that (Scripture, prayer, counsel, time):
- Whether I need a spouse to share it, or to support it:

**Lifestyle**
- City or countryside; fast or slow; many people or few; how I rest; how I keep
  my health:
- The one lifestyle mismatch I think I would find hardest to live with:

**Finances**
- Spender or saver; debt I carry and how I feel about it; who I give to; who I
  support:
- What I believe money is for:

**Relational capacity**
- How I say what I need:
- How I respond to correction:
- How I handle disappointment:
- The last time I apologised without defending myself:

### Part B — My patterns

If you have dated before, answer about past relationships. If not, answer about
your closest friendships and your family.

- The kind of person I tend to be drawn to, and what I think draws me:
- Something I have tolerated that I should not have:
- Something I have demanded that was unfair:
- Whether I tend to rush or hold back, lose myself or keep distance:
- How my closest relationships have ended or been strained, and my part in it:
- One pattern I would like to interrupt:

> If these questions bring up painful relationship experiences, pause here. A
> suitably qualified counsellor can help you process them. If what happened
> involved coercion, control, abuse, stalking or sexual pressure, contact a
> specialist abuse service for individual guidance. A trusted pastor may support
> you spiritually alongside that help, provided they understand their limits and
> do not pressure you towards confrontation, reconciliation or joint counselling.

### Part C — The list, sorted

**Step 1.** Write down everything that comes to mind when you think about what you
want in a spouse, without editing. Include serious requirements, ordinary
preferences, and qualities you feel embarrassed to admit wanting.

**Step 2.** Go through it one item at a time and ask:

- Where did this expectation come from?
- What problem do I believe it would prevent?
- Is it necessary for safety, faithfulness or practical workability?
- Could the same need be met in another way?
- Have Scripture, experience and wise counsel strengthened this requirement, or
  challenged it?
- Am I willing to apply the same standard to myself where appropriate?

**Step 3.** Sort each item into one of four boxes.

| Box | What belongs here |
|---|---|
| Foundational requirements | Necessary for any safe, faithful and healthy marriage: shared faith, honesty, respect, and freedom from violence, coercion, control or ongoing deception. |
| Personal non-negotiables | Connected to my conscience, children, established responsibilities, health, location or a well-tested calling. Specific, examined and open to testing. |
| Preferences | Genuinely desired and held with flexibility. |
| Protective rules to examine | Formed partly in response to fear, pain or previous harm. May contain wisdom but needs careful examination. |

### Part D — Checking the boxes

- Can I explain clearly why each item in the first two boxes is necessary?
- Have I separated universal character and safety requirements from my personal
  circumstances?
- Are any items in the first two boxes really preferences I am afraid to hold
  loosely?
- For each protective rule: what is the actual danger, what would genuinely
  protect me from it, and is there a wiser boundary that keeps the wisdom without
  the rigidity?
- If I am currently dating: has any item in the first two boxes already been
  bent?

### Part E — What I bring

Because the person you marry will be marrying you.

- Three strengths I would bring to a marriage:
- Three things a spouse would have to be patient with in me:
- One area I am actively working on:
- Someone in my life who would tell me the truth about the answers above, and
  whether I have asked them:

*Before you finish:* pray with the words of Lamentations — "Let us test and
examine our ways, and return to the LORD!" (Lamentations 3:40). Self-examination
in Scripture never ends with the self. It ends with returning to God, who knows
you better than your list does and loves you more than your fears allow.

## Next faithful step

Return to the last line of Part E and name one wise person who knows you well,
can speak honestly and will not use your vulnerability against you. This does not
need to be the person you are dating.

Ask them: *is there anything important about how I relate to people that my
description misses or gets wrong?* You do not need to show them the whole sheet.
Listen for one concrete observation rather than asking them to evaluate your
entire character.

## Journal reflection

1. What are you asking a spouse to accept that you have not yet fully accepted
   about yourself — and is there a responsibility you are quietly expecting
   marriage to solve for you?
2. Which of your stated values is least visible in your current behaviour?

## Need support?

Knowing yourself includes knowing what you have survived. If Part B surfaced a
betrayal or painful relationship experience that still weighs on you, consider
speaking with a suitably qualified counsellor. If it involved coercion, control,
abuse, stalking or sexual pressure, begin with a specialist abuse service rather
than trying to process it through a worksheet or ordinary couples counselling. A
trusted pastor may support you spiritually alongside appropriate professional or
specialist help. **Find help where you live →**

## Continue

If you are not yet in a relationship, Part A is the work — know your lines, as far
as you can, before anyone tests them. If you are dating and have never discussed
any of this, have the conversation. It is awkward for about ten minutes and then
it is one of the most useful things you have done.

And if you discovered while reading that a boundary you would have wanted has
already been crossed: for ordinary incompatibility, discovering a bent line does
not mean the relationship must end today. It does mean the issue should be
examined honestly — with God, with someone wise and, where it is safe and
appropriate, with the person you are dating. If the crossed line involves
violence, threats, coercion, stalking, sexual pressure or fear for your safety,
do not begin with a private confrontation or a couples conversation; get
individual support and make a safety plan first.
