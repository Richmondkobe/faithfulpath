# Lesson 11 — Money Reveals More Than a Salary

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to describe what you have observed
about how someone handles money, identify what financial information it is
reasonable to discuss at each stage of a relationship, and recognise the
difference between a financial warning sign and financial exploitation.

**Key Scripture**
"If then you have not been faithful in the unrighteous wealth, who will entrust to
you the true riches?" — Luke 16:11 (ESV)

This is the second half of the verse from Lesson 5. Jesus is teaching about
stewardship, not about marriage — but the logic is the same one: faithfulness with
money is treated as a training ground for faithfulness in greater things. It is a
reasonable training ground for marriage too.

## Core teaching

When people ask about a potential spouse's finances, the question they usually
mean is *what do they earn?* It is not a useless question. Income stability matters
when you think about children, housing or family support. But it tells you much
less about character than people assume. Two people on the same salary can be as
different as it is possible to be — one careful, generous, honest and content; the
other reckless, secretive, grasping and never satisfied.

Money is a window because it is where words and behaviour most often separate.
Everyone says they value generosity, honesty and responsibility. Money is where
you find out.

**What this lesson is not about.** Not how much a person has. Poverty is not a
character flaw, and neither is wealth. A student with loans, a young professional
in a low-paid ministry job, someone supporting parents on a modest income, a widow
who lost most of what she had — none of them is showing you anything about their
character by their bank balance. And a wealthy person may be miserly or dishonest
in ways the balance conceals. James warned the church sharply against honouring
the rich and slighting the poor (James 2:1–5).

It is also not the full financial conversation engaged couples need. In dating you
are not merging finances and you are not entitled to an audit. Financial privacy
is legitimate at this stage. What you are doing is watching character, and
learning the material facts that would affect a shared future.

**Six windows.**

**Spending** — not how much, but how. Do they generally understand what they can
afford? Can they tell an occasional pleasure from a repeated pattern that
undermines essential obligations? When money is tight, do they respond with
honesty and adjustment, or with concealment and further debt? Do not judge a
lifestyle from isolated purchases; a costly item may have been saved for, gifted
or necessary. But over time, what someone spends on when they have a choice is
worth noticing. Jesus said, "where your treasure is, there your heart will be
also" (Matthew 6:21) — spoken about storing treasure in heaven, but the
observation runs both ways.

**Debt** — not whether, but what, why and how they carry it. Do not assume that one
category proves wisdom and another proves poor character: consumer debt can result
from poverty, family crisis or exploitation, and study debt can be carried
recklessly. Ask, when it is appropriate, what the debt is, how it arose and what
the plan is. Then watch. Do they understand it, tell the truth about it, meet
obligations where reasonably possible? Proverbs observes that "the borrower is the
slave of the lender" (22:7) — not a command against all borrowing, but a sober
description of what debt does to freedom. Sometimes what a person needs is not
more discipline but professional debt advice, and willingness to seek it is itself
a good sign.

**Generosity** — "God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7), and *cheerful* is
doing real work. Generosity is willingness to use available resources for others'
good, without manipulation or performance. Its form and amount differ by income,
culture, responsibility and conviction, and practices around church giving differ
across traditions. Giving beyond your means to maintain an image or avoid guilt is
not mature generosity; stewardship includes the ability to say no, including to
relatives and church members who ask repeatedly. And look at the counterfeit:
generosity that buys affection, approval or obligation is not generosity. Public
knowledge does not invalidate a gift, but a need for recognition may reveal
something about its purpose.

**Secrecy** — privacy about details is legitimate in dating; secrecy about material
facts is not. Material facts include debts, legal liabilities, dependants, regular
family obligations, unstable or unlawful income, significant gambling losses,
bankruptcy, and commitments that would substantially affect a shared household.
You do not need to see statements, and honest disclosure does not require
passwords or account access. You do need honesty about the big things. Watch for
someone who will not discuss money at all once the relationship warrants it, whose
accounts shift, or who has clearly presented themselves as wealthier or poorer
than they are.

**Ambition** — drive is good, and Scripture commends diligence. Be fair: someone
working long hours may be surviving rather than worshipping money. The question is
what they would sacrifice for it. Their integrity? Their church? Their rest? You?
Paul's warning is precise — not that money is a root of evil, but that "the love
of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (1 Timothy 6:10). Watch too how they
handle risk. Legitimate investments carry risk, and risk tolerance is partly a
compatibility question. The character concerns are different: repeated investment
in products they cannot explain, promises of guaranteed returns, pressure to act
immediately, borrowing to speculate, chasing losses, treating risk as proof of
faith.

**Responsibility** — the plain, boring evidence, read fairly. Responsibility is not
the same as always having enough; payment history can reflect poverty, delayed
salary, illness or unreliable systems rather than character. Ask instead: do they
take obligations seriously, communicate honestly when they cannot meet them, and
seek workable solutions rather than disappearing, blaming, or making promises they
cannot keep? Do they plan where planning is possible, and seek help when
circumstances exceed their capacity?

**Money between the two of you.** *Who pays, and does it create obligation?*
Cultures differ, and so do couples. What matters is that neither is keeping score
and neither is using payment as leverage. Gifts do not purchase access, affection,
sex, commitment, or influence over your decisions. Not every expectation of
gratitude is manipulation; the question is whether obligation is being created
through pressure. *How do they treat your money?* Someone careful with their own
and casual with yours — borrowing, assuming you will pay, treating what is yours
as shared before anything has been shared — is showing you something. So is
someone who treats your financial decisions as theirs to approve. *And what are
your own patterns?* If you are the one who over-gives, who cannot refuse a
request, who uses money to secure affection, or who hides your own debt, this
lesson is looking at you too.

A cultural note. In many parts of the world, adults regularly send money to
parents, siblings and extended family. Lesson 10 described this as honourable, and
it can be. It is not automatically wise in every amount. What matters is whether
the commitment is freely acknowledged, sustainable, transparent before marriage,
and balanced against responsibility to a future household.

**Warning signs, and what is beyond warning.** Financial warning signs deserve
attention and, if they persist, a serious conversation and outside counsel. Weigh
seriousness, repetition, deception and — where the matter was safely clarified or
addressed — their response. If raising it may expose you to intimidation,
retaliation or further financial harm, seek individual advice instead. Repeated
borrowing with entitlement, deception or broken agreements. Pressure to lend,
co-sign or guarantee. Concealed gambling. Debt discovered rather than disclosed.
Spending that consistently contradicts stated values. Gifts used to create
obligation. Treating your money as theirs. Refusal to discuss money at all when
the relationship warrants it.

On co-signing, a general safeguard: **do not co-sign or guarantee another person's
debt while dating.** A co-signer may become legally responsible for the entire
debt, and missed payments may affect their own financial record; rules vary by
country, but the commitment is potentially serious. If exceptional circumstances
make you consider it, get independent legal and financial advice — not from the
partner, the lender or a salesperson — and proceed only if you could afford to
repay the whole obligation yourself.

**Financial exploitation is not a warning sign. It is harm**, and the response is
Lesson 6's: safety, outside help, not another conversation. It takes three shapes
worth naming.

*Romance scams.* Any request for money from a romantic interest you know only
online should be treated as a serious scam warning, regardless of the story or the
urgency. Do not send money while trying to work out whether the explanation is
genuine. People caught by these scams are not foolish; they are kind, and they
were targeted for it. The pattern is consistent: intense affection early, a crisis
requiring urgent money, reasons why meeting is impossible, and a second crisis
after the first is paid. Requests for wire transfers, gift cards or cryptocurrency
are typical. Scammers now use video calls, stolen identities and manipulated
media, so an available video call is not proof of identity.

*Investment scams.* Never transfer money, cryptocurrency or account access to an
investment introduced by an online romantic interest. Do not rely on screenshots,
apparent profits, or a platform supplied by that person; scammers build trust and
then direct people to apparently genuine trading sites they control. Verify
independently through the relevant regulator in your country.

*Financial abuse.* Taking your money, cards or documents. Forcing you to sign
financial documents. Opening accounts or credit in your name. Withholding money
for essentials. Taking wages or benefits. Preventing access to work or education.
Demanding receipts for every purchase. Using immigration sponsorship as leverage.
Making you financially dependent, step by step, until leaving would mean losing
everything. One person earning less, staying home, studying, living with
disability or receiving agreed support is not financial abuse; the concern is
whether access, information and freedom are deliberately restricted or used to
stop someone leaving.

**What to learn when.** You do not need to know everything in dating — you need
enough to decide whether a shared future is possible, and enough to stay safe.
*Early dating:* attitudes, general habits and obvious expectations, seen more than
heard. *Before exclusivity:* material facts affecting safety or the legitimacy of
the relationship — an existing marriage, an undisclosed dependant, an unlawful
source of income. *Before engagement:* substantial mutual disclosure of debts,
dependants, obligations, income stability and legal liabilities. *Before marriage:*
detailed planning, with independent local legal and financial advice where there
is substantial debt, property, a business, cross-border residence or a previous
marriage.

And as a general safeguard during dating: avoid merged accounts, co-signed debt,
shared property and financial dependence.

## Practical tool — Financial Warning Signs Checklist

Part A is about you. Part B records the six windows. Part C lists warning signs.
Part D is exploitation. Part E is money between you. Part F is what will be needed
before engagement. Nothing here is scored.

> **This is not a demand for the other person's statements or accounts.** It is a
> record of what you have seen and what you honestly need to know.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. Do not write account numbers,
> passwords, card details or document identifiers here — ever. If financial
> control or monitoring is present, do not record anything that could expose you;
> use a safer device or leave it blank and seek advice.
> **Finding Help Where You Live →**

> **If you are not currently dating**, Part A is the work. Your own money habits
> will be among the first things a future spouse learns about you, and it is
> better to know them first.

### Part A — My own money

- What I believe money is for:
- My typical approach to spending, saving and planning — and where that approach
  changes under stress:
- Debt I carry, why, and my plan:
- What I give, to whom, and how I feel about it:
- Obligations to family I carry or expect to carry:

### Part B — The six windows

Answer *Observed / Not yet observed / Mixed evidence / Concern observed*, with
evidence.

**Spending**
- They generally understand what they can afford, and adjust honestly when money
  is limited.
- What they spend on, over time, matches what they say they value.

**Debt**
- I know the honest outline of any significant debt — kind, cause and plan — at a
  level appropriate to where we are.
- They are honest about it rather than hiding or minimising it.

**Generosity**
- Within their means, they show generosity without using it to secure praise,
  obligation or control.
- They can say no to requests, including from family or church, when wisdom
  requires it.

**Secrecy**
- They can talk about money without evasion, at a level appropriate to the
  relationship.
- Their accounts of their finances have been consistent.
- They have not presented themselves as wealthier or poorer than they are.

**Ambition**
- Their financial ambitions broadly align with their stated values and do not
  depend on dishonesty, exploitation, reckless speculation or sacrificing
  essential responsibilities. They can explain the risks they take and accept
  appropriate limits.
- They are not drawn to fast money — schemes, chasing losses, investments they
  cannot explain.
- I have a sense of what they would and would not sacrifice for money.

**Responsibility**
- They treat debts and obligations seriously, and communicate honestly when
  difficulties arise.
- They respond responsibly to legitimate dependants within their means.
- They plan where possible, and seek help when circumstances exceed their
  capacity.

### Part C — Warning signs

Answer *Observed / Not observed / Not sure*.

- Repeated borrowing with entitlement, deception, or broken agreements.
- Pressure to lend, co-sign or guarantee.
- Concealed gambling losses, chasing losses, or gambling with essential funds.
- Debt or obligations discovered rather than disclosed.
- Spending that consistently contradicts stated values.
- Gifts used to create obligation through pressure, or money used to control.
- Treating my money as theirs, or my financial decisions as theirs to approve.
- Refusal to discuss money at all, when the relationship warrants it.

> Weigh seriousness, repetition, deception and — where the matter was safely
> clarified or addressed — their response. If raising it may expose you to
> intimidation, retaliation or further financial harm, seek individual advice
> instead. One serious incident — pressure to co-sign, concealed gambling debt,
> attempted misuse of your identity — may require immediate action and advice.
> Several lower-level concerns may reveal a pattern and a reason to slow down
> (Lesson 18).

### Part D — Exploitation

**Scam warning signs.** Answer *Yes / No*. **If any is Yes, do not send money, and
seek advice before anything else.**

- A romantic interest known only online has asked me for money, gift cards,
  cryptocurrency or account access.
- They have urged me to use an investment platform or adviser they selected.
- The opportunity involves urgency, secrecy or guaranteed returns.
- I cannot independently verify the person, company or investment.
- They become angry, romantic or threatening when I hesitate.

**Financial abuse.** Answer *Yes / No*.

- They have taken or used my money, cards or documents without my free agreement.
- I have been pressured to sign financial documents, or accounts or debt have been
  opened in my name.
- My access to my own income, essentials, work or education is controlled or
  interfered with.
- My identity documents are held or controlled by them.
- I have become financially dependent on them in ways I did not choose, and that
  would make leaving difficult.

> If any is Yes, seek confidential individual advice. Do not move or freeze money,
> change access or confront the person until you have considered whether those
> actions could increase danger.

### Part E — Money and us

- Who pays for what, and does it create obligation or score-keeping?
- How do they treat my money?
- How do I treat theirs?
- Are there gifts, in either direction, that have created obligation through
  pressure?
- Is there any lending, co-signing or shared spending between us that should not
  exist at this stage?

### Part F — Before engagement

Not for now, but so you know what is coming. Before engagement you will need
substantial and mutual honesty about debts and their plans; obligations to family;
income and its stability; legal, tax, business or support liabilities;
expectations about earning, managing, giving and supporting; and any financial
fact that would materially affect the other person's decision.

> Verification, with mutual consent, belongs there too — and it should be mutual,
> not one person auditing the other. Verification does not require shared
> passwords, unrestricted account access or copies retained without consent. It
> may mean reviewing relevant statements or documents together, obtaining
> independent credit or legal information through the proper process in each
> country, and allowing both people the same opportunity to verify material
> claims.

*Before you finish:* pray Proverbs 30:8–9 for yourself and for the person you are
considering — that neither of you would love money, fear its absence, or be
controlled by it, and that whatever you might one day hold together would be held
faithfully, in very little and in much.

## Next faithful step

Work out which stage you are actually at — early dating, approaching exclusivity,
approaching engagement — and name the one material fact appropriate to that stage
that you do not yet know. Then decide when an ordinary conversation about it would
fit, rather than asking for disclosure the stage does not warrant.

If you are not dating, answer this instead: what would you find hardest to tell a
future spouse about your money, and what would it take to be ready to say it?

## Journal reflection

1. Is there something about your money you would find hard to tell a future
   spouse? What makes it hard — shame, fear of judgement, or something you have
   not yet dealt with?
2. Do you use money — giving, paying, lending — to secure affection or approval?
   Where did that start?

## Need support?

If you have recognised exploitation rather than a warning sign — a request from
someone you have never met, a pattern of your money quietly becoming theirs, a
dependence you did not choose — put the conversation aside and get help. You are
not foolish. You offered trust, and someone chose to exploit it.

**If you have already sent money or disclosed account information**, first consider
whether the person has physical access to you, your devices or your finances. If
this may be part of coercive control, surveillance or a relationship in which you
fear retaliation, use a safer device and seek confidential specialist advice
before making changes the person may detect.

If there is no immediate personal-safety concern, stop further payments and
contact the relevant bank, card provider, transfer service, gift-card issuer or
cryptocurrency platform promptly. Ask whether the transaction can be stopped,
recalled, frozen or traced; recovery is not guaranteed. Preserve relevant
messages, receipts and profile information somewhere safe, report the account to
the platform, and report the fraud through the appropriate service in your
country. Change compromised credentials from a safe device. Be cautious of anyone
who promises recovery in exchange for another payment.

**If financial exploitation is part of a wider pattern of control**, abruptly
freezing accounts, moving money or confronting the person may increase risk. Seek
confidential domestic-abuse advice alongside financial advice before making changes
the person may detect, and use a safe device where monitoring is suspected. Where
theft or fraud has occurred without an immediate safety risk, contact the relevant
financial institution promptly. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

If money has never been discussed and the relationship is serious enough that it
should be, begin with an ordinary conversation about what each of you believes
money is for.

If you have identified a lower-level concern and can speak freely, seek an
independent perspective and then decide whether a direct conversation is
appropriate.

If the concern involves exploitation, identity misuse, coercion, surveillance or
fear of retaliation, do not begin by confronting the person; seek specialist
financial, legal or abuse support first.
