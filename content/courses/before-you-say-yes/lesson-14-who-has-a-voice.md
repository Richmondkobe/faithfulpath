# Lesson 14 — Who Should Have a Voice in Your Relationship?

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name the few people whose counsel
you actually need, ask each of them the right question, and weigh what they say
without handing them the decision.

**Key Scripture**
"Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed." — Proverbs
15:22 (ESV)

Proverbs commends counsel repeatedly, and it also shows advisers who were biased,
foolish or wrong. The value is not in the number of voices but in their wisdom,
character and knowledge. The aim is a wiser decision, not a consensus.

## Core teaching

There are two ways to get this wrong, and most people lean towards one.

The first is to tell no one. The relationship is private, or new, or complicated,
or you already suspect what people would say. So you keep it to yourself and make
one of the largest decisions of your life inside the relationship itself, while
both of you have hopes tied to the outcome.

The second is to tell everyone. Parents, friends, small group, the person who sat
next to you at church, a few hundred people online. Every opinion is gathered and
none is weighed, the advice conflicts, the relationship becomes a public event,
and you find yourself defending a decision you have not yet made to people who
were never asked to make it.

**Why you need other voices.** Attraction changes how you interpret what you see.
You cannot fully see your own relationship from inside it, any more than you can
see your own face without a mirror. Other people may notice what attraction, fear
or hope makes hard for you to see. They may also misunderstand what they observe —
which is why counsel is weighed rather than obeyed.

"The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to advice"
(Proverbs 12:15). That is not an insult to people who trust their own judgement.
It is an observation about the limits of any single point of view. Wisdom is not
having good judgement alone; it is knowing that your judgement, however good, is
partial.

And counsel is not only for warning. A friend who says *I've never seen you this
settled* is giving you information too. Some readers have been so shaped by fear
of being deceived that they seek out only the people who will find fault. That is
not discernment either.

**Whose voice, and what each one sees.**

*Parents and family* may know your history well and recognise recurring patterns
before you do. Others know very little about your adult life, or may be unsafe or
controlling. Family status alone does not establish wisdom — give weight according
to knowledge, character, freedom from prejudice, and the specificity of what they
have observed. A family blessing, freely given, is a real gift. Coerced permission
is a different thing, and no family member can supply or remove an adult's consent
to marriage.

*Pastors and church leaders* may offer valuable biblical and pastoral perspective,
particularly where they have known one or both of you over time. But pastoral
office does not automatically confer expertise in trauma, abuse, addiction, mental
health, finance or legal risk, and good pastors recognise the limits of their
competence. Two cautions. A pastor who holds confidential information about the
other person may not be free to disclose it — do not read appropriate
confidentiality as endorsement. And whatever your tradition's view of pastoral
authority, Hebrews 13:17 concerns the ordering of church life; it does not
authorise a leader to compel an adult to marry anyone. A pastor with a personal
interest in the outcome should say so, and you should weigh accordingly.

*Friends* see you most often and most ordinarily. They know what you were like
before and can tell you what has changed. Friendship brings closeness and also
partiality: some fear losing your time, others are so loyal they hesitate to
challenge you. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend" (Proverbs 27:6).

*Mentors and mature couples.* Paul asks older women to teach younger and older men
to model for younger (Titus 2:2–6) — a pattern of intergenerational formation, from
which dating counsel is a reasonable inference rather than a direct instruction.
Longevity alone is not enough: an unhealthy marriage does not become a model merely
because it endured.

*Professionals.* A suitably qualified counsellor can help untangle patterns within
their competence; financial, legal, addiction and safeguarding questions may need
different professionals. And specialist services, without hesitation, wherever
safety is in question. Prayer and observation are not substitutes for professional
help where harm is possible, and couples counselling is not the first response to
abuse.

*Their people* are not counsellors to you but witnesses. Notice, where you
naturally can, how the person is known by those who have known them long. Do not
ask their friends, pastor or family to disclose confidences, investigate them, or
give you a secret character reference.

*And God, through prayer and his word.* God is not one adviser among many;
Christians seek him first. Yet our understanding of what we believe he is saying
remains fallible, which is why Lesson 13 insisted on humility, testing and
freedom.

**What counsel can and cannot do.** It can inform you, warn you, encourage you, and
show you what you cannot see. It cannot decide for you, and it cannot consent for
you. It cannot guarantee an outcome: several wise people may support a relationship
that later fails, and one adviser may oppose one that becomes healthy. The purpose
is responsible discernment, not protection from every possible regret.

It also cannot see what only you see — the private moments, the small kindnesses,
the way you are together when nobody is watching. That matters, and you are right
to weigh what only you know. But advisers cannot see every private harm either, and
you remain responsible for telling the truth about what happens when no one else
is present.

**Choosing your counsellors.** You are not looking for a crowd, but for a few
people who are:

*Wise* — humble, sound in judgement, willing to recognise the limits of what they
know. *Honest* — they will tell you what you do not want to hear, and have done so
before. A person who has never disagreed with you is not a counsellor; they are an
audience. *Independent enough* — few personal advisers have no stake at all; the
question is whether they can separate their preferences and interests from your
decision, and will disclose a meaningful conflict. *Knowing* — personal advisers
are more useful when they know you and have naturally observed the relationship,
though professionals can advise on conduct, risk and options without ever meeting
the other person. Where abuse is possible, do not arrange a joint meeting so that
an adviser can hear both sides; meeting a skilled manipulator can produce false
reassurance. *Discreet* — they handle information carefully and explain the limits
of confidentiality, which should never be promised where safeguarding action may
be required. *Safe* — they know the difference between ordinary disagreement and
coercive control, and refer rather than mediate when they lack the expertise.

Build a small, varied circle: someone who knows your history, someone who sees your
present life, someone with mature Christian judgement, and where necessary an
appropriately qualified professional. One person may fill more than one role. Three
or four is a guideline, not a requirement. Not only people who will approve; not
only people who will not.

Rehoboam, newly crowned, took advice from the elders who had served his father and
then rejected it for the young men he had grown up with, who told him what he
wanted to hear (1 Kings 12:6–11). The narrative does not moralise, but the result
was the division of a kingdom. The pattern — asking the people who will agree with
you, and calling it counsel — is very old.

**Seeking counsel well.** Ask early, before you have decided; counsel sought
afterwards is usually a request for endorsement. Ask specific questions, and
different questions of different people. *What specific change have you noticed in
me? What conduct have you observed? Is your concern about safety, compatibility or
preference? What information might change your view? What lies outside your
expertise? Are you advising me, or asking me to obey you?*

Where the relationship is healthy and the stage appropriate, let trusted people
encounter the person naturally. Do not arrange secret tests, forced interviews, or
joint meetings where safety is in question. Do not curate the story — if you find
yourself leaving things out so that they will approve, you have already learned
something.

And keep the other person's confidences. What they disclosed to you is not yours to
hand to your counsellors. You can describe your own experience and seek help
without disclosing unnecessary details of theirs. But if someone may be in danger,
do not promise secrecy; seek specialist safeguarding advice.

**Weighing what you hear.** Specific observations deserve more weight than
unexplained impressions. *He goes quiet and cold whenever you disagree with him,
and it's happened three times in my presence* is an observation. *I just don't get
a good feeling* is an impression — though an impression of fear or unease should
not be mocked or treated as proof either way; ask what produced it.

Is it independent? Three friends who share a bias, or who talked to each other
first, are one voice, not three. Agreement does not prove accuracy — several
advisers may share the same church culture, prejudice or family interest. When
independent voices who have not compared notes name the same specific concern, take
it very seriously. When one person objects and everyone else is at ease, examine
the one; not because a lone voice is automatically prophetic, but because it may
be.

Is it about safety, or about preference? Weight concern about how you are treated
far more heavily than concern about whether the person is the sort your family
imagined. **Do not dismiss a credible safety concern by outvoting it.** Pause and
obtain specialist advice.

And remember that sincere counsellors can be wrong. Job's friends were sincere,
articulate and confident, and God said of them, "you have not spoken of me what is
right" (Job 42:7).

**When counsel becomes control.** A voice is not a vote, and it is not a veto. That
principle concerns personal authority rather than the seriousness of expertise: a
safeguarding professional cannot consent for you, but their risk assessment may
deserve far more weight than an ordinary preference.

Parents who demand approval rights; leaders who instruct rather than advise;
friends who condition friendship on your decision. On that last one, be careful in
both directions: a threat to withdraw friendship in order to force compliance may
be controlling, but a person may also set a legitimate boundary because the
relationship exposes them to danger, repeated crisis or mistreatment. Ask whether
they are trying to control your decision, or explaining what participation they can
no longer safely offer. Either way, it is a cost to count, not a command.

Honour, listen, weigh, and then decide, because the decision and its consequences
are yours. "Each will have to bear his own load" (Galatians 6:5).

**Too many voices.** The symptoms: conflicting advice you cannot reconcile; the
relationship discussed more than it is lived; the person becoming a topic among
people they have never met; paralysis; drama. The remedy is to shrink the circle.
There is a difference between informing people who care about you and announcing to
people who are merely interested. Some things are never counsel: posting
relationship disputes online, asking followers to judge a partner, sharing
screenshots or private messages, circulating confidential details through a prayer
group, or treating an AI system as your sole counsellor or safety assessor.

**Too few voices.** The symptoms: nobody has met them; nobody knows how serious it
is; you have asked no one anything; you notice you are avoiding certain people, or
certain questions. Ask why. Avoidance may mean you suspect a genuine concern and
would rather not hear it. It may also mean the available people are intrusive,
prejudiced or unsafe — ask not only why you are reluctant, but whether they have
earned access to the information. Sometimes it is the person you are dating
discouraging it, and Lesson 6 named that pattern. Sometimes it is simply isolation:
a new city, a new church, a life abroad, family far away. If it is fear of a
concern, that is the reason to ask, not the reason not to. If the available people
are unsafe, choose others. If it is isolation, build a circle deliberately, and
start with one person.

### Part A — My current circle

For each person who has a voice:

- Initials and role (parent, pastor, friend, mentor, professional):
- What do they know directly, and what only through my account?
- Possible interest or bias:
- Relevant experience or competence:
- What question am I asking this person?
- What are their confidentiality limits?
- Are they free to say *I don't know*?
- Would they tell me something I did not want to hear?

### Part B — Quality and gaps

- Which of these people are wise, honest, independent enough, knowing, discreet
  and safe? Which fall short, and on what?
- Do I have: someone who understands my history · someone who sees my life now ·
  someone with mature, humble Christian judgement · someone qualified for any
  specialist concern · someone independent of the relationship's outcome?
- Have I included only people who will approve — or only people who will not?
- Is anyone missing who should be here, and why are they missing?

### Part C — What has actually been said

For each concern or encouragement received:

- Who said it:
- What did they directly observe?
- What did I report to them?
- What inference did they make?
- What information might change their conclusion?
- Any prejudice, control or personal interest shaping it?
- About safety, or about preference?

### Part D — Weighing

- Do independent voices converge on any specific concern? What is it?
- Do they converge on any encouragement?
- Could the agreement reflect a shared culture, prejudice or interest rather than
  accuracy?
- Where one voice differs from the rest, do they offer specific evidence, relevant
  competence, and freedom from interest?
- Is there anything I have curated, softened or left out when seeking counsel?

### Part E — Too many or too few

- How many people know about this relationship, and how many have actually been
  asked for counsel?
- Is the relationship being discussed more than it is being lived?
- Has anyone I would normally ask been avoided? Is that because I suspect a
  concern, or because they have not earned access?
- If I am isolated, who could form a substitute circle, and who will I contact
  first?

### Part F — The decision

- Counsel has informed me of:
- I understand that counsel informs my decision but does not make it for me.
  *Yes / Not sure / I may be using counsel to avoid responsibility*
- I understand that the other person remains free to decide differently.
  *Yes / Not sure*
- Who may be materially affected by my decision, and what responsibilities do I
  owe them without giving them control of my consent?
- Is there a decision I am postponing by continuing to collect advice?
- Anyone who has made their relationship with me conditional on this decision —
  and whether they are trying to control me, or explaining what they can no longer
  safely offer:

### Part G — Safety

Answer *Yes / No*.

- I am afraid to tell anyone about this relationship.
- The person I am dating discourages my seeking counsel.
- The person I am dating forbids or punishes my seeking counsel.
- A trustworthy person has named a specific reason they fear for my safety.
- A leader's counsel has become pressure.
- Family pressure amounts to coercion.

If any is *Yes*:

- What exactly am I afraid will happen?
- Is it safe to contact an adviser from my usual device?
- Does the concern require a domestic-abuse, forced-marriage or safeguarding
  specialist?
- Should I avoid informing the partner or leader before receiving advice?
- Is anyone in immediate danger?

*Before you finish:* "Listen to advice and accept instruction, that you may gain
wisdom in the future" (Proverbs 19:20). Pray for the people who have a voice in
your life — that they would speak truthfully, and that you would hear them well.
Then pray for the humility to weigh what they say, and the courage to make the
decision that is yours.

## Next faithful step

**If you are dating and have told no one**, identify the safest appropriate person
to approach. If there is no coercion or safety concern, speak to them this week
about your own experience without disclosing the other person's confidences. If
abuse, forced marriage, spiritual coercion or safeguarding concerns may be present,
contact an appropriate independent specialist rather than beginning with an
ordinary adviser.

**If you are not dating**, complete Parts A and B and identify one person with whom
you could begin building an honest relationship now, before a decision is in front
of you.

**If you have told everyone**, choose the three or four whose counsel actually
meets the standard, and let the rest become people who are simply glad for you. You
do not have to announce the change.

**If your circle is already the right size**, ask one of them a better question than
you have asked before — a specific one, from the list in the teaching, about
conduct they have actually observed.

## Journal reflection

1. If your view differs from everyone else's, what evidence are you weighing that
   they do not have? Is it information you can appropriately describe without
   betraying confidence? What evidence might they be seeing that you have
   discounted?
2. Are you gathering counsel to inform a decision, or to avoid making one? Some
   people collect advice so that whoever is blamed afterwards, it will not be them.
   That is not humility, and no counsellor can carry a load that is yours.

## Need support?

These concerns come in levels and need different responses.

If the person you are dating **discourages** counsel, work out whether that is a
preference, a privacy concern, or a pattern of isolation. If they **forbid or
punish** it, that is potential coercive control, and the right first step is
individual help rather than another conversation with them.

If a **leader** is pressuring the decision, seek advice outside that leader's
influence — for example, an independent safeguarding professional, a genuinely
independent external or denominational safeguarding office, an appropriate
regulator or statutory service, or a suitably qualified counsellor unconnected to
the leader. Seniority within the same structure does not by itself establish
independence.

If **forced marriage or family coercion** may be involved, do not arrange family
mediation. If **violence, stalking or immediate danger** is present, Lesson 6's
guidance applies.

And if you are afraid to tell anyone about your relationship, that fear is the most
important thing to tell someone. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

If counsel converges on a concern, return to the concrete evidence rather than
treating the advice as a vote. Where the concern involves violence, coercion,
stalking, forced marriage, spiritual abuse or safeguarding, obtain specialist
guidance before discussing it with the person involved.

If counsel converges on encouragement, receive that too — you are allowed to be
told that something is going well.

And if the people you would normally ask are the people you have been avoiding, ask
whether that is because you fear a truth they may name or because they have not
proved safe, fair or discreet.
