# Lesson 15 — Can We Build a Life Together?

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to sort the real differences between
you into three groups — liveable, needing agreement before engagement, and
incompatible — and name what each of you would be giving up to build this life.

**Key Scripture**
"By wisdom a house is built, and by understanding it is established." — Proverbs
24:3 (ESV)

A proverb about households in general, not about choosing a spouse. But the
sequence is the point: wisdom builds, understanding establishes. Neither word
describes a feeling. Both describe knowing what you are working with before you
start.

## Core teaching

Lesson 2 asked whether the two of you share a foundation. This lesson asks whether
you could build a house on it.

The distinction matters, because Christians sometimes collapse the two. *We're both
believers, we pray together, our pastor approves* is a foundation. It is not a
house. Two people can share a real faith in Christ and still want different lives
— different countries, different paces, different pictures of family, different
ways of handling money, conflict and time.

**Compatibility is not sameness.** It does not mean finding someone like you.
Genesis describes the first companion as "a helper fit for him" (Genesis 2:18) —
the Hebrew carries the sense of one who corresponds to him, a counterpart rather
than a copy. The Preacher observes that "two are better than one" precisely because
when one falls, the other lifts him up (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10): the value is in the
difference, not the duplication.

So the question is not *are we alike?* It is *can we build one life out of two,
with the differences we actually have?* That has two parts: what the differences
are, and whether the two of you can handle differences at all.

**How two people handle differences often determines whether ordinary differences
remain workable.** It cannot make mutually exclusive futures compatible. Excellent
communication may clarify an incompatibility without resolving it, and that clarity
is still valuable. Two people who differ on many things but can talk honestly,
repair conflict and give way to each other will generally build well. Two people
who agree on nearly everything but cannot disagree without punishment will struggle
at the first real difference — and there is always a first real difference. Lesson
7's green flags are the evidence here: mutuality, honest disagreement, repair.

**Seven areas.**

**Personality and temperament.** Introvert or extrovert, planner or improviser,
expressive or reserved, needs order or thrives in mess. None is right or wrong, and
opposites often attract for good reason. The question is whether the differences
complement or grind. Does their energy restore you or exhaust you, over time and
not only on a good day? Can each of you give the other what they need without
becoming someone else?

**Communication and conflict.** How each of you raises a concern, hears one,
handles anger, needs space, comes back. A person who processes out loud and a
person who needs silence can build a life together, but only if each understands
the other's way and neither reads it as rejection. This is where *process
compatibility* lives, and it is worth a great deal — though it is not a substitute
for agreement on the things that cannot be split.

**Calling and direction.** What does each of you believe your life is for, and
could those two purposes be lived in the same house? A calling to overseas mission
and a calling to care for ageing parents in a home town are both good. They are not
obviously compatible. Lesson 3 distinguished sharing a calling from supporting one;
ask honestly which this would be, and whether the support could be given gladly for
decades rather than resentfully for years.

**Location.** Where you would live, and near whom. Country, city or countryside,
distance from each family, willingness to move. This sounds practical and it is,
but it is also one of the most common causes of quiet, long-term unhappiness,
because it is so often assumed rather than discussed. *I always thought we'd end up
back home* is a sentence that should be said before engagement, not after.

**Expectations of marriage.** Who works, who manages money, how decisions are made,
what roles look like, how much time together and apart. Compare pictures. Where
they differ, is the difference one of preference, which can be negotiated, or of
conviction, which may not be? Two people with opposed convictions about household
roles may both be sincere Christians and still be unable to build the same
marriage.

**Children.** Whether each of you hopes or intends to have children; convictions
about raising them; thoughts about timing and family size without treating either
as guaranteed; how you would respond to infertility, pregnancy loss, disability or
an unexpected pregnancy; and responsibilities towards children either person
already has. Some of this belongs to engagement. But the first question — *whether*
— and the outline of the rest belong before engagement, because they are among the
few differences that cannot be split down the middle.

**Lifestyle.** Pace, rest, food, health, hospitality, money habits, how a weekend
goes, how much company, how much solitude. These are small until they are daily,
and then they are everything. A person who needs quiet evenings and a person who
needs a full house every night will each feel, in time, that the other is asking
them to live a life that is not theirs.

**Sorting the differences.** Once you can see them, sort them into three groups.

*Liveable* — differences that will be part of the texture of your marriage, that
both of you can accept and may come to value. Temperament. Many lifestyle
preferences. Different ways of resting, celebrating, showing affection. These need
patience and humour, not resolution.

*Needing agreement before engagement* — differences that cannot simply be lived
with and must be settled, not perfectly but substantially, before you commit.
Children. Location. Household roles. Family obligations. Money expectations. The
direction of your lives. *We'll work it out later* is not sufficient here.

*Incompatible* — differences that cannot both be lived in one shared future and
that neither person can freely and honestly commit to changing now. Do not move an
item out of this category merely because one person might change later. These are
the hardest to admit, because they can exist between two good people who love each
other. Lesson 16 is about exactly that. Here, only this: **an incompatibility is not
a failure of love.** It is information about what a shared life would cost, and
honesty about it now is a kindness to both of you.

Be careful in the sorting. Attraction wants to move everything into the first
group. Fear wants to move everything into the third. Neither is honest.

One clarification before you sort. **A difference is not a red flag, and a red flag
is not a difference.** Incompatibility means two people want lives that cannot both
be lived. It does not describe conduct that is deceptive, controlling or unsafe,
and nothing in Lesson 6 belongs in any of these three groups. If something from
that lesson is present, this sorting is the wrong tool for it.

**Counting the cost.** Jesus asked which of you, intending to build a tower, does
not "first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it" (Luke
14:28). He was speaking of the cost of discipleship rather than of marriage — but
the image of sitting down before building is exactly right.

Counting the cost means asking what each of you would be giving up, and whether the
choice is genuinely voluntary. Every marriage costs something. A person who moves
country for a spouse, or sets aside a career path, or takes on a partner's family
obligations, has counted a cost and paid it, and many are glad they did. **The
danger is not cost. It is uncounted cost** — the sacrifice that was never named,
that one person assumed the other had accepted, and that surfaces years later as
resentment.

Counting a cost does not mean every sacrifice is wise. A marriage cannot be built
healthily by requiring one person to erase their identity, conscience, safety or
essential responsibilities.

**What this lesson is not.** It is not the detailed premarital work that engaged
couples need. It asks a prior question: is there enough here to justify those
conversations?

And it is not a guarantee. No amount of compatibility ensures a marriage will
thrive. Compatibility is the ground; faithfulness, repair, patience and grace are
what builds on it. Two very compatible people can neglect a marriage into ruin. Two
people with real differences can build something beautiful by attending to it
daily.

## Practical tool — Life Compatibility Reflection

Part A is for each of you alone. Part B is for the two of you. Part C sorts what
you find. Part D counts the cost. Part E is where the reflection leaves you.
Nothing here is scored.

> **Complete Part B only in a relationship where both of you can speak freely and
> safely.** If you are afraid to disagree, to say *not yet*, or to seek counsel, do
> not use this conversation to find out what will happen. Seek individual guidance
> first. If safety is in question, Lesson 6 comes first.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

> **If you are early in a relationship**, this tool is for later. Read the seven
> areas, watch them as ordinary life reveals them, and come back when there is
> something real to sort.

### Part A — Alone

For each area, write what you know about yourself, what you have observed about
them, and the difference, if any.

**Personality and temperament** — Me. · Them. · Difference. · Does their energy
restore or exhaust me over time?

**Communication and conflict** — How I raise, hear and repair. · How they do. · Have
I seen us disagree and repair? What happened?

**Calling and direction** — What I believe my life is for. · What they believe
theirs is for. · Could these be lived in one house? Would I be sharing their
calling or supporting it — and could I do so gladly for decades?

**Location** — Where I expect to live, and near whom. · Where they do. · Any
assumption I have not said out loud.

**Expectations of marriage** — My picture: roles, money, decisions, time. · Theirs.
· Where we differ, is it preference or conviction?

**Children** — Whether each of us hopes or intends to have children; convictions
about raising them; thoughts about timing and family size without treating either
as guaranteed; how we would respond to infertility, pregnancy loss, disability or
an unexpected pregnancy; and responsibilities towards children either of us already
has.

**Lifestyle** — Pace, rest, company, solitude, food, health, weekends. · Theirs. ·
The one daily mismatch I would find hardest.

### Part B — Together

Share what each of you wrote, area by area. For each:

- Where we agree:
- Where we differ:
- Whether the difference is preference or conviction:
- Whether either of us has been assuming the other had accepted something we never
  said:

> Notice, as you talk, whether you can disagree and stay connected. That is
> important evidence about how you handle differences. It does not by itself
> resolve the substance of the difference.

### Part C — Sorting

Place every difference into one of three groups.

- **Liveable** — part of the texture of our marriage; requires patience, not
  resolution:
- **Needing agreement before engagement** — must be substantially settled, not
  deferred:
- **Incompatible** — cannot both be lived in the same future without one person
  surrendering something central, and no freely chosen resolution presently
  exists:

Then: what would a wise outside voice say about this sorting? Lesson 14's circle is
the place to ask.

### Part D — Counting the cost

- What I would freely be choosing to relinquish, change or carry:
- What they say they would freely be choosing — not what I assume for them:
- Have we each named the practical and emotional consequences?
- Is the choice genuinely voluntary, or is either person agreeing mainly from fear
  of losing the relationship?
- Could I take responsibility for this choice without treating the other person as
  permanently indebted to me?
- What might make the cost unsustainable, and what support would be needed?

### Part E — Where this leaves me

- Differences we both presently regard as liveable:
- Matters requiring substantial agreement before engagement:
- Anything one or both of us presently regard as incompatible:
- What remains unclear:
- What wise, independent counsel has contributed:

The next appropriate path I am choosing:

- Continue discerning without moving towards engagement.
- Have one necessary conversation.
- Seek independent counsel.
- Slow down and allow more evidence to emerge.
- Acknowledge a possible incompatibility and continue to Lesson 16.
- I am not ready to choose a path yet.

> No number of items determines the outcome. One unresolved difference about
> children, location or calling may matter more than several ordinary lifestyle
> differences. The purpose is to name the life being proposed and what it would
> require — not to make the worksheet decide.

*Before you finish:* pray for the wisdom of Proverbs 24:3 — not for the differences
to disappear, but for the honesty to see them, the humility to sort them
truthfully, and the discernment to know which house can be built and which cannot.

## Next faithful step

Choose one material assumption that has not been spoken aloud. If both of you can
speak freely and safely, arrange an appropriate time to discuss it; do not
introduce a life-changing issue casually or at the end of an already difficult
conversation.

If you are not able to say it — if naming what you would be giving up would bring
pressure, guilt or punishment — do not treat this as a step to complete. That
difficulty is the finding, and it belongs in the support section below.

## Journal reflection

1. Did you move anything into *liveable* because you could not bear for it to be
   anywhere else? Or into *incompatible* out of fear rather than evidence?
2. Is the life you would build one that both of you actually want — or one that one
   of you has quietly agreed to for the sake of the other?

## Need support?

This lesson assumes two people who can each say what they want from a life and be
heard. If naming a difference brings pressure, guilt, withdrawal or punishment, the
problem is not compatibility and this is not the tool for it. Lesson 6 describes
what that pattern is, and the answer is individual guidance rather than a better
conversation.

If what surfaced here is something you have known for a long time and have been
unable to say — a country you cannot leave, a conviction you cannot set down, a
certainty about children — you may need help saying it more than you need help
deciding. A suitably qualified counsellor or a wise, independent person can help
with that. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

You do not need to conclude today.

Do not judge the situation by how many differences appear in each group; consider
their substance. Several ordinary differences may be workable, while one mutually
exclusive future may be decisive.

If a material issue remains unresolved, identify whether it needs conversation,
counsel, time or an honest acknowledgement of incompatibility. Lesson 16 will help
with the last of those without assuming what you must decide.
