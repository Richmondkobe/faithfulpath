# Lesson 17 — When to Continue With Wisdom

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name what kind of difficulty you
are actually facing, recognise what makes one workable, and choose to continue
deliberately rather than by drifting.

**Key Scripture**
"Bearing with one another in love." — Ephesians 4:2 (ESV)

Paul is describing life in the church, not in a dating relationship, and the
virtues he names — humility, gentleness, patience — apply wherever imperfect people
share life. Note what the phrase does not mean. Bearing with is making room for
what is imperfect in someone. It is not carrying another adult's responsibilities,
concealing harm, or accepting conduct that destroys safety or dignity.

## Core teaching

Most of this course has been about seeing clearly. This module is about what to do
once you have seen.

There are three honest answers to *should we continue?* — yes, not yet, and no. Each
is done badly in a different way. This lesson is about yes: not a yes that closes
its eyes, but one given with them open.

Lessons 15 and 16 may have left some readers uneasy. Some of you recognised your
relationship there, and Lesson 19 is for you. But many of you thought *that is not
us — but we do have problems*, and then wondered whether the problems were the kind
that should end things. Many difficulties between people of sound character are
ordinary friction. Others reveal a substantial incompatibility, an untreated
personal pattern, or a safety concern. This lesson helps you tell which is actually
present.

**Continuing is a decision, not a drift.** Many relationships are never actually
decided; they simply go on, because ending would take a conversation nobody wants
and marrying would take one nobody is ready for. Months pass. The relationship
becomes a habit, then an assumption, then a wedding. Nobody ever said yes; they
just never said no.

A wise yes is deliberate. It says: I have looked at what is difficult, I have named
it, I believe it can be carried or changed, and I am choosing to keep going on that
basis. The difference matters because a deliberate yes can be reviewed. A drift
cannot — there is nothing to review, only a growing sense of obligation.

**Four kinds of difficulty.** Not every problem is the same problem, and treating
them alike wastes time and sometimes causes harm.

*A skill difficulty.* You both want the same outcome and lack a method. You both
want to handle conflict well and are handling it badly. Skill difficulties generally
respond to effort, because the destination is shared; what is missing is a way of
getting there.

*External strain.* Health, grief, work, immigration, family or financial pressure is
bearing on one or both of you, and the relationship is absorbing the weight. This is
not a fault in the relationship, but it distorts every conversation about it. Name
the strain before treating its effects as a verdict.

*A personal pattern.* One or both of you brings an established pattern — with anger,
withdrawal, fear, dishonesty, money, substances — that has appeared before and is
now appearing here. Personal patterns are not usually solved inside a dating
relationship, and it is not the other person's job to solve them. They call for
individual work, and often professional help.

*A directional incompatibility.* You want materially different lives or marriages.
This is Lesson 15's third group and Lesson 16's whole subject. It does not respond
to effort the way a skill difficulty does, because no amount of trying harder
changes what a person actually wants.

**A category explains what kind of response may be appropriate; it does not excuse
harm.** External strain does not make intimidation or cruelty acceptable. Calling
anger, dishonesty, substance use or controlling conduct a "personal pattern" must
not downgrade a safety concern into a self-improvement project. Where fear,
coercion, violence or loss of freedom is present, Lesson 6 remains the correct
route.

The categories overlap and a difficulty can move between them. A conflict may begin
with external strain, expose a personal pattern, and eventually reveal a directional
difference the strain had been hiding. Sort it now, work on it, and sort it again at
the review.

**What makes a difficulty workable.** Three features: **it can be named specifically
by both people, both people are willing to address it, and there is some evidence
that honest effort produces change.**

*Named specifically.* "We fight a lot" is not a named difficulty. "When one of us is
hurt, we both withdraw and the silence lasts for days" is. Specificity matters
because you cannot work on a fog. It also separates a difficulty from a label:
*they lied about debt three times* is specific; *they are a terrible person* is a
verdict, and verdicts cannot be worked on. Specificity does not require pretending
every problem is equally shared — a problem may affect both people without being
caused equally by both, and naming it honestly may mean naming one person's conduct.

*Mutually addressed.* This is the feature most often missing. One person sees the
problem, names it, prays over it, raises it — and the other agrees it is a problem,
in the sense that they do not argue, and then nothing changes because they never
took it up as their own. Lesson 16 warned about the person who is always adapting.
Here the concern is the person who is always working. A relationship in which one
person carries all the effort is not being repaired; it is being managed, and the
manager will eventually run out.

Mutual does not mean equal responsibility. If one person repeatedly lies, the
other's task is not to accept half the blame for the dishonesty. Their part may be
to state clearly what honesty requires, and to decide what they will do if it
remains absent.

*Evidence of change.* Not a promise; evidence. Promises are cheap at the point of
crisis and expensive to keep afterwards. Look for something that actually happened
differently — the silence lasted a day instead of four, the apology came without
being extracted. Ordinary, repeated, small change is far better evidence than one
grand gesture, because it shows the person can do the thing without an audience.

One qualification: when a difficulty has only recently been identified, absence of
evidence is not evidence of unwillingness. Nobody can show sustained change in a
problem they learned about last week. If both people freely choose to continue,
agree a specific response and a review period in which change could reasonably
become visible. Neither person owes the relationship that period, and either may
reconsider sooner. Do not move towards engagement during it merely on the strength
of good intentions.

Where all three features are present, you have encouraging evidence that a
difficulty may be workable. **That is not a verdict that the relationship should
continue.** You must still consider the whole of it — shared faith, character,
compatibility, attraction, freedom, counsel and safety.

**What movement looks like.** A workable difficulty worked at does not simply get
better in a straight line. Some need several approaches before improvement shows,
and the first thing you try may not be the right one. Movement is usually uneven:
better for a month, a setback, then better again.

Conversely, brief improvement immediately after threatened consequences is not
necessarily sustained change. Someone who behaves well for three weeks after *I will
leave if this continues* has shown they can behave well under threat. That is not
nothing, and it is not the same as change. It is one of the ways an unsafe
relationship can appear to be repairing itself.

So the evidence is a cluster, not a single measure: the relationship is more honest,
respectful and free than it was; the problem is recognised earlier, by both of you;
recovery afterwards is quicker; the change has continued outside a crisis, over a
period suited to the problem; the burden on the other person has reduced; and where
help is needed, there is willingness to seek it.

Evaluate the quality and mutuality of effort, not merely its existence. **Two people
trying hard in a relationship that leaves both of them lonelier is not a repair in
progress.**

**Continuing well.** *Say it out loud, together.* Tell the other person what you
have decided, what you have named, and what you hope will change — and let them do
the same. This is not a secret performance review or a disguised ultimatum. A
relationship in which one person is privately assessing the other is not a
partnership.

*Agree what you are working on.* One or two priorities, not everything. Couples who
try to fix six things at once fix none and conclude that effort does not work.

*Agree what change would look like* — not perfection, something observable. But
observable does not mean identical for every couple: a person living with trauma,
neurodivergence, illness or a demanding schedule may need a different pace, and a
measure that ignores that becomes a stick rather than a guide.

*Get appropriate help*, matched to the problem: a pastor for spiritual support, a
suitably qualified counsellor for relational patterns, an addiction professional for
substance use, a financial professional for serious debt, a legal adviser for
immigration or custody, a specialist service for coercion or danger. **The dating
partner must never become the other person's treatment programme.**

*Keep your counsel informed.* People who decide to continue often withdraw from the
friends and mentors who raised concerns, because it is uncomfortable to keep seeing
them. Do the opposite. But do not ask friends to monitor, test or report on the
person you are dating. They are not a community project.

*Set a review point suited to the problem.* A yes that is never reviewed becomes a
drift. Some communication habits show movement within weeks; recovery, financial
repair or longstanding personal patterns take much longer. A review date exists to
prevent indefinite waiting, not to create an artificial deadline. And do not become
engaged while a major unresolved concern is still being examined. Engagement is not
proof that a problem has been solved.

**When continuing is the wrong decision.** The desire to make things work is one of
the most easily misused instincts a Christian has.

Do not continue **because ending feels like giving up.** Covenant faithfulness calls
married people to persevere through ordinary hardship, and it never makes violence,
coercion or abuse spiritually acceptable. In dating, perseverance must not be
confused with an obligation to turn every relationship into a marriage. Before the
vows, stopping is not giving up.

Do not continue **because you have already invested so much.** Years, money, a
shared church, a public relationship — none of it obligates you to invest more. The
question is never what it has cost; it is what it is.

Do not continue **on the strength of a promise**, or **because a serious problem is
only acknowledged when a breakup is threatened** and the help it requires is then
repeatedly refused. Acknowledgement that exists to prevent an ending is not the
same as taking responsibility.

Do not continue **because you are afraid there will be no one else.** Lesson 20 is
for that fear. It is a real fear, and it is not a reason.

And do not use this lesson at all if what you face is not a workable difficulty but
harm. Where there is fear, coercion, controlling behaviour, intimidation, violence,
or a sustained pattern of degrading contempt, **do not use the language of working
on it to rename harm as a shared repair project.** That language is exactly what
keeps people in danger. Repair assumes two people of goodwill facing a shared
problem. Where that assumption fails, this lesson does not apply.

**A word on grace.** All of this may sound like a project plan, and there is a
reason: relationships that continue well tend to be ones where both people are
willing to be concrete. But a relationship is not a problem to be solved, and enough
work does not make any pairing workable.

The reason to continue, when continuing is right, is not that the difficulties have
been engineered away. It is that you have seen the other person clearly — their
faults, their habits, the friction between your two lives — and you still choose
them, and they still choose you, and both of you are willing to keep being changed.

## Practical tool — Continue, Repair or Reconsider Worksheet

> **For ordinary difficulties where both people can speak freely.** Not appropriate
> where there has been violence, sexual coercion, intimidation, stalking, financial
> exploitation, or fear of the other person's reaction. One serious incident may be
> enough to make Lesson 6 — not this worksheet — the correct starting point.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

Parts A to D alone. Part E considers the whole relationship. Part F is where you
name what you are choosing. Part G is completed with the other person, if you can be
honest together. Nothing here is scored.

### Part A — What I am continuing despite

Describe observable conduct or a specific incompatibility rather than labelling the
person.

- What happened, in one or two sentences:
- When it most recently happened:
- What each of us did:

> This does not assume that responsibility was equal or shared. Record each person's
> conduct accurately rather than dividing blame for the sake of balance.

- What effect it had on each of us:
- How serious it was:
- Is it repeated? How often?
- Does it involve deception, or a loss of anyone's freedom?
- Is anyone afraid? *(If yes, stop here and go to Lesson 6.)*
- If it was raised safely, what happened? If it was not raised, was fear of the
  response part of the reason?
- Does the other person recognise it as a difficulty, in their own words?

### Part B — What kind of difficulty is this?

Tick all that apply. More than one is normal.

- **Skill difficulty** — do we share the goal but lack the method?
- **External strain** — is pressure from outside the relationship driving this?
- **Personal pattern** — whose pattern is it, what effect does it have on the other
  person, and what individual or specialist help does it require?
- **Directional incompatibility** — do we want materially different lives or
  marriages?

> If you ticked directional incompatibility, Lessons 15 and 16 are the place for it
> rather than this worksheet.

### Part C — Is it being worked on together?

- Did we agree on specific responsibilities? What were they?
- Has each person followed through?
- Does one person require repeated reminders?
- Is the emotional and practical burden reasonably shared — not necessarily equal,
  but reasonable given whose conduct is in question?
- Can either of us admit when the agreed approach is not working?

> Taking action after an agreed request is valid effort. It does not always have to
> be spontaneous.

### Part D — What has actually changed?

- What has happened differently?
- Has the improvement continued outside a crisis, or only after consequences were
  threatened?
- Has it reduced the effect on the other person?
- Is the change visible without one person monitoring constantly?
- Have setbacks been acknowledged and repaired?
- Is the pace of change reasonable for the seriousness of the problem?
- Is the relationship more honest, respectful and free than before?
- What has not changed at all?

> One instance of change is an encouraging beginning, not sufficient evidence. If
> the difficulty is newly identified and there has not yet been time for change to
> show, record that change has not yet had time to become visible. If both people
> freely continue, identify what response has been agreed, what meaningful movement
> might look like and when the situation will be reviewed. The purpose is clarity,
> not continuous monitoring or a secret test.

### Part E — The whole relationship

A workable difficulty is not a reason to continue on its own. Mark each
*Yes / Not sure / No*.

- Our shared spiritual foundation remains intact.
- No serious safety concern has been identified.
- Our core life direction remains compatible.
- Attraction and freely chosen desire remain present.
- I can say no to this person without being punished for it.
- I remain connected to independent people.
- Neither of us is treating engagement as proof that the problem is solved.
- The relationship contains enjoyment and friendship, not only repair work.

> Any *No* needs attention before anything below, whatever Parts A to D showed. And
> note what this section is not: strengths do not offset harm. **Green flags are not
> credits that cancel violence, coercion, stalking, serious deception or control.**

### Part F — Where this leaves me

Describe your situation in your own words, then name the path you are choosing now.

- What I am continuing despite, if I am continuing:
- What kind of difficulty it is, and whose work it requires:
- What I have actually seen change, and what I have not:
- What Part E raised that still needs attention:
- What wise, independent counsel has contributed:

Then choose one:

- **Continue, and work on it.**
- **Continue without increasing commitment**, with a review point and no movement
  towards engagement until the concern is clearer. *(Lesson 18)*
- **Slow the relationship down.** *(Lesson 18)*
- **Reconsider the relationship.** *(Lesson 16, then Lesson 19)*
- **Seek specialist support before deciding anything.**
- **I am not ready to choose a path yet.**

> No combination of answers produces this decision. One unaddressed personal pattern
> or directional difference may matter more than several encouraging signs.
> Continuing today creates no obligation to continue at the review, and either
> person may reconsider sooner if new information, an incompatibility or a safety
> concern arises.

### Part G — If continuing, the agreement

With the other person, where both of you can speak freely and safely.

- The one or two things we are working on:
- What each of us is responsible for:
- What support each of us needs, and from whom:
- Which trusted adviser or qualified professional, if any, will support our work —
  and what may appropriately be shared with them:
- What would indicate meaningful progress:
- What would indicate that reconsideration is necessary:
- The review date, suited to the problem:
- What commitments we will not make before that review:

*Before you finish:* continuing with wisdom is not the same as continuing with
certainty, and you will not have certainty. What you can have is honesty — with the
other person, with your counsel, with yourself. Ask God for the humility to see your
own part in what is difficult, the courage to name what is not yours, and the
patience to bear with ordinary weakness without carrying what is not yours to carry.

## Next faithful step

If you are continuing, say so out loud to the person you are continuing with — what
you have decided, what you have named, and what you hope will change — and let them
say the same. A decision they never heard is not a shared agreement; it is a private
assessment, and it will not survive contact with the first setback.

Where you cannot have that conversation safely or freely, do not have it. That
difficulty is itself the finding, and the support section below is the right place
for it.

## Journal reflection

1. Have you ever actually chosen this relationship, or have you only never ended it?
   If you are choosing it, what are you choosing it despite?
2. If you stopped raising the difficulty, would it still be worked on?

## Need support?

Effort is available to you. It is never owed. Nobody has to try something first in
order to earn the right to say no, and no worksheet in this course creates an
obligation to keep going.

If what you are facing is harm rather than a workable difficulty — fear, coercion,
control, intimidation, violence, or sustained contempt — this lesson is the wrong
tool, and repair language will make it harder to see that clearly. Begin with a
specialist abuse service for individual guidance rather than a joint conversation or
couples counselling.

If a personal pattern is the difficulty and it is yours, individual help is the right
route and it is a good thing to seek. If it is theirs, you may support them and you
cannot do the work for them. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

You do not need certainty to continue.

If both of you can freely name an ordinary difficulty, accept appropriate
responsibility and work on it without fear or coercion, that is encouraging evidence
— not certification that the relationship should continue. Consider it alongside the
whole relationship.

If you choose to proceed, set an appropriate review point, keep independent counsel
available and return honestly when that point arrives. Lesson 18 provides the
structure, whether you are formally slowing down or simply reviewing progress.
