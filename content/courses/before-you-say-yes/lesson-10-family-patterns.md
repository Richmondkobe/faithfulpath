# Lesson 10 — Family Patterns, Loyalties and Boundaries

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to describe what your own family
taught you about how relationships work, recognise what the other person's family
expects of a marriage, and sort a family objection into the kind it actually is.

**Key Scripture**
"Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife,
and they shall become one flesh." — Genesis 2:24 (ESV)

*Leaving* has been read very differently across cultures, and the difference
matters. In extended-family settings it does not mean cutting off; it means the
new household now comes first in loyalty and decision while the wider family
remains close and honoured. In more individual settings the danger runs the other
way, and leaving can shade into abandoning.

## Core teaching

You do not marry a family. But you marry into one, and you marry someone who was
formed by one. Both of those facts will be in the room for the rest of your life,
and it is better to look at them now, while you are still deciding, than to
discover them at the first family gathering after the wedding.

This lesson is written for readers across very different cultures. In some,
families are involved from the first conversation and marriage joins two
households as much as two people. In others, families are consulted late or barely
at all. Scripture was written within cultures where families were deeply involved
in marriage, yet it also records meaningful expressions of personal willingness.
It does not give us a single modern courtship system. Whatever the culture, family
participation should not erase the free and responsible consent of the two people
marrying.

**Patterns: what a family teaches.** Everyone learned how relationships work
before they were old enough to choose what to learn. How conflict was handled —
shouting, silence, negotiation, or never at all. How affection was shown, or
whether it was. How money was talked about. Who made decisions. What faith looked
like at home as opposed to at church. What was kept secret, and from whom. What
brought honour and what brought shame.

These lessons often become our default response, though they are not our destiny.
Later relationships, culture, discipleship, counselling and deliberate practice
can reinforce, challenge or transform them. Some people repeat their family's
patterns. Some react against them so hard that they land in the opposite ditch.
Most do some of each, and the parts they have never examined are the parts that
will surprise a spouse.

So the question is not *was it a good family?* A warm family is not automatically
a green flag and a difficult one is not automatically a red one. Someone from a
loving home may have absorbed patterns that will not work in the marriage you
would build. Someone from a painful home may have looked hard at what they were
given, kept what was good, and deliberately set down the rest. The question is
whether they know what they were taught, and what they have decided to do with it.

Three questions get at this. What did they take from their family, and do they
know it? What did they reject, and have they replaced it with something or merely
with its absence? And what have they not looked at yet? That third one is what a
marriage will eventually ask, whether or not anyone asks it now.

Two cautions. **Insight alone does not prove change** — a person may describe their
family pattern accurately and still repeat it. Look for self-awareness *and*
evidence of a different practice. And **family examination should not become
fault-finding.** Ask what each family did well and what deserves carrying forward:
hospitality, perseverance, generosity, shared faith, respect for elders, care for
children, the ability to repair after conflict. A healthy household is not built
only by rejecting harmful patterns but by recognising inherited strengths.

The same questions apply to you. You cannot see another person's patterns clearly
while your own are invisible to you.

**Loyalties: whom they belong to.** Scripture calls God's people to honour father
and mother (Exodus 20:12; Ephesians 6:2). That honour endures, though it does not
take the same form in every season. Adult honour does not necessarily mean
obedience, unlimited access, financial compliance, or remaining exposed to abuse.
But it is real, and in much of the world it carries obligations Western readers
may not immediately think of: financial support for parents, care in old age,
responsibility for younger siblings' schooling, contributions to the wider family.

Paul's words to Timothy, in the context of caring for widows within a household,
are strong: "if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for
members of his household, he has denied the faith" (1 Timothy 5:8). The particular
form that responsibility takes is shaped by culture, resources and circumstances.
Not every request from a relative carries the authority of a biblical command, and
the distinction matters.

Note that **dating does not yet create the primary loyalty of marriage.** A
boyfriend or girlfriend should not demand spousal priority, financial access or
authority before a marriage exists. At this stage the question is whether each
person appears capable of forming that new loyalty if they eventually marry.

Some signs of imbalance, in both directions. They cannot make a decision,
including about the relationship, without their parents' approval — not
consultation, approval. Their family's hostility towards you is dismissed or
quietly enabled and they will not address it. They are hiding the relationship
from their family, and the reason is not safety. Or, the other way: they are
entirely estranged. Be careful here. Distance from family may be necessary, and
nobody owes a dating partner detailed disclosure of trauma before they are ready.
As trust grows the relationship will eventually need enough truthful information
to understand how the situation could affect a shared future — but *I am not ready
to give details; the distance is necessary for my wellbeing* may be an honest and
sufficient answer at an early stage, and a person who has left an abusive home
owes you no reconciliation with it.

Genesis 24 belongs to a very different social world and is not a courtship
blueprint. But the narrative deliberately records Rebekah being asked, "Will you
go with this man?" and answering, "I will go" (24:58). Her stated willingness
matters inside a process in which the family was deeply involved. That balance is
a good one to look for, in their family and in yours.

**Boundaries: protecting what the two of you are building.** In dating you are not
making these decisions yet. You are finding out whether the two of you could make
them together.

*Who decides, in the couple?* Can you decide together while seeking counsel
appropriately, or is a parent given authority whenever disagreement arises? *What
flows outward?* How much of the relationship's private life will each of you share
with family — conflicts, finances, the state of the marriage? In some families
everything is shared and privacy reads as secrecy; in others nothing is, and
openness reads as weakness. Two people from those families will need to talk.
*What flows inward?* Financial support to family is honourable, but a spouse
should not discover after the wedding that a third of the household income is
already committed. Living arrangements — parents or siblings in the home,
expectations of return to a home village — should be known rather than assumed.
*And children, if there are children:* which languages, customs and traditions to
pass on, where you would worship, how grandparents would participate.

You may not need every operational detail while dating. But before engagement you
need substantial agreement about children, residence, financial obligations,
family involvement and how final decisions will be made. *We will work it out
later* is not sufficient where the underlying expectations are already opposed.

**Meeting the family.** Notice how the person you are dating treats their parents
and siblings, and how they are treated — but be fair: one visit shows you one day,
cultures express warmth very differently, and a family under the strain of meeting
a stranger is not at its most natural. Notice the ordinary tone of established
relationships, as it naturally appears, without inviting relatives to disclose
private information or evaluate the person behind their back. Notice how they
respond to you: welcome is a gift, coolness may be prejudice, protectiveness,
culture or simple reserve, and you cannot tell which from one meeting. Hostility
is worth naming to the person you are dating, and their response to it matters
greatly, although severe hostility may also carry consequences that cannot be
solved by the couple's goodwill alone. And notice how they behave around their
family — many adults temporarily return to familiar roles, and the question is
whether that is a harmless shift in tone or whether their adult judgement and
ability to hold boundaries disappear.

Do the same honestly about your own family, and about yourself in their presence.

**When families object.** Five kinds, and an objection may belong to more than one.
Ask which part is supported by evidence and which is shaped by fear, prejudice or
control.

*Specific conduct or safety concerns* — they have watched you with this person and
are worried about something concrete. Listen carefully. Families are sometimes
wrong, but they are often the first to see a pattern that attraction has hidden
from you. Ask exactly what they noticed.

*Practical concerns* — finances, distance, caregiving, immigration, education,
health, readiness. These may be right or wrong, but they are about facts, and
facts can be examined together.

*Theological or conscience concerns* — a denominational objection is not
automatically prejudice. It may conceal prejudice, or it may concern genuine
differences about worship, baptism, children or church authority. Examine its
substance.

*Prejudice or status concerns* — objection to ethnicity, tribe, nationality,
caste, class, disability or family background, treated as inferiority, with no
concern about character or conduct. Prejudice is not evidence that the
relationship itself is wrong. It is, however, a serious cost to evaluate. The
partner's willingness and ability to protect the future household, maintain
appropriate boundaries and refuse mistreatment matter greatly. You are not
required to enter years of hostility merely to prove that the prejudice is unjust.

*Control* — opposition rooted in a demand to make the adult child's decision: any
choice they did not make, any independence, any relationship at all. An adult may
honour parents through listening, gratitude, care and respectful speech without
granting them final authority over whom to marry. Ephesians 6 addresses children
within a household; marriage establishes a new adult household. Someone who has
never been allowed to make a decision their parents disapproved of may find that
this relationship is the first place it happens, and that is a hard place for it
to happen. Be patient with each other, and get outside help.

**Customs, and a line that must be clear.** Where a family's consent is required by
custom, or where bridewealth, dowry or formal engagement ceremonies carry real
weight, the dating stage is when to find out what is expected and whether you and
your family could honour it. Those conversations belong properly to engagement,
but a couple who discover at engagement that the two families' expectations are
irreconcilable have found out too late.

And be clear what such customs cannot do. **No payment transfers ownership of a
person or removes anyone's right to refuse or leave.** An arranged marriage and a
forced marriage are not the same: in an arranged marriage families may lead
introductions and negotiations, but both people remain free to accept or refuse.
Where meaningful refusal is removed through threats, violence, confinement, shame,
financial deprivation or immigration pressure, the arrangement has become
coercive. And *my family expects* used by a partner as a way of ending a
discussion has stopped being honour and become something Lesson 6 described.

## Practical tool — Family Background Questions

Part A is yours alone. Part B records what you have observed. Part C gives
questions to ask each other over time. Part D sorts expectations. Part E is for
when families object. Part F is safety, and it stands whatever else is written.
Nothing here is scored.

> **This is not a dossier on the other person's family.** Do not question their
> relatives behind their back or record confidential family information you have
> no right to keep.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone — a partner, a
> parent, or anyone else in your household — may monitor your account, device or
> browser, do not record anything here that could place you or a relative at
> risk. Use a safer device where possible, or leave it blank and speak to a
> specialist service instead. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

> **If you are not currently dating**, Part A is the work. Do it now: knowing your
> own family's patterns and expectations before you meet someone whose family will
> ask about them is the most useful preparation there is.

### Part A — My own family

- How conflict was handled in my home, and what I do now when there is conflict:
- How affection was shown, and what I now expect from a partner:
- How money was talked about, and what I absorbed:
- Who made decisions, and what I assume about decision-making in marriage:
- What faith looked like at home, and what I expect in a household:
- Gifts and strengths I received from my family:
- Traditions I hope to preserve:
- Patterns I want to change:
- Evidence that I am already practising something different:
- Obligations I carry to my family now, or expect to — financial, care, siblings:
- What my family expects of the person I marry, and how much weight I give that:
- Whether my family's involvement in my decisions is consultation or approval:

### Part B — What I have observed about theirs

Answer *Observed / Not yet observed / Mixed evidence / Concern observed*, with
evidence and dates.

- They can speak about their family with honesty and reasonable self-awareness.
- They know what they took from their family and what they rejected, and I have
  seen evidence of a different practice, not only insight.
- Their family's role in major decisions is understood, freely accepted, and
  compatible with the kind of marriage we would both be choosing.
- They relate to their parents or family with whatever combination of honour,
  truthfulness, responsibility and protective distance their circumstances
  reasonably require.
- If there is distance from family, they can state whatever information is
  presently appropriate without being pressured for private details, and I
  understand enough to know whether the situation may materially affect a shared
  future.
- I have respected their right not to disclose trauma before they are ready.
- Their family's response to me has been:
- Their response to their family's response to me has been:
- Around their family, their adult judgement, responsibility and boundaries
  remain.

### Part C — Questions to ask each other over time

Not all at once, and not as an interview.

1. What did your family teach you about handling disagreement?
2. *(Optional)* What would someone who knew you growing up say you learned from
   your family? What might they say you are still working through?
3. What do you owe your family, now and in the future?
4. What would your family expect of the person you marry?
5. How involved do you expect your family to be in our decisions if we married?
6. How much of a marriage's private life do you think should be shared with
   family?
7. What customs would your family expect at engagement or marriage, and how do you
   feel about them?
8. Where would you expect us to live, and near whom? Is either family expecting to
   live with us now or later?
9. Are there debts or regular financial commitments to family?
10. Who would make decisions if a parent needed long-term care?
11. Are there family customs concerning gender roles or household authority?
12. Are there relatives who would expect access to our home, finances or children?
13. Are there legal, immigration or inheritance matters that could affect us?
14. If we had children, how would we decide which languages, customs and
    traditions to pass on? Where would we worship, and how would grandparents
    participate?
15. What information about our relationship would remain private?
16. Is there anything about your family you would want me to know before we went
    further? *(Share at the level you are ready to.)*

### Part D — Sorting expectations

Take everything either family expects — customs, involvement, support, living
arrangements — and sort each into one of four groups.

- Required by conscience (I could not honour God and refuse this):
- A cultural expectation I would honour gladly:
- Negotiable — could be adapted with honest conversation:
- I would not accept this (and whether the reason is conviction, cost, or fear):

For anything marked *required by conscience*, ask: what is the scriptural basis?
Is this truly a Christian obligation, or a cultural expectation I have
spiritualised? Does it respect both people's consent and dignity? Have I allowed
the belief to be tested by wise counsel?

Compare your list with theirs. Where the same item sits in different groups for
each of you, that is a conversation to have now.

### Part E — If families object

- What exactly has been said, and by whom:
- Which categories apply — specific conduct or safety, practical, theological or
  conscience, prejudice or status, control? Could more than one apply?
- What part of the objection may be legitimate even if another part is prejudiced
  or controlling?
- What information would help us evaluate it?
- Are we dismissing wise concern merely because we dislike the family's manner?
- Are we calling prejudice "wisdom" because confronting it would be costly?
- If control: is either of us able to make a decision our parents disapprove of,
  and what help do we need?

### Part F1 — Coercion or immediate safety

Answer *Yes / No*.

- Either of us is being forced or pressured through threats, violence,
  confinement, severe financial or immigration leverage, or consequences that make
  refusal unsafe.
- Either fears being taken abroad, confined or prevented from returning.
- Passports, money, devices or identity documents have been taken or controlled.
- Refusal may place siblings or other relatives at risk.
- Family or community members are monitoring movements or communications.
- Either person is below the lawful age of marriage.
- We have been instructed to conceal the pressure from safe professionals.

> **If any answer in Part F1 is Yes, do not arrange a family confrontation or
> mediation, and do not tell the relatives involved that you are seeking help.**
> Use a safer device to contact a specialist forced-marriage, safeguarding or
> domestic-abuse service. If danger is immediate, contact the emergency service
> where you are.

### Part F2 — Serious family-boundary concerns

- A partner's family is hostile or cruel to me, and I am expected to absorb it for
  the sake of peace.
- "My family expects" is repeatedly used to end discussion or override my voice.
- Either person is unable to express disagreement with their family without
  punishment or loss of essential support.
- Family prejudice is being minimised rather than addressed.

> These concerns require honest evaluation and independent counsel. If threats,
> surveillance, confinement or fear are also present, treat the situation as Part
> F1 and seek specialist help before involving the family.

*Before you finish:* Ruth, a foreigner, said to Naomi, "Your people shall be my
people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16) — a voluntary expression of extraordinary
loyalty after bereavement, not a command requiring anyone to abandon their own
people or submit to a new family. But her story shows that belonging across
cultural and family lines can be chosen. Pray that both families may be honoured
where honour is possible, that harmful expectations may be resisted, and that any
future household may receive what is good from both histories without being
controlled by either.

## Next faithful step

Choose one question from Part C that is appropriate for the present stage of the
relationship. Ask at a time when both of you have enough privacy and space to
answer — not as an interrogation or an ambush. Questions 3, 4 and 8 often reveal
important expectations, but they need not be treated casually. Record only what
materially affects your own discernment; do not store private family information
that is not yours to keep.

If you are not dating, answer the same question about yourself and your own family
instead. Most people find they have never said it out loud.

## Journal reflection

1. What did you take from your family that you have never examined? This is the
   question a marriage will eventually ask, whether or not anyone asks it now.
2. What did you reject — and have you replaced it with something, or only with its
   absence?

## Need support?

Family is where some people learned that love and harm come from the same hands.

If the person you are dating comes from an abusive family, your role is to support
them, not to fix the family or insist on reconciliation. If their family is
hostile or cruel to you, it is not your job to win them over, and a partner who
expects you to absorb mistreatment for the sake of peace is asking something they
should not ask.

No family can consent to a relationship on your behalf, and none can require you
to enter one. If a family is using threats, violence, confinement, surveillance,
severe or sustained shaming, or financial or immigration leverage in a way that
removes meaningful freedom to accept or refuse a relationship, seek specialist
advice. Custom does not make coercion safe.

Where forced marriage, confinement, threats or so-called honour-based abuse may be
involved, contact a specialist forced-marriage or domestic-abuse service
privately, using a safe method of communication. Do not start with a family
meeting or community mediation. If travel is being planned and you fear being
taken somewhere against your will, seek specialist advice **before** travelling.
Warning signs include confiscation of passports or identity documents, pressure to
travel for an undisclosed purpose, threats against siblings, surveillance by
several family or community members, withdrawal from work or education, and the
fear that telling one relative will reach the whole family.

**Specialist forced-marriage services generally operate in office hours.** Outside
those hours, contact a 24-hour domestic abuse service, or — where there is
immediate risk, including being taken abroad — the police or emergency services. In
the UK the Forced Marriage Unit has an out-of-hours number: 020 7008 1500, asking
for the Global Response Centre.

If danger is immediate, contact the emergency service where you are.

## Continue

If you are dating and these conversations have not happened, begin them gradually
where both of you can speak freely and safely. Do not arrange a family meeting
merely to test how relatives will respond. If coercion, monitoring or fear is
present, seek specialist advice before involving the family or community.

If the families have objected, sort the objection honestly; treating prejudice as
wisdom, or wisdom as prejudice, will cost you either way.

And if this lesson touched something painful in your own family history, be gentle
with yourself. You are not required to have a family story that looks like anyone
else's. You are asked only to know it, and to bring it into the light at a pace
that is safe for you.
