# Lesson 9 — Sexual Boundaries, Practically

> ### Before you begin
> This lesson assumes two people who are each freely choosing. If you have been
> pressured, coerced or assaulted, the plan in this lesson is not what you need —
> the support section is. **Finding Help Where You Live →**
>
> This course is written for adults. Laws on age of consent, sexual images, and
> relationships involving authority or age differences vary by country, and no
> plan written here can create legal consent. **If you are under eighteen and feel
> pressured or unsafe, speak with a safe adult or a child-protection or
> safeguarding service now.**

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to state your own conviction and your
own line in plain words, name the situations in which you are most likely to
cross it, and recognise the difference between a shared failure and coercion.

**Key Scripture**
"Make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires." — Romans 13:14 (ESV)

Paul is writing about sinful desire generally rather than dating, but the
instruction provides practical wisdom here: do not repeatedly arrange
circumstances in which keeping your conviction becomes unnecessarily difficult.
The verse directs attention not only to resistance in the moment but also to
decisions made beforehand.

## Core teaching

Most Christian teaching on sex before marriage can be summarised in two words, and
most single Christians have heard those two words many times. What they have heard
far less often is anything practical — how two adults who are attracted to each
other, who intend to honour God, and who spend a great deal of time together
actually keep the conviction they hold. The rule is clear. The practice is where
people struggle, and where a great deal of shame lives.

This lesson is about the practice. It assumes the historic Christian conviction
that sexual intercourse and deliberately sexual activity belong within marriage.
Christians draw different practical lines around forms of affection such as
kissing and prolonged physical closeness; those differences make honest, specific
planning necessary, but they do not remove the call to sexual integrity.

**What Scripture says, and what it does not.** Paul told the Thessalonians that
God's will was their sanctification. Translations differ over the precise wording
of 1 Thessalonians 4:4, but the surrounding instruction is clear: God calls
believers to sexual holiness, self-control and conduct that does not wrong another
person (1 Thessalonians 4:3–6). To Corinth, a city famous for sexual licence, he
wrote *flee from sexual immorality* (1 Corinthians 6:18). Hebrews says plainly,
"Let marriage be held in honour among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled"
(13:4).

Now what Scripture does not say, because much harm has been done by things
attributed to it.

It does not say sex is dirty or shameful. The same Bible contains the Song of
Songs and 1 Corinthians 7:2–5, which assumes and encourages sexual generosity
within marriage. The problem was never the desire; it was the act separated from
the covenant it was made for.

It does not say your worth depends on your sexual history. Nowhere. A person who
has sinned sexually and repented is forgiven, fully, and is not a lesser
Christian, a damaged gift, or a second-class spouse. If you were taught otherwise,
you were taught wrongly.

It does not make one sex responsible for the other's desire. Scripture places
responsibility for sexual integrity on each person. One person is not responsible
for managing another's desire.

It does not say marriage will automatically fix a sexual relationship formed
outside it, or that engagement is a lesser form of marriage. *We're basically
married* is not a category Scripture recognises. The covenant is established
through vows made and recognised, not through intention.

And it does not say that a person who was coerced or assaulted has sinned. If
sexual activity happened to you without your consent — through force, threat,
pressure, incapacity, or while you were asleep or unable to refuse — that was not
your sin. It was a wrong done to you.

**Why "just don't" is not enough.** If the conviction is clear, why do so many
sincere couples fail to keep it? Partly because desire is strong and God made it
so. But largely because couples rely on willpower in the moment instead of wisdom
in advance. They have a rule and no plan. They are alone, late, tired,
affectionate and unprepared, and they discover that a conviction held in the
abstract is very hard to act on in a particular room at a particular hour.

Joseph, pressed by his master's wife, "left his garment in her hand and fled"
(Genesis 39:12). His situation involved persistent pressure and a serious power
imbalance, so it is not equivalent to ordinary dating temptation. But his
immediate decision to leave illustrates something practical: sometimes wisdom
means leaving the room rather than staying to prove your strength.

**Drawing the line.** I am not going to give you a single universal line for every
form of affection — not because there is nothing to say, but because a line drawn
in advance by the two of you, for your own situation and consciences, is far more
likely to be kept than one imported from a book. What follows is a framework and
the questions.

*Clearly sexual activity* — intercourse and deliberate sexual activity belong
within marriage. This is the historic Christian conviction, and it is not up for
negotiation between the two of you.

*Sexually explicit digital activity* — sexual messages, live sexual video and
intimate images are not morally harmless merely because the couple is physically
apart. They are sexual activity, and they carry a risk nothing physical does:
permanence.

*Affection requiring personal boundaries* — kissing, extended physical closeness
and private settings affect people differently. Draw lines here that respect
conscience, avoid deliberate escalation, and honour the stricter boundary of
either person.

One clarification, because purity-culture teaching has hurt people here: an
involuntary physical response is not itself a moral failure. Bodies respond. The
question is what you deliberately seek, continue or encourage once you recognise
where an interaction is leading.

Six questions, for each of you alone and then together. Can I participate in this
with a clear conscience before God? Does this deliberately seek escalation? Can
either person stop without pressure, guilt or punishment? Does it respect the
convictions we have honestly stated? Would continuing make wise judgement more
difficult? Am I hiding it because I know it contradicts what I said I believed?

Some couples will draw a stricter line than others. That is allowed. What is not
allowed is one person drawing the line for both, or the line being negotiated in
the moment it is being tested.

**Making no provision.** A plan has four parts. *Environment* — where and when you
are most vulnerable, and what you will do about it. For many couples that means
late nights, private spaces with nobody else around, tiredness, alcohol. The
practical response is not to pretend those situations are safe but to plan around
them: say goodnight earlier than you want to, spend time where other people are,
notice when an evening is heading somewhere and change its direction while that is
still easy. *Pace* — how affection develops over time, moving together rather than
one pressing and one yielding. If one of you is always initiating and the other
always holding back, name it; that pattern is not sustainable and it is not fair.
*Digital* — never pressure anyone to create or send intimate material, never share
such material without consent, and think carefully before creating any of it at
all in a relationship that may not last. Any sexual image involving a minor
creates serious safeguarding and legal concerns regardless of who produced or
shared it. If an intimate image involving a minor already exists, do not forward
or redistribute it, even to seek advice. Contact an appropriate safeguarding or
legal service for guidance on what to do next, because possession, preservation
and reporting requirements vary. *Accountability* — someone outside the
relationship who knows your line and may ask whether you are keeping it. Not to
police you, but because a boundary nobody else knows about is very easy to quietly
move.

**Consent, coercion, and what is never a boundary problem.** Everything above
assumes two people each freely choosing. Some situations are not that.

Consent is clear, voluntary, informed, specific and ongoing. It requires more than
the absence of resistance. Silence, freezing, submission through fear, or
eventually giving in after repeated pressure is not freely given consent. It
concerns each occasion and can always be withdrawn. A previous yes never removes
the right to say no. Neither does an engagement ring, a shared flat, a declaration
of love, or a line the two of you agreed a month ago. **A written plan, including
the one below, is never continuing consent.**

Consent is not a negotiation to win. A respectful conversation about different
convictions is not the same as continuing to persuade someone who is reluctant or
has said no. Repeated requests, guilt, threatened abandonment, withdrawal used as
punishment, or financial, housing, immigration or spiritual leverage can make
apparent agreement coercive rather than free. Be fair: a person may legitimately
conclude that incompatible convictions mean the relationship should end, and may
feel disappointed or need space without using those feelings to obtain sex. The
difference is whether the feeling is being used as a lever. Where it is, that is
not a boundary problem to be solved with a better plan. It is one of the patterns
Lesson 6 described, and the response is the same: outside help, not another
conversation.

The same applies to sexual activity involving alcohol or drugs that impair
someone's ability to understand, choose and communicate freely, and to any
activity that continues after someone has said stop, gone silent, or stopped
participating. If a person freezes or goes quiet, they have not consented. Pause
and check.

And if you are a survivor of sexual abuse or assault — many readers are — know two
things. Your body may respond to closeness in ways you did not choose: freezing,
going quiet, dissociating, panicking. Those responses are not consent and they are
not your failure. And you get to decide when and how much to tell the person you
are dating. A partner worth having will receive what you share with care, will not
press for details, and will not treat your history as a problem for them to
manage. If telling them feels unsafe, that is information about the relationship,
not about you.

**When the line is crossed.** It happens, to sincere and praying couples. Grace
does not erase reality; it lets you face reality honestly without condemnation.
But first be clear which of four situations you are in, because they need
different responses.

*A mutually chosen crossing of a shared conviction* — both freely crossed a line
you had both agreed, and both regret it. *One person crossed their own conviction
while the other did not share it* — the response is honest conversation about
whether your convictions are compatible, and whether the person with the stricter
conviction was in any way pressed. *Pressure or coercion was present* — this is not
a shared lapse and not a boundary conversation. The person who was pressured does
not need to confess anything to the person who pressured them. *A medical concern
may be present* — where pregnancy or infection may be possible, seek timely advice
from a qualified healthcare provider or sexual-health service. Receiving medical
care is not evidence of weak faith. After a possible assault, a specialist service
can explain time-sensitive medical and forensic options. In many places, receiving
care does not require an immediate decision about reporting, but confidentiality,
safeguarding and reporting requirements vary by country, service, age and
circumstances. Where possible, ask what may have to be reported before disclosing
identifying details.

For the first situation: confess honestly to God (1 John 1:9) and speak truthfully
with the other person, seeking confidential pastoral or professional support if it
helps, sharing only what is yours to disclose — accountability should never become
public exposure, forced confession or detailed sexual reporting. Then neither
spiral nor shrug. Despair says *we've ruined everything*; your worth is not
ruined. Indifference says *we've gone this far, we may as well*; that is the logic
that turns a lapse into a habit. Revise the safeguards: a line was crossed
somewhere, at some time, in some situation, so ask what did not hold and change
that. A crossing that produces no change in the plan is a crossing that will
happen again. Accept consequences — not punishment, but the honest recognition
that some things have changed.

And do not use marriage as the solution. Paul's *better to marry than to burn with
passion* (1 Corinthians 7:9) acknowledges desire as one legitimate reason to
pursue marriage. It is not permission to marry an unsuitable person, bypass
discernment, or accelerate a wedding because boundaries failed. A marriage entered
mainly to solve sexual temptation may exchange one immediate problem for a
lifelong one.

When crossings repeat despite sincere effort, ask a larger question than *how do
we do better?* If one person is consistently the one who pushes, the question is
about that person, and Lesson 6 has already asked it. If both consistently fail,
do not assume a single explanation: physical intimacy may be carrying a
relationship that has little else, but the pattern may equally reflect unclear
boundaries, incompatible convictions, shame followed by secrecy, missing
safeguards, compulsive sexual behaviour, substance use, pressure not yet named, or
a relationship moving faster emotionally than either person recognises. Each needs
a different response, and most are best worked out with a pastor or counsellor
rather than alone.

## Practical tool — Sexual Boundaries Plan

Part A is for you alone. Parts B and C are for the two of you, if both can speak
freely. Part D is for after a mutually chosen crossing. Part E stands whatever
else is written.

> **This plan is not a contract and it is never continuing consent.** Consent
> concerns each occasion and can be withdrawn at any time, regardless of anything
> written here.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. Neither person is required to show the other their private worksheet, and
> neither may share the other's answers without permission. If coercion or
> pressure is present, do not complete Parts B and C — use Part E and seek
> individual support.

> **If you are not currently dating**, Part A is the work, and it is worth doing
> now rather than later. Leave Parts B, C and D for when there is someone to
> complete them with.

### Part C — Our plan

**Environment**
- The situations in which we are most vulnerable.
- What we will do about each: leave earlier, meet where others are, change the
  evening's direction while it is still easy.

**Pace and consent**
- How we want affection to develop over time, together rather than one pressing
  and one yielding.
- How we will check that affection is mutually wanted.
- What words or signals will mean *pause* or *stop*.
- How we will respond, immediately and respectfully, when either person changes
  their mind.
- Can either of us leave the setting safely and independently?
- Whether either of us has felt hurried or left holding the line alone, and what
  we will change.

**Accountability**
- The person or people outside the relationship who will know our line and may ask
  whether we are keeping it.
- Are they discreet and safe? Do they understand the difference between consensual
  failure and coercion? Are they likely to respond with wisdom rather than
  humiliation? Do they know when specialist help is required?
- What we have both agreed may be shared with them, and what remains private.
- Individual support either of us may also choose, without the other's
  permission.

### Part D — After a mutually chosen crossing

Only if the activity was freely chosen by both. If either person was pressured,
afraid, incapacitated or unable to refuse, skip this and use Part E.

> Record only the minimum detail needed to revise the plan. Do not enter an
> explicit sexual account, identifying evidence or details of a possible assault
> into this worksheet. If you are uncertain whether the activity was fully
> voluntary, stop and seek confidential individual guidance rather than completing
> this section together.

1. What boundary was crossed, and are both people independently certain that
   participation was freely chosen?
2. Does either person need medical or sexual-health advice?
3. What conviction or agreement was crossed?
4. What circumstances made the crossing more likely?
5. What safeguards need to change?
6. Does either person feel blamed, pressured or afraid?
7. What confidential support would be useful, and what is each of us free to
   disclose?

> If the pattern repeats despite sincere changes, or if either person feels unable
> to maintain a limit freely, seek help now rather than waiting for a particular
> number of incidents.

### Part E — Safety statement

These stand regardless of anything written above.

- Consent is clear, voluntary, informed, specific and ongoing. It is given for
  each occasion and can be withdrawn at any time. A prior yes, an engagement, a
  shared home, or a written plan is never consent.
- Using sulking, withdrawal, guilt, repeated requests, threats, or financial,
  housing, immigration or spiritual leverage as pressure to obtain sexual activity
  can become coercive. Coercion is not a boundary problem to be solved with a
  better plan.
- A person who freezes, becomes silent or stops participating has not consented.
  Pause and check; do not continue without clear willingness.
- Sexual activity with a person who is asleep, incapacitated, or unable to
  understand, choose and communicate freely is not consensual.
- Anything that happened to me without my consent was not my sin.
- If pregnancy, infection, injury or assault may be involved, medical help may be
  time-sensitive.
- Either of us may seek confidential outside help at any time without the other's
  permission.

*Before you finish:* pray 1 John 1:9 honestly, whether you are making a plan for
the first time or picking one up after a failure. Then ask God for the practical
wisdom Romans 13:14 describes — to make no provision — so that the conviction you
hold can be kept in the ordinary rooms and hours where it is actually tested.

## Next faithful step

Look at the vulnerabilities you listed in Part A and choose the one that recurs
most. Then decide one concrete change to the circumstances, not to your resolve:
an earlier end to the evening, a different place to meet, someone else present, a
plan for getting home. Write the specific change, not the intention behind it.
*We'll be more careful* is not a plan. *We don't stay at the flat after ten* is.

## Journal reflection

1. Where have you been taught things about sex, or about your own worth, that this
   lesson says are not in Scripture? What do you want to unlearn?
2. If shame has been shaping how you think about this, whose voice is it? And what
   would you say to a friend who came to you in the same position?

## Need support?

If you have read the section on consent and coercion and recognised something — if
you have been pressured, or worse — put the plan aside. That is not what you need.

Begin with a specialist sexual-violence or abuse service for individual guidance.
Where pregnancy, infection, injury or possible assault may be involved, medical
help can be time-sensitive. Seeking medical care does not by itself mean that you
have chosen to make a police report, although safeguarding or mandatory-reporting
duties may apply in some circumstances. Ask the service to explain confidentiality
and its limits where possible.

A trusted pastor may support you spiritually alongside that help, provided they
understand their limits and do not press you towards confrontation, reconciliation
or joint counselling. If you fear the person's reaction, get that support before
raising anything with them. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

You do not need to settle the future of the relationship today. Medical care after
possible pregnancy, infection, injury or assault may, however, be time-sensitive.

If you are not dating, Part A is enough: know your conviction and the
circumstances in which keeping it may become difficult. If you are dating and both
of you can speak freely and safely, create the shared plan before relying on
willpower in the moment. If pressure, fear or coercion is present, do not attempt
a joint plan. Seek individual support.

If you have crossed a conviction — recently, long ago or repeatedly — you are not
disqualified. You can respond honestly, receive grace and begin again from where
you are. If what happened was not freely chosen, it was not your shared failure to
confess.
