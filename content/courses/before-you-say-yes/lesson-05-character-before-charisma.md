# Lesson 5 — Character Before Charisma

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name what you have actually
observed about a person's conduct, distinguish it from the impression they make,
and recognise which windows into their character are still empty.

**Key Scripture**
"One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much, and one who is
dishonest in a very little is also dishonest in much." — Luke 16:10 (ESV)

Jesus said this while teaching about faithfulness and stewardship, not about
choosing a spouse. But the principle is one of the most useful things in this
course: character does not need a crisis to become visible. It shows in small
things, precisely because people do not treat small moments as moments in which
they need to impress.

## Core teaching

Most of us have met someone with unusual presence. When they walk into a room,
the room adjusts. They pray beautifully. They lead worship, or preach, or run the
youth group, and people are drawn to them. When they show interest in someone,
that person feels chosen.

Sometimes such people are also honest, kind, faithful and safe. Sometimes they
are not. The difficulty is that early on, genuine character and polished
presentation can be hard to tell apart.

Charisma is the ability to attract attention, inspire confidence or make others
feel engaged — confidence, charm, eloquence, humour, spiritual fluency, natural
leadership. It may arise from genuine warmth and goodness, or exist without
either. That is why it is neither proof of character nor evidence against it.
Many godly people have it, and it serves the church well.

But charisma cannot establish whether someone is honest when it costs them,
faithful when nobody is watching, kind to people who cannot help them, or able to
hear that they are wrong. Those things are character: the pattern that remains
when a person is no longer managing the impression they make. Individual acts of
kindness or generosity can be performed. A pattern can only be seen over time.

The particular danger is that charisma does not merely fail to reveal character.
It can hide its absence. Someone very good at being liked can travel a long way
on that alone, and the people who like them are often the last to notice what is
missing. This is especially true in churches, where visible gifts are quickly
rewarded with visible roles, and where fluency in spiritual language gets mistaken
for the substance it describes.

Scripture gives a sobering picture in Absalom, who stood at the city gate,
greeted everyone warmly, sympathised with their grievances and "stole the hearts
of the men of Israel" (2 Samuel 15:6). His attention was strategic — warmth used
to build loyalty while he prepared to seize power. That is not a profile of every
charismatic person. It shows that making people feel seen can be genuine care or
a means of influence, and that time and conduct reveal which.

Notice how the New Testament assesses character when it matters. Paul's lists for
church leadership (1 Timothy 3; Titus 1) are almost entirely observable conduct:
how they manage a household, whether they are quarrelsome, how they handle money,
their reputation among outsiders. The context is leadership rather than marriage,
and the passages are not a dating checklist. But the method is instructive.
Scripture does not ask whether a person is impressive. It asks what they have
consistently done.

You cannot see a heart. You can see conduct, and conduct in certain situations is
especially revealing. **Nine windows**, none decisive alone.

**Family relationships.** Warmth towards family is not automatically good and
distance is not automatically bad — some families are loving, others controlling,
neglectful or abusive. Notice whether the person can speak truthfully without
unnecessary cruelty, set appropriate boundaries and explain their choices with
self-awareness. Do not require reconciliation or continued contact where abuse
has occurred.

**People from whom they have nothing to gain.** Waiters, cleaners, drivers,
junior colleagues, children. Courtesy here is not conclusive, but repeated
contempt or entitlement is significant. Be fair across cultures and temperaments
— directness or awkwardness is not contempt. Someone charming to you and
consistently dismissive of the waiter is showing you which one is the
performance.

**How they speak about former partners.** Where a past relationship involved
ordinary mutual conflict, can they recognise their own contribution? Where they
describe coercion or abuse, do not demand false equivalence or assume they must
share responsibility for being harmed. Beyond that: are the facts consistent over
time, is there appropriate privacy, are they still entangled, do they compare you
constantly — and is there a pattern, the same complaint about every person, that
tells you more about them than about their exes?

**Authority and accountability.** Can they disagree honestly without deception,
retaliation or contempt? A history of conflict with every employer and pastor
deserves examination — but so does unquestioning submission, because spiritual
authority can be abused and healthy character sometimes includes leaving. Ask
what happened rather than assuming that leaving proves rebellion or staying
proves faithfulness.

**Money.** Lesson 11 is given to this. For now: money is where words and behaviour
most often separate. Someone careful with their own money and careless with yours
is worth noticing.

**Disappointment.** A plan falls through, a request is refused, you cannot see
them when they wanted. Some are gracious. Some sulk. Some punish. Some rage.
Disappointment is one of the most honest moments a person has, because it is
where their preferences meet a world that will not arrange itself around them.
How they treat you then — especially when you are the cause, especially when you
have said a reasonable no — is among the most important things you will learn.

**Correction.** Can they listen without intimidation, punishment or
counterattack? Can they return to it after the first emotional response, and
acknowledge what is true even while disagreeing with part of it? Or does
correction produce defence, retaliation, or the use of a minor complaint about
delivery to avoid the substance? "Quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger"
(James 1:19). A person who cannot be corrected cannot grow, and a marriage to
someone who cannot grow is a marriage with a ceiling.

*A fairness note on those last two windows.* Some people freeze, go quiet or need
time to process. That may reflect anxiety, temperament, neurodivergence or past
trauma rather than manipulation. A delayed response is not automatically avoidance
and temporary silence is not automatically punishment. The question is whether
they say they need time and come back to the conversation, or use withdrawal to
control and punish.

**Temptation and small dishonesties.** Not great sins — you will probably not see
those early — but small ones. Easy white lies, corners cut when nobody is
looking, small promises kept or not, borrowed things returned, the truth told
when it is slightly embarrassing. This is where Luke 16:10 does its work.

**How they speak about people in general.** Is there a running current of
contempt, mockery or suspicion? Do they share other people's private business?
The way someone speaks about others when those others are absent is a fair guide
to how they will one day speak about you.

**How to look through the windows.** You are watching, not testing — do not
engineer situations, and do not manufacture opportunities to fill the sheet. But
do not make a major commitment while important areas remain hidden or
undiscussed. You are looking for patterns, though severity matters: one awkward
interaction may tell you little, while a single act of violence, sexual coercion,
credible threat or major deception may tell you enough to seek safety
immediately. You are collecting evidence, not impressions — *he seems kind* is an
impression; *when the taxi driver got lost and made us late, he stayed calm and
thanked him anyway* is evidence. Write it down; impressions fade and distort.

And one addition: **pay particular attention to the response to failure.** Nobody
passes all nine windows cleanly, and anyone who appears to is either not being
seen clearly or is very good at presentation. In ordinary failures, the response
afterwards often reveals important character: do they notice, own their part,
repair what they can and change over time? Severity still matters. Violence,
sexual coercion, credible threats, stalking and serious controlling behaviour are
not made safe by a polished apology.

Underneath all nine sits one question: **is there integrity between this person's
public and private conduct?** Consistency does not mean identical behaviour
everywhere; everyone has more energy for a platform than for a kitchen. It means
the public virtues are not contradicted by private cruelty, dishonesty or
contempt. Are they as patient with you alone as in company? Do they keep the
standards they preach, and the promises nobody witnessed?

A word about your own bias, both directions. Some readers overvalue charisma and
find quiet people invisible. If that is you, be honest about it — not because you
must be drawn to anyone in particular, but because confidence may be taking weight
in your judgement that belongs to faithfulness and integrity. Other readers, hurt
by a charismatic person, have become suspicious of anyone with presence. That is
not discernment either. Charisma is not a red flag. It is simply not a green one.

## Practical tool — Character Observation Sheet

A record to keep over time, not a form to complete in one sitting. Return to it
periodically as ordinary life provides new evidence. Write dates. Write evidence,
not impressions. Nothing here is scored.

> **Using this sheet well.** It is for clarifying your own observations, not for
> investigating or secretly monitoring another person. Do not access private
> accounts, question people behind their back, provoke reactions, or record
> confidential information you have no right to keep. Describe sensitive
> experiences with restraint.

> **Privacy and safety.** This sheet saves to your account. If someone may monitor
> your account, device or browser activity, do not record incidents here that
> could place you at risk. Use a safer device where possible, leave sensitive
> fields blank, and visit **Finding Help Where You Live**. Do not include another
> person's confidential information unless it is necessary for your own
> reflection and safe to store.

> **If you are not currently dating or seriously considering someone**, read
> through the nine windows but do not complete the sheet about an imagined
> partner or repurpose it to assess a friend. Keep it for later. For now, use the
> questions to consider what another person would observe in you.

### Part A — The nine windows

For each, record what you have actually observed and when. If nothing yet, write
*not yet observed* and leave space. If something concerned you, mark it clearly
and note how serious it was, whether it has happened before, and — where the
matter was safely clarified or addressed — their response.

> Do not raise an incident directly merely to complete this tool. If addressing
> it could bring intimidation, punishment, surveillance or danger, leave that
> question unanswered and seek individual support first.

1. **Family relationships and boundaries** — What I have observed about honesty,
   responsibility and boundaries. If there is distance from family, is it
   reasonably explained by safety, health or other circumstances? Am I judging
   them for not having the family relationship I wish they had?
2. **People from whom they have nothing to gain** — Observed. Any concern
   (severity / repeated? / their response where it was safely addressed).
3. **Former partners** — Observed: consistency of the facts, privacy,
   entanglement, healing, comparison. Any concern.
4. **Authority, responsibility and accountability** — How they respond to
   legitimate limits. Can they challenge authority without deception or contempt?
   Do they describe every authority figure as unreasonable? Have they shown
   willingness to be accountable to trustworthy people?
5. **Money** — Observed. Any concern.
6. **Disappointment, including when I am the cause** — Observed. Any concern.
7. **Correction** — Observed. Any concern.
8. **Temptation and small dishonesties** — Observed. Any concern.
9. **How they speak about other people** — Observed. Any concern.

### Part B — How they respond to "no"

- A time I declined a request, changed a plan or set a boundary:
- Their immediate response:
- Did they respect the answer without pressure, repeated persuasion, intimidation
  or withdrawal used to punish me? If they needed time to process disappointment,
  did they communicate that and return without making me responsible for fixing
  their feelings?
- Did I feel free to maintain my decision?

### Part C — Response to failure

- What happened?
- How serious was it?
- Did they recognise it without being forced?
- Did they take responsibility for their part?
- Did they make repair where possible?
- Did their later behaviour change?
- Did I feel free to raise it?

### Part D — Integrity

Answer *Observed / Not yet observed / Mixed evidence / Concern observed*.

- Their public virtues are not contradicted by private cruelty, dishonesty or
  contempt.
- They are as generous, patient or kind when nobody will hear about it.
- They keep the standards they hold others to.
- They keep promises made privately as carefully as those made publicly.
- They do not repeatedly seek admiration or react badly when attention moves
  elsewhere.

### Part E — People who have known them over time

- People I have naturally met, with the person's knowledge, who have known them
  over time:
- What I have directly observed about those relationships:
- If I have not met established friends, family members or members of their
  community, are there reasonable explanations?

> Do not ask others for confidential character reports behind the person's back.

### Part F — Reading the sheet

- Which windows are still empty, and is that because life has not provided the
  situation, or because I have been kept from it?
- Where there are concerns, are they incidents or patterns — and how serious?
- Does what I have observed match what they say about themselves?

*Before you finish:* pray for the person honestly — not for the relationship
first, but for them, that they would be faithful in very little. Then pray the
same for yourself. The windows you are looking through are ones someone else may
one day look through at you.

## Next faithful step

**If you are dating or considering someone**, choose one window and record one
dated piece of evidence. Return to the sheet periodically as ordinary life
provides more — not on a schedule that turns observation into monitoring.

**If you are not dating**, choose one window and ask what evidence another person
would currently see in you. Name one ordinary action through which you want to
practise greater faithfulness.

## Journal reflection

1. Do you tend to overvalue confidence, gifting and presence, or to distrust it?
   What has that cost you before?
2. Is there anything in the nine windows you are reluctant to write down
   honestly? What, and why?

## Need support?

This lesson is about the ordinary formation of character, observed patiently.
Some behaviour does not belong in that category at all.

If any window has already shown you violence, threats, sexual coercion, stalking,
intimidation, serious deception, or a person you are afraid to disappoint, stop
using this as an ordinary character exercise. Do not keep observing, and do not
confront them alone if confrontation could increase the danger. Begin with an
appropriate specialist abuse service for individual safety guidance; seek
medical, legal or other professional help where the situation requires it. Lesson
6 explains these patterns, but you do not need to complete that lesson before
getting help. **Find help where you live →**

## Continue

If you are early in knowing someone, you have almost nothing in the windows yet.
That is fine — start looking and start writing. If you have known someone for
months, the sheet may fill quickly, and the useful question becomes which windows
are still empty after all that time.

If you are not dating, keep the empty sheet. Its value begins when there is a
real person and real evidence to record.

And if you recognised yourself in the description of charisma without character,
this lesson is for you as much as for anyone you might date. Character can be
built. It is built the way it is seen: in small things, over time, with repair
after failure.
