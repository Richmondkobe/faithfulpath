**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to weigh any claim that God has
spoken about your relationship — someone else's or your own — and ensure that a
decision to proceed rests on grounds that can be responsibly examined.

**Key Scripture**
"Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good." —
1 Thessalonians 5:20–21 (ESV)

Paul wrote this to a church that valued prophecy, and both halves matter. He does
not tell them to stop listening for God. He tells them that whatever they believe
they have heard must be weighed. Testing is not unbelief; it is the instruction.

## Core teaching

*God told me you're going to be my wife.*

Some readers have said those words. Some have had them said to them. Some have
heard them from a pulpit, or from a visiting prophet, or from a mother who had
been praying. And nearly every Christian who has dated has faced the quieter
version, inside their own head: *Is this God? Is this what he wants? How would I
know?*

This lesson is written for readers across the range of Christian conviction about
how God speaks today, and it does not try to settle that question. What it offers
are tests grounded in Scripture and recognisable across traditions.

**Start with what is clear.** God has already given a great deal of direction about
whom to marry. Marry in the Lord. Seek truth, holiness and good fruit. Do not
spiritualise conduct that is deceptive, coercive or unsafe. Be wise, be patient,
seek counsel, test everything. That is God's revealed will, and it is not hidden.

Notice what Scripture does not do. It does not promise that God has selected one
specific person and that your task is to detect them. When Paul discusses a
Christian widow's freedom to remarry, he says she may marry "whom she wishes, only
in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:39). Within Christian faithfulness, a believer
exercises genuine choice rather than searching for a hidden name. Nothing in
Scripture turns choosing a spouse into a treasure hunt in which one wrong step
means missing *the one*.

Christians have commonly understood God to guide through Scripture-shaped wisdom,
prayer, counsel, conscience and providential circumstances. Wisdom he promises to
give (James 1:5). Counsel he commends (Proverbs 11:14). Conscience itself must be
formed and tested; a strong conscience can still be mistaken, fearful or poorly
instructed. And Christians differ over impressions, dreams and prophetic words —
some believe God guides through these today, others that they ceased or should be
treated with great caution.

Two things are true on every side of that difference. Paul's instruction to the
church that prized prophecy was to test it and to "let the others weigh what is
said" (1 Corinthians 14:29). And **no claim that God has spoken — whoever makes it
— binds another person's conscience.**

**The problem with "God told me" in dating.** There is nothing wrong with believing
God has led you towards someone; many good marriages began with exactly that
sense. The problem is how the claim functions once spoken aloud.

*It is not self-authenticating.* The other person cannot examine the private
experience it came from. Its content, fruit and function can be tested — that is
what this lesson does — but the claim alone proves nothing.

*It puts pressure on the other person.* Once God has been invoked, saying no feels
like saying no to him. That is precisely why the phrase is so often used —
sometimes deliberately, often not.

*It frequently baptises a feeling.* Loneliness, fear, desire, pressure: any of them
can feel, from the inside, like conviction. Christians believe God renews the
heart, but renewal does not make every impression infallible.

*Sincere people are wrong.* The person saying it may believe it entirely and still
be mistaken. Sincerity is not accuracy.

*And it does not become your obligation.* **You do not need a competing revelation
in order to say no.** Your free and considered consent is necessary in its own
right, and no one else's experience can supply it.

**How to test a claim** — whether it is someone else's or your own.

*Does it conflict with Scripture?* God does not speak against his word. A word to
marry an unbeliever, to proceed with someone unsafe, or to abandon existing
obligations is not from him, however it felt. Do not confuse contradiction with
denominational disagreement — but no impression can authorise sin, deception,
coercion or the abandonment of genuine responsibilities.

*Does it bypass wisdom?* God's guidance does not ask you to ignore what you have
seen. If the character evidence is poor, or the patterns of Lesson 6 are present,
a claim that overrides them is asking you to trust an impression over a fact.

*Does it require haste?* A claim should tolerate enough time for consent, character
observation and counsel. Urgency created by another person is not evidence of
divine urgency.

*Does it require isolation?* Some spiritual experiences are legitimately private,
and privacy is not the concern. Isolation from wise evaluation is. Does the claim
require concealment from safe, independent people who could help you weigh it?

*Does it attempt to determine someone else's decision?* Whatever one believes about
contemporary prophecy, a private impression about marriage does not give one
Christian authority over another adult's decision.

*Has it been weighed, and by whom?* By more than one mature and genuinely
independent believer — people free to disagree, who know enough of the actual
relationship to speak wisely, and who have nothing to gain. Counsel supplies
perspective, not divine certification, but its absence is telling.

*What fruit does it produce in conduct?* Does the claim lead the speaker towards
humility, patience, truthfulness, self-control and respect for the other person's
freedom? Or do they use it to pressure, intimidate, isolate or punish? Your own
anxiety may be important information, but it is not by itself a theological test:
obedience can be frightening, and manipulation can temporarily feel peaceful.

*Is the person open to being wrong?* This may be the most telling test. Someone who
knows the difference between God's authority and their own interpretation can say:
*this is what I believe God has shown me, and I may be mistaken, and you are
free.* A person who cannot allow that possibility has confused their conviction
with God's authority.

And one more, the plainest. Deuteronomy gives Israel two tests: a prediction that
fails was not spoken by God (18:22), and even a sign that occurs must be rejected
if its message leads away from faithfulness (13:1–5). Fulfilment is not enough;
failure is decisive. Applied carefully: if someone says God told them you would
marry them, and you do not, then God did not say it. But **you need not wait for
the outcome before refusing pressure now.** Your no does not have to prove anything
about the prophecy. It is enough that another person's claim cannot supply your
consent.

**Three substitutes that get used more confidently than they deserve.**

*"I have peace about it."* The verse usually cited is "let the peace of Christ rule
in your hearts" (Colossians 3:15) — which in context is about the church being
called together in one body, not a private decision technique. Attraction can
produce reassurance that gets read as peace about an unwise decision. Fear, trauma,
or simply the weight of a decision can produce anxiety about a right one. Peace is
worth noticing. It is not worth obeying on its own.

*"God opened a door."* Circumstances are part of providence and worth noticing. But
an open door is not a command to walk through it. Paul describes a wide door in
Ephesus alongside many adversaries (1 Corinthians 16:8–9), and elsewhere leaves an
open door in Troas out of concern for Titus (2 Corinthians 2:12–13). Doors open for
many reasons.

*Fleeces.* Gideon asked for signs with a fleece (Judges 6:36–40) — but he had
already received God's instruction before asking. The signs function less as a
discernment method than as God's patience with a frightened man. *If they text me
before nine, it's a sign* is not discernment. It is chance dressed as revelation.

**Other hard questions.**

*Is there one person God has for me?* Scripture does not teach a single soulmate
you must find. Looking back, many married Christians see God's hand in how they
met. Looking forward, the question Scripture asks is not *is this the one* but *is
this wise*. Rejecting soulmate fatalism does not make compatibility unimportant;
it frees you to evaluate it honestly.

*What if I miss God's will?* His moral will for you is stated plainly: your
sanctification (1 Thessalonians 4:3) — becoming like Christ, which can happen
married or single, with this person or another. If you choose wisely and it does
not work out, you have not fallen out of his purposes. If you choose foolishly and
repent, his grace is not smaller than your mistake.

*They said God told them. Am I disobeying by saying no?* No. Their word is not
binding on you, and a no given honestly after prayer and thought is not rebellion.
It may be the most obedient thing you do.

*My pastor said… / A prophet said…* Leaders can be wrong. The counsel of a wise and
trustworthy pastor may deserve real weight, but a pastor who tells you whom to
marry has stepped beyond counsel. A prophetic word naming a person is exactly what
Paul said should be weighed, and you are among those who weigh it. A leader should
never use office, prophecy, confidentiality or fear of spiritual consequences to
secure a romantic decision — and where the leader is personally interested in the
relationship, the conflict of interest is especially serious.

*I prayed and felt nothing.* This is normal and is not a sign of God's absence.
Most decisions in the Christian life are made not by special revelation but by
wisdom applied to what is known. Silence in prayer is often an invitation to use
the tools God has already given.

*I don't feel led to end it, even though it's harming me.* You do not need a
special word to leave harm. God has already spoken: "The prudent sees danger and
hides himself" (Proverbs 22:3). Waiting for a feeling of release before leaving an
unsafe relationship is waiting for something Scripture has already given. If
immediate safety is involved, do not delay while seeking further confirmation.

**Your own "God told me."** Everything above applies to you as much as to anyone.
If you believe God has drawn your attention towards someone, hold it with open
hands — and in most cases, do not announce it. Translate it into an ordinary
honest statement: *I am interested in getting to know you.* Let mutual interest and
character develop without placing God's name behind your desire. If the
relationship becomes established and the experience becomes relevant, it can be
shared later as testimony rather than leverage. If it is from God, testing will not
harm it. If it is not, testing is a mercy.

Before the tool, a prayer. Pray it if it fits; leave it if it does not.

> *Lord, I do not want to be deceived, and I do not want to deceive myself. Where
> your name has been used to move me, give me the nerve to look at what is
> actually being asked. Where I have used your name to get what I wanted, forgive
> me and let me take it back. Teach me to want your will more than I want this to
> be your will. And give me the wisdom you promised to give — not a sign, not a
> feeling, but the plain wisdom to see what is in front of me and decide honestly.
> Amen.*

## Practical tool — Testing Spiritual Claims Worksheet

Use this for any claim that God has spoken about your relationship — from the
person you are dating, a leader, a family member, or yourself. Nothing here is
scored.

> **Privacy and safety.** This saves to your account. If someone may monitor your
> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

> **If you are not currently dating**, Part C is still worth doing, and Part D is
> worth knowing before anyone says any of this to you.

### Part A — The claim

- What was said, as exactly as I can recall:
- Who said it, and when:
- What is actually being asked of me — the same sentence with the spiritual
  reference removed:
- Would I accept that request if it were made in plain words?

### Part B — Warning tests

Mark *Yes* where the concern applies, *No* where it does not, *Not sure* where you
cannot tell.

- The claim conflicts with Scripture.
- The claim asks me to ignore character or safety evidence.
- The claim creates pressure to act before testing.
- The claim requires isolation from safe counsel.
- The claim attempts to determine my decision.
- The claim has not been weighed by independent believers who know the
  relationship and are free to disagree.
- The speaker uses fear, anxiety, urgency, guilt or promised spiritual reward to
  pressure my response.
- The speaker cannot acknowledge possible error.

> No private claim binds your conscience. A *Yes* identifies an additional warning
> about the claim's content, function or use. Several are a reason to talk to
> someone outside the relationship. And note: **no number of *No* answers proves
> that God spoke.** These questions can expose reasons not to trust a claim; they
> cannot certify private revelation as divine.

### Part C — My own sense of leading

If you believe God may be leading you.

- What I sense, in my own words:
- What ordinary desire or fear might I be interpreting spiritually? *(Lesson 1's
  motives: loneliness, pressure, desire, age anxiety, comparison, the wish to be
  rescued.)*
- Run the eight warning tests above on my own claim. Any *Yes*:
- If the other person declines, can I accept it without arguing that they have
  rejected God?
- The ordinary, honest statement I could make instead of announcing the
  impression:

### Part D — The substitutes

- Am I relying on peace as my main guide? What might be producing that peace, or
  that lack of it?
- Am I reading an open door as a command?
- Have I set up a fleece or a sign?

### Part E — The decision on examinable grounds

Set the spiritual claim aside entirely and answer from the earlier lessons.

- *Lesson 2* — shared foundation?
- *Lesson 3* — requirements intact?
- *Lessons 5 and 7* — character and green flags, with evidence?
- *Lesson 6* — any red flags or safety concerns?
- *Existing counsel* — what have trustworthy, independent people said so far?
  Return to this question after Lesson 14, when you have examined whose counsel
  deserves weight.

> A private claim cannot compensate for missing character, unresolved
> incompatibility, lack of consent or safety concerns. A decision to proceed
> should rest on grounds both people can examine: shared faith, observed
> character, compatibility, wise counsel, freedom and safety. A person may decline
> without proving a case. Treat any private impression as secondary and
> non-binding.

### Part F — Safety

Answer *Yes / No*.

- A spiritual claim is being used to override my judgement, silence my questions,
  hurry me, or make me afraid to say no.
- I have been told that refusal will bring divine punishment or cause me to miss
  God's will.
- The person claims my consent is unnecessary because God has already decided.
- A leader has used private pastoral information to influence my decision.
- I have been threatened with exclusion, public embarrassment or loss of ministry.
- I am being discouraged from consulting anyone outside a leader's authority.

> **If any is Yes**, seek help outside the leader's influence — for example, an
> independent safeguarding professional, an external or denominational
> safeguarding office where genuinely independent, an appropriate regulator or
> statutory safeguarding service, a specialist spiritual-abuse service where
> available, or a suitably qualified counsellor unconnected to the church. Do not
> assume that someone is independent merely because they hold a more senior
> position in the same structure. If threats, stalking or physical danger are
> present, Lesson 6's immediate safety guidance applies.

*Before you finish:* "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives
generously to all without reproach" (James 1:5). Ask first for the wisdom God has
promised. You may or may not receive a strong impression or a confirming word. You
do not need one in order to obey what Scripture has made clear, and none can
replace truth, character, freedom and wise judgement.

## Next faithful step

Which of these fits you?

**If you have used "God told me" to pressure someone** and contact remains
appropriate and welcome, correct it once and plainly: *I should not have put it
that way. My sense of guidance does not determine your decision, and you are free
to say no.* Do not use the correction to reopen a relationship, seek reassurance
or ask for another response. If they have ended contact, asked for distance or
blocked you, respect that boundary and make the correction through changed conduct
rather than another message.

**If someone has said it to you**, take Part A's plain-words translation to one
person outside the relationship who is free to disagree with all of you. Not to ask
whether the word was real. To ask what they think is actually being requested.

**If neither applies and you are simply trying to hear God**, write down the
ordinary honest sentence you would say if you set the spiritual language aside
entirely — and notice whether you would still be willing to say it.

## Journal reflection

1. Are you equally willing for God's answer to be no? If not, what would it cost
   you — and is that cost the real reason you are hoping for a yes?
2. Could you continue obeying God without this relationship? Answer slowly. The
   question is not whether it would hurt.

## Need support?

When a spiritual claim is used to override your judgement, silence your questions,
hurry your decision, or make you afraid to say no, the pattern from Lesson 6 is
present — whoever is speaking, partner, parent, or leader. Take the spiritual
language off, look at what is actually being asked, and talk to someone outside
the situation.

**If a church leader is the one applying the pressure**, ordinary pastoral advice
is not the right route, because the person who would normally help is the person
involved. Seek help outside the leader's influence — for example, an independent
safeguarding professional, an external or denominational safeguarding office where
genuinely independent, an appropriate regulator or statutory service, a specialist
spiritual-abuse service where available, or a suitably qualified counsellor
unconnected to the church. Seniority within the same structure does not by itself
establish independence.

If the leader has used private pastoral information, threatened exclusion or loss
of ministry, or discouraged you from consulting anyone outside their authority,
treat that as a safeguarding matter rather than a disagreement. If threats,
stalking or physical danger are present, Lesson 6's guidance applies.

## Continue

You do not need to make a decision today.

If you are simply trying to work out whether God is leading you, and feeling that
you cannot hear him — use what he has already given. Scripture. Wisdom. Counsel.
Time. Character evidence. You may find that he has been speaking all along, in the
ordinary ways, and you were waiting for the extraordinary.
