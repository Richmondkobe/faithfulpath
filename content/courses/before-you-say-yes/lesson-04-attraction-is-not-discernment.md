# Lesson 4 — Attraction Is Not Discernment

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to tell the difference between what
you feel about someone and what you actually know about them, and to notice when
you are supplying an explanation for something they did.

**Key Scripture**
"Man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart." —
1 Samuel 16:7 (ESV)

Samuel had been sent to anoint a king from among Jesse's sons. He saw the eldest,
Eliab, and assumed the choice was obvious. God's reply corrected not his eyesight
but his method. The context is choosing a king rather than a spouse, but the
principle carries: what is visible first is not what matters most. Note also what
the verse does not offer you. You cannot see another person's heart as God does.
What you can see is the fruit that heart produces over time.

## Core teaching

*There was just something about him. I knew the moment I saw her.* Most people who
have been in love have said something like this, and most people listening
understood exactly what they meant.

Christians tend to mishandle attraction in one of two directions. Some treat it
as the whole story: if the feeling is strong enough it must be love, and if it is
love it must be right. Others treat it with suspicion, as though wanting someone
were evidence of shallowness and the truly spiritual choice would be the person
you feel nothing for. This lesson is written against both. Attraction is a gift.
It can invite you to know someone, but it cannot tell you whether to build a life
with them. Attraction may open the door. Discernment decides whether it is wise
to walk through it.

Attraction usually begins as a response rather than a decision. You do not choose
to find someone's face compelling or their presence restful. You do choose
whether to feed it, act on it, or build a relationship around it. It comes in
more than one kind — physical, emotional, intellectual, and what many Christians
would call spiritual, being drawn to the way someone loves God. These overlap and
do not always arrive together. Scripture treats none of it as shameful; whatever
else readers find in the Song of Songs, its poetry plainly celebrates romantic
and physical delight. The question is never whether you may feel it. The question
is what it can tell you and what it cannot.

**What it can tell you.** That a door is open. Attraction is enough for a first
conversation and a decision to get to know someone. Nobody is obliged to pursue a
relationship with a person they feel nothing for simply because that person is
godly and available. It also tells you something about yourself: what you are
drawn to reflects what you value, what you find safe, what has shaped you. And it
matters for marriage. Paul's teaching in 1 Corinthians 7 treats desire within
marriage as real and significant rather than embarrassing. It sets no required
level of premarital chemistry, but some Christians, reacting against a culture
that worships attraction, have swung so far the other way that they marry someone
they admire but do not desire, on the theory that feelings will follow. Sometimes
attraction does grow as trust and admiration deepen. Sometimes it does not.
Nobody should enter marriage pretending to feel what they do not feel. Pretending
is not a spiritual discipline, and concealing it creates loneliness for both
people.

**What it cannot tell you.** Whether a person is honest, kind, faithful,
self-controlled or safe. Where their life is going. How they handle money. What
they are like when disappointed. How they treat people who cannot help them. The
reason is structural: early attraction responds to limited information, while
character becomes trustworthy only through repeated patterns. Attraction can be
immediate; character requires time, repeated patterns and more than one kind of
setting. Proverbs says the same of charm — "Charm is deceitful, and beauty is
vain" (Proverbs 31:30). *Vain* there means fleeting, unable to secure what it
seems to promise. The proverb does not call beauty evil. It refuses to treat it
as reliable evidence.

**Why attraction clouds judgement.** If attraction merely told you nothing, it
would be harmless. The trouble is that it changes how you interpret what you see.
When you are strongly drawn to someone, ambiguous things get read in their
favour. A sharp remark becomes *just their sense of humour*. Lateness becomes
*they're so busy*. A flash of temper becomes *they're passionate*. Silence about
their past becomes *they're private*. The same behaviour in someone you were not
attracted to would simply be noted. Intelligent, normally discerning people
explain away behaviour they would recognise instantly in someone else's
relationship. The explaining is not stupidity. It is attraction, doing what
attraction does.

Intensity makes it worse. A very strong early feeling can seem like evidence that
something significant is happening — that this one is different. But intensity is
not meaning, and it is worth asking what is producing it. Sometimes two
well-matched people. Sometimes being pursued after a long time of not being
pursued. Sometimes a person who is very good at making people feel special,
quickly. And sometimes, uncomfortably, unpredictability itself — attention that
comes and goes, warmth followed by coolness, which can produce a stronger pull
than steady kindness ever does. Lesson 6 returns to this. For now, notice only
that the strength of the pull is not proof of the goodness of the person.

One more way attraction misleads. We are drawn to what feels familiar, and
familiarity is not always safety. If something in this person resembles dynamics
from your family or an earlier relationship, ask whether it feels comforting
because it is healthy or merely recognisable because you have lived it before.
*I've known you forever* is worth examining before it is trusted.

**What discernment is.** The slower thing: a prayerful, evidence-based judgement
about character, direction, fit and safety, made over time and with help from
people who are not attracted to them. It does not cancel attraction; it tests it.
"The simple believes everything, but the prudent gives thought to his steps"
(Proverbs 14:15). Hebrews describes mature believers as those with "powers of
discernment trained by constant practice" (Hebrews 5:14) — a trained capacity,
which improves with honesty and patience. It does not come free with a strong
feeling.

In practice that means five things. **Give it time** — attraction is immediate,
character is not. **Let ordinary life show you who they are** — people are at their
best on a date. You want to see them tired, inconvenienced, with people they do
not need to impress, and receiving a reasonable no. Do not manufacture tests or
provoke someone to see how they react; simply let the relationship out of
carefully managed settings. **Separate what you feel from what you know** — two
columns. The first fills quickly. The second is the one that matters. **Notice
what you are explaining away** — perhaps the single most useful discipline here.
Not every explanation is an excuse, and one incident should not be ignored merely
because it happened once. Ask three things: how serious was it, has it happened
before, and what did they do when it was addressed? **Involve people who are not
attracted to them** — they see the same person without the halo. Their view is not
automatically right; families and churches can be controlling or prejudiced, and
Lesson 14 deals with how much weight counsel should carry. But when several
trustworthy, reasonably independent people name the same concrete concern, do not
dismiss it quickly.

If you feel little attraction to someone you otherwise admire, do not force it
and do not feel guilty. Give it honest time in ordinary settings. If it does not
grow, you are not failing anyone by being truthful about that.

## Practical tool — Chemistry vs Character Reflection

For use when you are attracted to someone, early or further in. If you are not
currently drawn to anyone, Part A can be done on its own, about your patterns,
and the rest kept for later. Answer alone, in writing. Nothing here is scored, and
nobody sees it unless you show them.

### Part A — What I am drawn to

- What first drew me to this person? Be specific and honest, even if the answer
  is embarrassing.
- Which kind of attraction is strongest — physical, emotional, intellectual,
  spiritual? Which is weakest?
- Does this person resemble people I have been drawn to before? In what ways?
- Does anything about them feel familiar in a way that connects to my family or
  my past? Does it feel comforting because it is healthy, or recognisable because
  I have experienced it before?

### Part B — What I feel and what I know

Fill both columns. In the second, every entry needs evidence: something you have
actually seen or heard, and when.

| What I feel about them | What I know about them, with evidence |
|---|---|
| | |
| | |
| | |

Then compare them. Which is longer? How long have you known this person? If the
second column is short, that is not a verdict on them. It is a measure of how
much you actually know, and therefore of how many decisions you are in a position
to make.

### Part C — What I have explained away

For anything they have said or done that you found yourself supplying an
explanation for. Use a fresh set for each incident.

> Do not raise an incident directly merely to complete this tool. If addressing
> it could bring intimidation, punishment, surveillance or danger, leave that
> question unanswered and seek individual support first.

- What happened?
- What explanation did I give?
- How serious was it?
- Has it happened before?
- What did they do when it was addressed — or has it not been addressed?
- What would I tell a friend who described this in their relationship?

### Part D — The pace and the pull

Answer each *Yes / Partly / No*.

**Pace**
- I am not making commitments that exceed what I actually know about this person.
- The emotional intensity has not created pressure to decide quickly.
- I can slow the pace without fearing that they will punish me, withdraw
  affection or lose interest.
- I have kept up my other relationships since this began.

**Observation**
- I have seen them in more than one kind of setting.
- I have observed how they respond to ordinary inconvenience or disappointment.
- I have seen how they treat people they do not need to impress.
- I have seen whether they can receive a reasonable "no."
- Where appropriate, I have observed some of their established relationships.
- Their attention has been steady rather than coming and going.

> A *No* involving pressure, isolation, punishment, withdrawal used to control
> you, or an inability to respect your pace deserves attention now. Speak with
> someone trustworthy and independent; if you fear the person's reaction, seek
> individual or specialist support before confronting them.
>
> Inconsistent attention is something to examine as a pattern: is it ordinary
> availability, or repeated warmth and withdrawal that leaves you anxious and
> easier to control? A *No* caused simply by limited time means that you need
> more observation before making a larger commitment.

### Part E — Outside voices

- What specific behaviour have the people who know me observed in this person?
- Do two or more trustworthy people identify the same concern independently?
- What evidence supports their concern?
- What evidence contradicts it?
- Could prejudice, control or personal interest be affecting their opinion?
- Am I dismissing the concern because I dislike its implications?

### Part F — If I feel little attraction

Only if this applies.

- What do I admire about this person?
- Have I spent time with them in ordinary settings, not only structured or
  pressured ones?
- Am I feeling guilt for not being attracted? Where is that guilt coming from?
- Is there any honest sign that attraction is growing?

than your knowledge of the person.

*Before you finish:* thank God for creating human beings with the capacity for
affection, desire and delight. Then ask for the slower grace of discernment — the
trained capacity of Hebrews 5:14 — so that a particular attraction is neither
shamed nor mistaken for God's approval.

## Next faithful step

Look at the Observation list in Part D and identify one thing that ordinary life
has not yet allowed you to see. Do not create a test, inconvenience or
disappointment. Instead, notice the natural settings that are still missing —
time with established friends, participation in ordinary responsibilities, or
plans that are not carefully staged as dates. Write down what remains unknown,
and return to it when ordinary life provides real evidence.

## Journal reflection

1. If the physical attraction were removed, what would be left?
2. What character concern are you currently tempted to minimise because you are
   attracted to this person? And if a friend described that same behaviour in
   their relationship, what would you tell them?

## Need support?

Intense attraction, a fast pace and gradual separation from your other
relationships form a pattern worth taking seriously together; Lesson 6 deals with
it directly.

Something else is worth saying plainly here: attachment and attraction can remain
strong even when someone is harming you. Continuing to want the person does not
mean the relationship is safe, and it does not mean the harm is less serious. If
you fear their reaction, get individual support before confronting them or
announcing that you intend to leave, and do not use couples counselling as the
first response to coercion or abuse. **Find help where you live →**

## Continue

If this lesson has made you uncomfortable — if you recognised the explaining, or
the intensity, or the familiarity — that discomfort is a signal worth examining,
not a verdict. It may be discernment. It may be anxiety, or an old wound, or
something else. Do not silence it, and do not obey it blindly. Bring it into
prayer, compare it with evidence, and talk it over with someone wise before you
take it to the person.

And if you are simply newly attracted to someone: enjoy it. It is a good thing to
feel. Then slow down enough to let the second column fill.
