# What Have I Actually Observed?

Four lessons ago you began looking at another person. Attraction and what it
hides. Character and the windows it shows through. The patterns that wear
spiritual language. The quiet goodness that is easy to miss.

This page does one job: separating what you have actually seen from everything
else that has gathered around it. No scoring, no verdict, and nothing here
decides anything.

> If you are not currently dating, do not complete this page about an imagined
> person or use it to assess a friend. Read it as preparation for later. For now,
> look back at what Lesson 4 showed you about your attraction patterns and what
> Lesson 7 showed you about the green flags you want to offer. This is where your
> route pauses until dating becomes relevant.

## Four kinds of information

Most of what we believe about someone we are dating is a mixture of these. Each
can contribute something, but they must not be confused.

**What they said.** Their account of their faith, history, intentions, family and
circumstances. Honest self-disclosure matters, and a direct admission may be
significant evidence. Other claims may still need time or independent
confirmation, particularly when they affect your safety, consent or future.

**What you hoped or assumed.** The picture you supplied while filling gaps in what
you knew. This is ordinary human behaviour, not foolishness. It becomes dangerous
when an assumption is treated as evidence.

**What other people reported.** Information from friends, family, church members
or others who know them. Consider how directly the person knows the facts, what
concrete behaviour they describe, whether independent accounts agree, and whether
prejudice, loyalty, control or personal interest may be influencing them.

**What you observed.** What you personally saw or heard, with enough detail to say
what happened and when. Repeated observations across different settings are
particularly valuable for assessing character patterns. Severity also matters:
violence, sexual coercion, stalking, credible threats or serious deception do not
need to happen repeatedly before they carry weight.

## Sort a few

Take three or four things you believe about this person — ideally ones that
matter, not trivia — and put each into one of the four. *He is generous. She is
honest about money. He is close to his family. She would never speak to me the
way my last partner did.*

For anything placed under observation, write what you saw or heard and when. If
you cannot do that, it may be a claim, report, hope or assumption rather than a
personal observation.

This is not a test of the person. It is a measure of how much you actually know —
and therefore of which decisions you are in a position to make.

## Questions worth sitting with

- **What pattern is becoming visible?** What has happened more than once or across
  more than one setting? Also note any single incident serious enough that
  repetition is not required.

- **What remains unknown?** Which windows from Lesson 5 are still closed, and is
  that because ordinary life has not opened them, or because you have been kept
  away?

- **Is there something I have been explaining away?** Lesson 4 asked you to write
  these down. Read what you wrote.

- **Is there something healthy I have failed to recognise?** This runs the other
  way and matters too. Quiet goodness is easy to discount, especially if you have
  been hurt before and are watching hard for danger.

- **What needs clarification?** Identify anything that may require a safe
  conversation, more time and natural observation, independent verification or
  outside counsel. Do not manufacture a test, and do not initiate a conversation
  if you fear the person's response.

## Before Module 3

Module 3 moves from observing to participating: boundaries, physical intimacy,
family, money, and what a person's past does and does not tell you. It asks you
to say things out loud and hold lines, which is different from watching.

That work assumes a relationship in which you can speak freely. If you cannot —
if saying no brings punishment, if raising a concern brings intimidation, if you
are managing your words to prevent a reaction — Module 3 is not the next thing you
need, and setting boundaries is not the tool that will fix it. Lesson 6 explained
why. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

And if this page has left you realising how little is actually in the fourth
category, that is not a failure. It is the most useful thing you could have
discovered at this point. Where no safety or serious character concern exists,
ordinary time in ordinary settings may allow more evidence to emerge. Some
unknowns require a direct conversation or independent confirmation instead. Time
should not be used to postpone action on something already serious.
