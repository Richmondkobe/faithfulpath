# Lesson 16 — When Good Christians Are Wrong for Each Other

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify evidence that a
relationship may need ordinary work, more time or an honest acknowledgement of
incompatibility — and recognise that you may decline a good person without denying
their goodness.

**Key Scripture**
"Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts!" — Psalm 139:23
(ESV)

David asks God to examine him rather than to confirm him. That is the posture this
lesson needs. The prayer is not *show me that I am right*, and it is not *show me
that I am wrong*. It is a request to be looked at honestly by someone who already
knows.

## Core teaching

Everything before this has been about recognising problems: motives that mislead,
character that fails, patterns that harm. But some relationships have none of that.
Two sincere believers. Sound character on both sides. No red flags, no safety
concerns, families who approve, a church already planning the wedding. And still
the relationship may not be a workable or mutually wanted fit — not because either
person is bad, but because **goodness alone does not create compatibility.**

Nobody prepares you for this. When serious wrongdoing is present, the reason a
relationship should not continue may be easier to name, although ending it can
still be emotionally difficult or physically dangerous. When neither person has
done wrong, even the reason can feel difficult to name. So people stay. And
substantial incompatibility ignored before marriage does not disappear after it.

One clarification first. *Wrong for each other* does not mean God created one ideal
match and this person is not it. Lesson 13 set that idea aside. It means the
relationship, as it actually exists, cannot support a marriage both people can
enter freely and faithfully.

**Good is not the same as right.** The hardest thing to hear is that **a person's
goodness is not a reason to marry them.** Goodness is a reason to respect them, to
be glad you know them, to hope well for them. Sound character is essential to a
healthy marriage — three lessons were spent on it — but it does not establish that
two particular people should marry. There are many good people in the world and you
will not marry most of them. The fact that this particular good person is in front
of you, and wants you, and has been kind, does not create an obligation.

You cannot marry as an act of gratitude, or charity, or because refusing would hurt
someone who does not deserve to be hurt. A marriage entered for those reasons will
not honour the goodness that prompted it. It will slowly wear it down.

Scripture does not command you to marry someone merely because that person is good,
available or interested. It sets boundaries — in the Lord, with wisdom, without
coercion — and within them it leaves you free. Paul's phrase about the widow is the
one Lesson 13 used: she may marry "whom she wishes" (1 Corinthians 7:39). **The wish
is not a spiritual defect. It is part of what you are allowed to weigh.**

**What this looks like.** No single sign, and none of these is a fault in either
person or a verdict on its own.

*You want different lives.* Lesson 15's third group. A calling, a country, children,
a shape of family that one of you needs and the other cannot honestly give. Ask
whether the difference concerns a central conviction, an unavoidable responsibility
or a deeply established direction — or whether it is a preference neither of you
has seriously explored. And a claimed calling should be tested with the same
humility Lesson 13 required: *God called me* must not become a way of making the
other person surrender every competing hope.

*The same person is always the one adapting.* Healthy relationships involve mutual
adaptation, and the balance will not be equal in every season. The concern is when
the relationship's normal structure repeatedly requires the same person to surrender
convictions, relationships, responsibilities or parts of their identity, while the
other remains largely unchanged.

*The relationship depletes rather than restores, over time.* Not on a bad day, and
not because a partner must always restore you. Relationships take effort and
sometimes accompany people through exhausting seasons. The question is whether the
relationship itself repeatedly leaves you depleted, guarded or unable to recover —
or whether work, health, grief or family pressure is straining both of you. Name the
source before treating exhaustion as a verdict.

*The recurring disagreements are not about incidents.* Some conflicts arise not from
a failure to apologise but from enduring differences in conviction, direction, needs
or expectations. The question is not whether either person can be fixed, but whether
those differences can be carried without chronic resentment or loss of integrity.

*You are less yourself.* Lesson 6 asked this as a warning sign; here it can be true
without any wrong being done. Be specific. Which part of you has become quieter? Did
they ask for that? Is this healthy maturity, ordinary adjustment, fear, or
self-erasure? Can you express the missing part safely — and what happens when you
do?

*Independent counsel converges on a concrete dynamic.* Give weight when advisers who
have not compared notes name the same specific thing — chronic self-silencing,
incompatible plans, repeated exhaustion — rather than merely saying you do not look
right together.

*A persistent reluctance that honest time has not resolved.* You have prayed for the
doubt to lift and given real time in ordinary settings. A persistent reluctance
deserves honest examination. It is not automatically God's warning — no feeling is —
but nor should it be suppressed merely because the other person is good. Ask what it
is attached to, what evidence supports it, and whether it changes when specific
concerns are addressed. **Premarital doubt should not be argued away. It should be
understood.**

**What it is not.** Be honest in the other direction, because this lesson can be
misused.

It is not *ordinary doubt* — every serious relationship produces moments of *what if
I'm wrong?* That is the weight of the decision, not a verdict on it. It is not *fear
of commitment dressed as discernment* — some people leave every good relationship at
the point where it would require something of them and call it a lack of peace. If
you have done that more than once, examine the pattern before acting on it again. It
is not *perfectionism* — nobody will match you in every area, and a person waiting
for the absence of all friction will wait forever. It may not be incompatibility at
all if *external stress* is temporarily reducing both people's capacity, or if an
*unresolved personal struggle* makes closeness difficult in every serious
relationship. That possibility is not a reason to stay, but it may call for
individual support before the pattern is interpreted.

And it is not *needs work*. A workable relationship has a specific difficulty both
people can name, mutual willingness to address it, and some evidence that honest
effort produces change. Lesson 17 is about relationships like that, and they are
worth the work. A fundamental incompatibility remains substantially unchanged
despite honest effort, because solving it would require one person to surrender
something central or become someone they are not. Even then, effort alone does not
obligate continuation.

Telling the difference is the whole task, and it is rarely possible alone. Use the
earlier tools. Use counsel. Use time — but honest time, with something specific
tried, not time spent hoping the question will go away.

**The theology of a good ending.** Dating is not marriage. Engagement is a serious
intention and a public promise, and ending one can carry real moral, emotional,
financial and communal consequences — but proceeding without free conviction is not
the solution to those consequences. **Before the vows, you are two people discerning,
and discernment is allowed to conclude no. That is what it was for.**

God does not require you to marry someone in order to reward their goodness, or to
avoid disappointing them, or because he brought you together. That a meeting was
meaningful, even providential, does not prove that marriage is its required outcome.

And a clear, respectful no may be painful, but **pain is not the same as cruelty.**
Marrying someone you should not marry, and letting them discover it after the vows,
is the deeper harm.

**If they believe it is right and you do not.** You are not obliged. Their certainty
is theirs. Your no does not require a competing certainty, and it does not require
you to prove a case — **a freely given no is sufficient.** Once you have genuinely
decided, do not call it *not now* merely to soften a no. False hope can prolong
pain. Communicate clearly and respectfully where it is safe to do so. Where you fear
retaliation, stalking, coercion or danger, clarity does not require a private or
in-person conversation; make a safety plan first.

**If you believe it is right and they do not.** This is the harder side, and many
readers are on it. They are not rejecting God, and they are not failing to see what
God has shown you. They are exercising exactly the freedom this lesson describes —
and if you argue that their no is disobedience, you have moved into the pattern
Lesson 6 named. Receive the decision as final unless they independently and clearly
reopen it. Do not continue contact for the hidden purpose of changing their mind.
Grieve it; it is a real loss. Do not turn it into a spiritual contest.

Two good people, and one of them says no. That is allowed. It is painful, and it is
allowed, and God's purposes for you are not smaller because of it.

Before the tool, a prayer. Pray it if it fits; leave it if it does not.

> *Lord, I keep looking for someone to blame, and there is no one. They are good,
> and I do not think this can be built. Take away the idea that loving you means
> overriding what I can plainly see. If I have reached a decision, give me the
> courage to communicate it honestly and safely, without turning it into something
> it is not. Hold them, whatever I decide. And where I am wrong about all of this,
> show me that instead. Amen.*

## Practical tool — Good Person, Wrong Relationship Reflection

For a relationship in which no abuse or serious safety concern has been identified
and both people appear to show genuine character. **If fear, coercion or control is
present, return to Lesson 6 rather than using the language of incompatibility to
soften it.** Nothing here is scored.

> account, device or browser, do not record anything here that could place you at
> risk. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

### Part A — What I am seeing

For each, answer *True / Partly true / Not true*, then the prompts.

**Different lives** — We want different lives in a way that would require one of us
to live a life not our own. · Is the difference a central conviction, unavoidable
responsibility or established direction — or an unexplored preference?

**One person adapting** — Which decisions, relationships or parts of identity are
repeatedly surrendered? · Who normally adapts, and is the pattern freely chosen,
circumstantial, or expected? · What happens when the adapting person asks for
change?

**Depletion** — Over months, the relationship itself has left me depleted, guarded
or unable to recover. · What external strain might be affecting us both?

**Enduring differences** — What exact convictions, needs or future plans are in
conflict? · Is there a mutually acceptable arrangement? What would each person have
to surrender, and could they do so without lasting resentment?

**Less myself** — Which part of me has become quieter? Did they ask for that? · Is
this healthy maturity, ordinary adjustment, fear, or self-erasure? Can I express the
missing part safely, and what happens when I do?

**Persistent reluctance** — Honest time in ordinary settings has been given, and a
reluctance remains. · What is the feeling attached to? What evidence supports it?
Does it change when specific concerns are addressed?

### Part B — Alternative explanations

For each concern above:

- How long has this been present, and in what settings?
- What alternative explanation might fit — ordinary doubt, a pattern of leaving at
  commitment, perfectionism, outside stress, an unresolved personal struggle?
- Have I communicated it? What happened after I did?
- Does the same pattern occur in my other close relationships?

> If nothing specific has been tried, ask whether both people freely want to try
> something and whether doing so would be safe and appropriate. A structured attempt
> may clarify a workable difficulty, but it is not a requirement before either
> person may say no. Do not use an experiment to postpone a decision already made or
> to test an unsafe relationship.

### Part D — What I know to be true

Mark each *I believe this / I struggle to believe this*.

- Dating is not marriage, and discernment is allowed to conclude no.
- Respecting their goodness does not require romantic commitment.
- Ending the relationship does not require proving that either person is wrong.
- I cannot marry someone as gratitude, charity, or to avoid hurting them.
- I am allowed to say no, and I am responsible for communicating it honestly.
- They are allowed to say no without giving me an explanation I consider
  sufficient.
- Neither person should use God, counsel or previous sacrifice to overturn the
  other's decision.

> Take anything you struggle to believe to a wise, independent pastor, mentor or
> suitably qualified counsellor who will not pressure either outcome.

*Before you finish:* pray for the other person first, and mean it — that God will
lead them into a life of faithfulness, love and freedom, whether or not that life
includes you. Then pray Psalm 139:23 for yourself. Ask not for the feeling you think
you should have, but for the truth about what is in front of you, and the courage to
act on it kindly.

## Need support?

This lesson describes a particular grief: the grief with no one to blame. If you
have recognised your relationship here, you are not ungrateful, disloyal or
faithless. You are seeing clearly, which is what this course has asked of you
throughout. Seeing clearly can carry a particular heaviness because there is no
wrongdoing to organise the grief around and no one to blame.

Grief of this kind is worth taking to someone rather than carrying alone,
particularly if it is affecting your sleep, your work, or your ability to function
day to day. A suitably qualified counsellor may help, and so may a wise friend who
will not rush you towards a decision in either direction.

If you have been the one told no, and the loss has woken a fear that no other
relationship will come, Lesson 20 addresses that directly — and you do not have to
wait until you reach it. **Finding Help Where You Live →**

## Continue

You do not need to decide today, and this is not a lesson to act on within an hour
of reading it.

If you found nothing of yourself here — if the evidence presently suggests a good
relationship with ordinary work remaining — receive that encouragement without
treating this lesson as certification. Lesson 17 will help you consider how to
continue wisely.
