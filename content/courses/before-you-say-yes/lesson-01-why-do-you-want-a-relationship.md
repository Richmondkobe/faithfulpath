# Lesson 1 — Why Do You Want a Relationship?

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to name the motives that are driving
your desire for a relationship right now, and recognise which of them has already
influenced a decision you have made.

**Key Scripture**
"It is not good that the man should be alone." — Genesis 2:18 (ESV)

God says this of Adam before anything has gone wrong, in a world he has just
called good. The immediate context is the creation of marriage, but the wider
point stands on its own: people were made for relationship, not isolation.
Scripture never treats the longing for companionship as a weakness to be repented
of. It does not follow that everyone must marry in order to live a complete life
— but it does mean the desire itself is not your problem to solve.

## Core teaching

Most dating advice begins with the other person. What to look for. What to avoid.
How to tell whether someone is right for you. This course will get there. It
begins somewhere else, because before you can usefully ask "Is this person right
for me?" you need to ask a harder question: why do I want a relationship now, and
what am I hoping it will give me?

That sounds simple. It is not. Many people have never answered it honestly, and
some have never been asked. We assume the answer is obvious — because I am an
adult, because I am lonely, because everyone else is married, because I want
children. Some of those are good reasons. Some are mixed. Some are not reasons at
all but pressures we have stopped noticing.

This matters for a practical reason rather than a philosophical one. **The motives
you bring into dating shape what you are willing to overlook.** Someone dating
because they are afraid of being alone may tolerate what a person at peace would
not accept. Someone dating to prove something to their family will choose
differently from someone dating to build a life with a companion. Your reasons
shape your eyes. If you do not know your reasons, you do not yet know what your
eyes may refuse to see.

Seven motives shape most Christian dating, and most people will recognise several
of them at once. This is not a list of sins.

**Loneliness** is real, and heavier than people who are not lonely tend to
understand. It is also a poor decision-maker. When you are lonely, presence feels
like love and attention feels like care. The useful question is not "am I
lonely?" — you may well be. It is: what do I value about this person besides the
relief of not being alone?

**Pressure from church and family** is usually well meant, and is a common reason
people say yes to someone they should have refused. If you catch yourself
thinking "at least my mother would stop asking," you are not choosing a spouse.
You are trying to end a conversation.

**Sexual desire** is normal, and Scripture names it without embarrassment
(1 Corinthians 7:9). But it has the same weakness as loneliness: it makes
proximity feel like compatibility. If the physical attraction were removed, what
would be left?

**Age anxiety** lowers your standards while telling you that you are being
realistic. It reframes patience as pride. It says "this may be your last chance."
Wisdom answers that an unhealthy or deeply unsuitable marriage is not a final
chance to seize. Fear of remaining single is not a sound reason to enter a
relationship that the evidence does not support.

**Social comparison** does not tell you what you want. It tells you what other
people appear to have. Those are different things, and there is no schedule you
have failed to keep.

**The wish to be rescued** is subtler — the hope that the right person will arrive
and a stuck life will finally start. It rarely works. A relationship adds a
person to your life; it does not do the work of building one. When that wish is
strong, controlling people may be drawn to the opportunity to be needed.

**Genuine readiness** is the seventh, and some people do have it. They are
learning to take their identity from Christ rather than from their relationship
status. Their circumstances may be unsettled — between jobs, changing churches,
recovering from loss — but they are not asking another person to become their
whole source of security and purpose.

Nobody sits entirely in that last category. Every honest person has some
loneliness, some pressure, some anxiety mixed in. The goal is not to purify your
motives until only readiness remains. It is to know the mixture, so that when a
decision comes you can tell which voice is speaking.

One word about contentment, because some readers have been told the answer to all
this is simply to be content in Christ and stop wanting a relationship. Paul
wrote that he had learned to be content in any situation (Philippians 4:11) — but
biblical contentment is not the absence of desire. It is the presence of trust.
Paul was content in prison and content in plenty. He did not stop preferring
freedom to chains. He learned that his peace did not depend on which he had.

That is the standard here too. Contentment does not mean you have stopped wanting
a spouse. It means your sense of who you are, and whether God is good to you,
does not rise and fall with whether one arrives. Someone with that kind of
contentment can date carefully, because they can afford to say no. Someone
without it may struggle to refuse anyone, because every no feels like a door
closing on their last hope.

That is why this lesson comes first. Everything the rest of this course asks of
you — recognising warning signs, holding boundaries, discerning wisely, walking
away when you must — depends on your being able to survive the relationship not
working out.

If you are already in a relationship, these questions become more useful, not
less, because you can now ask: which of these motives made me say yes to this
person, and would I still say yes without it?

## Practical tool — Relationship Readiness Reflection

This is a guided self-reflection, not a diagnostic or psychological test. It will
not tell you whether you are "allowed" to date. It is designed to help you notice
which pressures are present in your life right now, how much they are influencing
your decisions, and what foundations you already have for dating wisely.

Set aside twenty to thirty minutes. Answer alone, in writing, and answer honestly
— nobody else will see this unless you choose to show them.

For each statement, mark how true it is of you at the moment:

**1** = Not true · **2** = Slightly true · **3** = Somewhat true · **4** = Mostly
true · **5** = Very true

### Part A — Pressures I am experiencing, and how they affect my decisions

Each motive has four statements. The first two ask whether the pressure is
present. The third asks whether it has influenced your decisions. The fourth asks
whether you have ways of responding to it that do not involve a dating decision.

**Loneliness**

1. I regularly experience significant loneliness.
2. Attention from a potential partner quickly creates strong emotional attachment
   in me.
3. I have considered remaining in an unhealthy or unsuitable relationship because
   being alone felt worse.
4. I have relationships and practices that help me respond to loneliness without
   making a dating decision.

**Pressure from church and family**

1. People close to me regularly ask when I will get married, or make me feel that
   my singleness disappoints them.
2. I feel like the odd one out at church or in my family because I am single.
3. I have pursued, continued or considered a relationship partly to satisfy other
   people's expectations.
4. I can hear other people's opinions about my relationship status without
   letting them decide for me.

**Sexual desire**

1. Sexual desire is a significant part of why I want to be in a relationship.
2. I find it difficult to imagine a serious relationship without a strong
   physical spark early on.
3. Physical attraction has, in the past, led me to overlook things I should have
   noticed.
4. I have honest ways of managing sexual desire that do not depend on rushing
   into a relationship.

**Age anxiety**

1. I feel that time is running out for me to find someone.
2. I worry that being careful now might mean missing my last chance.
3. I have lowered my standards, or considered doing so, because of my age.
4. I am able to think about my age honestly without it making me hurry.

**Social comparison**

1. Seeing engagements, weddings or babies on social media affects my mood.
2. I feel behind compared to friends or siblings.
3. I have felt pushed towards a relationship mainly because of what other people
   seemed to have.
4. I can see other people's happiness without measuring my own life against it.

**The wish to be rescued**

1. I hope a relationship will help me feel settled, stable or secure in ways I do
   not feel now.
2. I sometimes feel my life will not really begin until I am married.
3. I have been drawn to someone mainly because they seemed able to fix something
   in my life, or because I could fix something in theirs.
4. I am taking responsibility for building my own life, whether or not a
   relationship comes.

### Part B — Foundations that support wise dating

Tick each that is true.

- My relationship with God is steady whether or not I am dating. ☐
- I am actively part of friendships and a church community. ☐
- I have people in my life who would tell me the truth about a relationship, and
  I would listen. ☐
- I could say no to a relationship that was not right, even if I liked the
  person. ☐
- I can receive correction without feeling attacked. ☐
- I am taking responsibility for my own life — my work or study, my finances, my
  health, my past. ☐

### Part C — Reading your answers

**Part C does not total your answers.** It helps you read each group of four
together: whether the pressure is present, whether it has influenced a decision
and whether you have another way to respond to it.

More important than any single answer is whether that motive has caused you to
rush, overlook warning signs or remain where you were not treated well.

For each motive in Part A, look at the four answers together:

- **If the first two are high but the third is low**, the pressure is present but
  has not been running your decisions. That is worth noticing and worth
  protecting.
- **If the third is high**, the pressure has already influenced a decision at
  least once. Write that motive down somewhere you will see it. When you next
  feel strongly about someone, ask whether this motive is speaking.
- **If the fourth is low**, you may have few ways of responding to that pressure
  apart from finding a partner. That is one of the most useful things this
  reflection can show you, because it is something you can work on now.

For Part B: the foundations you tick do not cancel the pressures you identified.
They show what already supports wise dating. Anything you could not tick
identifies an area you may want to strengthen.

### Part D — Written reflection

Answer each in a few sentences.

1. Which motive is strongest for you right now? Were you surprised? Why or why
   not?
2. Think of a past relationship, a strong attraction, or a pattern you have
   noticed in yourself or in people close to you. Which of the motives was most
   active? What did it cause you — or them — to overlook?

*Before you finish:* Ask God to search you rather than only searching yourself.
You may use Psalm 139:23–24 as your prayer: "Search me, O God, and know my heart!
Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead
me in the way everlasting!"

## Next faithful step

Choose one area that would make you more ready than you are now. Choose only one,
and write what the next step would be.

- A steadier daily walk with God that does not depend on my circumstances
- Deeper friendships or a real place in a church community
- A clearer sense of my own purpose or work
- Financial or practical stability
- Healing from a past relationship, loss or harm
- Learning to tolerate being alone without it feeling like a crisis
- Other:

## Journal reflection

1. Finish honestly: "If I never married, the thing I would find hardest to accept
   is ______." What does your answer tell you?
2. Finish this one: "If I never married, my life would still be worth living
   because ______." If you struggle to complete it, that is not a failure. It is
   one of the most important things you have learned today, and it belongs in a
   conversation with someone you trust.

## Need support?

If reflecting on the second question leaves you feeling that your life is not
worth living, or thinking about harming yourself, stop the exercise and reach a
qualified professional, emergency service or crisis line where you are. Do not
stay alone with those thoughts. **Find help where you live →**
