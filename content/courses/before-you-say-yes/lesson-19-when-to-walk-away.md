# Lesson 19 — When to Walk Away

> ### Before you begin
> This lesson covers two very different situations. **Before you read any advice
> about how to end a relationship well, answer one question: are you afraid of how
> this person will react?**
>
> If you are, or if you are unsure, the ordinary guidance in this lesson is not for
> you and following it could put you at risk. Go to *Ending safely* below, and
> contact a specialist service before you do anything else.
>
> **Finding Help Where You Live →** · **Leave this page →**

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to tell which kind of ending you are
facing, end an ordinary relationship clearly and kindly, and — where the other
person's reaction is itself the danger — plan with a specialist rather than
improvising.

**Key Scripture**
"The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit." — Psalm
34:18 (ESV)

David is writing about God's people in trouble, not about the end of a dating
relationship. But the assurance belongs to hurting people generally, and it is worth
having in front of you before the rest: ending something that had to end is still a
loss, and God does not require you to pretend otherwise.

## Core teaching

The third answer is no. This lesson is about giving it well.

It comes last for a reason. Most of this course has been about seeing — and by now,
a great deal has usually been seen. But seeing is not the same as acting, and the
gap between the two is where many people live for months or years. They know. They
have known for some time. And they stay, because ending a relationship is one of the
hardest ordinary things a person does, and because Christians in particular are
often given no help with it. We are taught how to begin relationships and how to
sustain marriages. We are rarely taught how to end something well.

**Two situations, kept apart.** The first is the ordinary ending: two people of
goodwill, in a relationship that has turned out not to be right, or that has been
worked on and has not worked. The second is the ending of an unsafe relationship,
where the other person's reaction is itself the danger. The first is mostly about
honesty and kindness. The second is mostly about safety, and **the rules are
different — following the first set in the second situation can be dangerous.**
Answer the safety question before you read any further, and if you are unsure, treat
yourself as being in the second.

**Knowing it is time.** You do not need certainty. You do not need a villain. You do
not need the other person's agreement, your family's permission, or a word from God
that overrides your judgement. Discernment is allowed to conclude no — that is what
it was for — and **a freely given no is sufficient.** You are not required to prove a
case.

The seriousness of the reflection should match the seriousness and length of the
relationship. Someone three dates in does not need a worksheet to conclude that this
is not for them. **You do not owe any dating relationship a programme of repair, and
you never owe an unsafe one more time.**

*The incompatibility is directional.* You want materially different lives, and time
and effort have not changed that.

*The evidence did not come.* A difficulty was named, a fair period allowed, and the
change did not appear — or appeared only under threat, or depended on your
reminders, or did not survive the end of the conversation.

*Help was refused.* A serious problem is acknowledged when a breakup is threatened,
and the help it requires is repeatedly declined. Acknowledgement that exists to
prevent an ending is not responsibility.

*The whole relationship no longer holds.* The original concern may have been small,
but something that cannot be worked around has come back as a No: the shared
foundation, the freedom to say no, the presence of freely chosen desire on both
sides.

*There is harm.* Fear, coercion, control, intimidation, violence, sexual pressure,
financial exploitation, sustained degradation. If any of these is present you do not
need to have tried everything. You need to be safe.

And be truthful about what an ending is not. It is not a threat to produce change —
a breakup announced in order to be talked out of is a manipulation, and it teaches
the other person that your no does not mean no. It is not a test of whether they
will fight for you. It is not a request for reassurance. If you are using it as any
of those, stop and say what you actually want. But if what you want is for it to be
over, **that is reason enough.**

### Ending an ordinary relationship well

**Be clear.** A person being ended with deserves to know that this is what is
happening. Not *I need some space*. Not *let's take a break and see*. If you have
decided, say so in words that cannot be misheard as a question: *I have decided to
end our relationship. I am not asking you to change my mind.* It will feel brutal as
you say it. It is kinder than the alternative, which is someone spending weeks
trying to reopen a door you had already closed and did not tell them was closed.

**Do it directly, where it is safe to.** Where there is no safety concern and the
relationship has been serious, a face-to-face conversation is usually the most
respectful form. Choose a neutral place with enough privacy for dignity but with
other people nearby and a clear exit, and arrange your own transport. Not at a
family occasion, at church, or in front of friends. Where a relationship has been
brief, or largely online, a call or a clear written message is not a failure of
courage — match the medium to the relationship, not to your discomfort. And do not
leave them to work it out from your silence. The slow fade is not a gentler ending;
it is an ending the other person is made to perform on your behalf.

**Say why, briefly, without a verdict.** In an established relationship it is
generally respectful to give a brief truthful reason. That does not entitle them to
your private thoughts, an exhaustive account, or an explanation they consider
sufficient. **A reason is not a case.** One or two true sentences, then stop.

Resist two temptations. The first is softening the reason until it is no longer true
— *it's not you, I just need to focus on myself* — which leaves them with a reason
that does not fit what they experienced and will not survive their first question.
The second is making the reason so complete that it becomes an indictment. If the
relationship is ending because of their conduct, you may say so plainly, once. You
do not need them to agree.

**Do not negotiate, and do not offer a smaller relationship.** They may propose
changes, promises, counselling, a different pace. If you had wanted those, Lessons 17
and 18 were where to want them. And do not promise immediate friendship as a
consolation; it may become possible later, but it should not be used to soften a
decision or preserve access to a relationship that no longer exists.

**Do not use God to avoid owning your decision.** You may have prayed and sought
counsel and reached this before God; that is how it should be. But *I don't have
peace about it* is usually a way of avoiding a reason you could give but would rather
not — and, exactly as Lesson 13 warned when the words were used on you, attributing
the decision to God makes the other person unable to respond. Say *I have decided*,
and own the judgement.

**Prepare privately; do not announce publicly.** Tell one or two trustworthy people
beforehand for prayer, support or safety. They should not hear it from someone else.

**Agree, if you can, what will be said.** In a shared church or friendship group, an
ending is a public event whether you want it or not. A brief shared account — *we
decided we weren't right for each other* — spares you both. But **neither person may
forbid the other from speaking truthfully and confidentially to a pastor,
counsellor, advocate, lawyer or trusted supporter.** That matters most where
something went wrong that did not, at the time, register as unsafe.

**Then step back.** Communicate the post-breakup boundary clearly where it is safe to
do so. It may be time-limited or indefinite, and the other person does not have to
agree for your boundary to be valid. Return what needs returning. Do not send a
message a week later to check they are all right — that is for their friends, and
your checking will read as a door reopening. If you share a church, ask a trusted
leader to help establish respectful boundaries without automatically displacing
either person from their spiritual community; where safety is involved, the burden of
accommodation must not fall on the person at risk.

**Do not become the one who helps them through it.** Their grief is theirs, it is
real, and it is not evidence that you were wrong. Offer kindness in the ending, then
let their friends, family, pastor or counsellor support them.

### If they will not accept it

Sometimes the other person refuses to receive the ending. They keep calling. They
turn up. They write long messages explaining why you are mistaken. They tell you God
has shown them you are meant to be together, or that after everything they have
given, you owe them another chance. They enlist their family, your family, a pastor.

You have said no. You do not owe further explanation, further conversations, or a
hearing for the case they wish to make. Repeat the decision once, in the same words,
and then stop responding. If contact becomes persistent, threatening or frightening,
do not follow a universal rule about replying one more time. Follow specialist or
legal guidance about whether to respond, preserve communications, block contact or
report it.

**If they threaten self-harm**, contact an emergency or crisis service where the risk
appears immediate. If it is safe and appropriate, you may also alert a responsible
person who can reach them. Do not go to them alone, promise secrecy, bargain with the
breakup or become their sole crisis contact. A threat made conditional on your
staying is coercive even when the distress is genuine.

**And watch the escalation.** There is a difference between a grieving person who
cannot yet let go and a person who will not let you go. If contact continues after
you have stopped responding; if it moves to your workplace, your family, your
friends; if it includes threats, however veiled; if they appear where you are
uninvited; if they are tracking where you go — this is no longer grief. It is the
situation the next section is about, even if it did not look like it while you were
together.

### Ending safely

This is for anyone afraid of what will happen when they end the relationship. If you
are not sure whether that describes you, read it anyway. Fear is sometimes something
people only recognise once it is named.

One thing first. Abuse services in many countries report that the period around the
ending of a relationship is when the risk of harm from a controlling or violent
partner is highest, and that risk and stalking can continue after separation. No
printed page can give you a safety plan fitted to your circumstances. What this
lesson can do is tell you the shape of what specialists say, and tell you to find one
— **before you act, not after.** If you are in immediate danger, contact the
emergency service where you are or go to a safer place first; everything below is
planning, and planning must never delay urgent protection.

**If you are uncertain how they may react**, do not arrange a meeting merely to find
out. Contact a specialist service first and describe the behaviour that concerns you.
Let the resulting safety assessment determine whether the ending should happen in
person, remotely, through another person or without advance notice.

**If you are afraid.** Do not arrange a private conversation to end it. The instinct
to do it properly, face to face, is a good instinct in an ordinary ending and a
dangerous one here. **A phone call or a message is not cowardice; it is the
appropriate medium when the other person's reaction is itself the danger.** Before
you say or send anything:

- Tell at least one trustworthy person or specialist advocate what you are doing and
  when, and stay in contact through it.
- Identify the safest available place to go, following specialist advice. Ask the
  specialist how and when to secure essential documents, medication, money and other
  necessities. Moving them prematurely may be noticed, so do not make changes the
  person could detect without considering the risk and order of events.
- **Do not give notice.** Do not say *we need to talk this weekend*. A controlling
  person uses the notice period.
- Contact a specialist service first. They will help you plan the order of events.
  That is what they exist for, and it is not overreacting.

> **Digital safety.** If you suspect your phone, computer or accounts are monitored,
> **do not immediately remove applications, disable access or change settings on that
> device.** Sudden changes may alert the person and may destroy evidence you will
> later want. Use a safer device — one they have never accessed — to contact a
> specialist service, and ask them to help you decide what to preserve, what to
> change, when, and whether you need a new device or account. The things to work
> through with them include passwords on email, banking, social media and cloud
> accounts; location sharing on your phone and through apps you may have forgotten;
> unfamiliar applications; shared accounts and subscriptions; and whether a separate
> email address for anything to do with your safety would help. Which of these to do,
> and in what order, is a decision for you and the specialist together.

**Keep a record.** If there have been threats, unwanted contact or incidents you may
one day need to describe, write them down with dates. Do not keep the only copy on a
device or account the other person may access, and ask an advocate how to preserve
evidence safely and lawfully where you live. Keep only information already lawfully
in your possession and necessary for obtaining advice. Do not copy, forward or retain
illegal or intimate material in order to create evidence.

**Where family or community is the pressure.** Do not use family mediation or joint
pastoral counselling; both assume everyone is free to speak, and you are not. Do not
raise your plans at home, and do not travel abroad at a family's request while this
is unresolved. Contact a service specialising in forced marriage or family-based
coercion and let them advise on timing before you say anything. A responsible church
will support your freedom and safety; if leaders pressure you to comply, to disclose
your plans, or to reconcile without protection, seek independent specialist help.

**If you live together, share finances, or there is a child or a pregnancy.** Take
legal advice appropriate to your country — on housing, money, custody — from a legal
adviser rather than a pastor or friend, however wise. But legal planning must not
delay urgent protection: safety first, then the paperwork.

**Afterwards.** Follow the communication boundary in your safety plan — that may mean
no contact at all, or contact only through a solicitor, advocate or documented
channel. Do not respond to apologies, gifts or spiritual appeals. Tell selected
trustworthy people only what they need to know so that they do not disclose your
location, pass on information or arrange contact; follow the safety plan about who
should know what. If the person continues to contact you, follows you, or turns up,
seek specialist or legal advice promptly about documentation, protective options and
reporting procedures where you live. If danger is immediate, contact emergency
services. You do not have to wait for the behaviour to escalate before asking for
help.

### After

Whatever kind of ending it was, there is grief on the other side, and it is allowed.

If you ended it, you may be surprised how much it hurts, and you may take the hurt as
a sign you were wrong. It is not. **Grief is the cost of having cared, not evidence
that you should have stayed.** Do not fill the space immediately with someone else;
the weeks after an ending are when Lesson 1's question is most easily answered badly.

If you were told no, receive it as final. Do not stay in contact for the hidden
purpose of changing their mind. Grieve it as the real loss it is — and if it has
awakened a fear that no one else will come, Lesson 20 was written for you.

In both cases, take the ending back to the people who walked with you through the
relationship. Not to be told you were right, but to learn.

And if the ending was an unsafe one, be gentle with yourself about how long it took.
People stay in harmful relationships for reasons that make sense from inside them —
hope, love, fear, faith, children, money, shame — and leaving is usually a process
rather than an event. Whatever stage of leaving or recovery you are in, the steps you
have taken towards safety matter. Separation may be a process, and risk or unwanted
contact can continue afterwards.

Before the tool, a prayer. Pray it if it fits; leave it if it does not.

> *Lord, I have been carrying this for a long time and I am tired. Give me the
> courage to say the true thing once, plainly, and not to take it back because their
> face is hard to look at. Keep me safe while I take the next step. I entrust the
> other person to you without making myself responsible for their choices. And when
> it is done and the room is quiet, be near — you have promised to be near the
> brokenhearted, and I am going to need that. Amen.*

## Practical tool — Should We Continue? Decision Worksheet

**Two halves, and the first question decides which you use. Answer Part A before
anything else.**

> **If this worksheet could be discovered by the other person, do not complete or
> keep it.** Use a safer device, or speak directly to a specialist service.
> **Finding Help Where You Live →**

### Part A — The safety question

Answer *Yes / Not sure / No*.

- I am afraid of how this person will react if I end the relationship.
- This person has threatened, intimidated, controlled, followed, tracked, hit,
  sexually pressured or financially exploited me, or has threatened to harm
  themselves if I leave.
- I have to manage my words or behaviour to prevent this person becoming threatening
  or intimidating.
- This person has access to my devices, passwords, location, money, documents or
  immigration information.
- They have isolated me from people who might help me.
- I am afraid of what my family or community will do if I end it.
- I believe that seeking help, or completing this worksheet, could place me at risk.

> **If any answer is Yes or Not sure, do not complete Parts B to D. Go directly to
> Part E, and contact a specialist service before you act.**

### Part B — What I have established

From the tools you have already used, if the relationship has been serious enough to
warrant them. If it has not, go to Part D.

- The concern, from Lesson 15, 16, 17 or 18:
- What has been tried, and for how long:
- What changed:
- What did not:
- Which description fits: directional incompatibility · evidence did not come · help
  refused · whole relationship no longer holds
- Which *No* or *Not sure* answers remain in the whole-relationship check:

### Part C — What may be influencing me

Answer truthfully.

- I am staying because ending it would take a conversation I do not want to have.
- I am staying because of what it has already cost me.
- I am staying because they have promised to change.
- I am staying because I am afraid there will be no one else. *(Lesson 20)*
- I am staying because ending it will disappoint my family, my church, or people who
  have invested in us.
- I am staying because I would feel guilty.
- I am staying because I believe God brought us together and ending it would be
  disobedience. *(Lesson 13)*

> These do not make the decision for you, and they do not have to be resolved before
> you leave. They show what may be influencing you — bring them into the light, so
> that fear, guilt, pressure or the cost already paid does not quietly decide in your
> place.

And in the other direction:

- I am ending it to see whether they will fight for me.
- I am ending it because I want to be talked out of it.
- I am ending it as a threat, to produce change.

> If you are using an ending as a test, a threat or a request for reassurance, stop
> and say truthfully what you actually want. You remain free to end a dating
> relationship without proving that your reasons are perfect.

### Part D — The decision

A dating relationship continues only with two freely given yeses. It ends when either
person gives a clear no. Choose one and write why in a sentence.

- **Continue.** *(Lesson 17)*
- **Slow down.** *(Lesson 18)*
- **End it.**
- **I am not ready to choose yet.**

If you are ending it, the plan:

- How I will tell them — in person, by phone, in writing — and why that fits:
- Where and when, with other people nearby, a clear exit and my own transport:
- The one or two people I will tell beforehand, for support:
- The decision, in words that cannot be heard as a question:
- The reason, in one or two true sentences, without a verdict:
- What I will not do: negotiate · promise immediate friendship · give a reason that
  is not true · attribute the decision to God instead of owning it · let it become
  public before I have told them
- What I will say publicly, preserving both truth and appropriate privacy:
- The communication boundary I will establish, including any unavoidable practical
  contact:
- What needs returning:
- Who I will talk to afterwards, and what I want to learn:

### Part E — Ending safely

> **Do not enter or save your safety plan in this course.** The prompts below show
> what a specialist may help you consider; they are not fields to complete here. A
> personalised plan may contain information that would increase danger if
> discovered. Create and store it only in the way your specialist adviser
> recommends.

A specialist will usually help you think through:

- Which service to contact, and what they advise about timing.
- If you are in immediate danger: the emergency service or safer place to go first.
- At least one trustworthy person or advocate who knows what you are doing and when.
- The safest available place you can go, following their advice.
- How and when to secure documents, medication, money and essentials.
- The medium you will use — not a private conversation.
- What you will say, briefly.
- A digital-safety plan: a safer device, what to preserve, password changes and
  their timing, location access, shared accounts.
- A record of threats or incidents, kept where they cannot reach it.
- If they threaten self-harm: which emergency or crisis service to contact, and
  whether to alert a responsible person close to them.
- If family or community pressure is involved: which specialist service to contact,
  and their advice on what to say at home and when.
- If you live together, share finances, or there is a child or pregnancy: legal
  advice, after safety is secured.
- Afterwards: the communication boundary, who to tell not to pass on information,
  where to report continued contact, and who will support you.

*Before you finish:* ending a relationship, even one that had to end, is a loss, and
you do not have to pretend otherwise before God. Pray for the other person — for
their safety, their healing and their future, whether or not you can imagine it.
Then pray for yourself. You have done a hard thing. Let him be near.

## Next faithful step

**If Part A returned any Yes or Not sure:** your next step is not a conversation. It
is contacting a specialist service and letting them help you plan the order of
events. Do not give notice, do not arrange a private meeting, and do not make sudden
changes to devices or accounts before you have spoken to them.

**If Part A was clear and you have decided:** write the two sentences — the decision
and the reason — and read them aloud to yourself until you can say them without
softening.

**If you have not decided:** name the one thing that would have to be true for you to
stay, and ask whether you have already seen the answer.

## Journal reflection

1. If you already know, how long have you known? What has kept the knowing and the
   doing apart?
2. What do you understand now that you did not understand earlier? What would you
   want to carry into a future relationship — without blaming yourself for
   information, coercion or danger you could not clearly see at the time?

## Need support?

If you are afraid of what will happen when you end this, you are the person this
lesson's safety section was written for, and a specialist abuse service is the right
first call — before a conversation, before a pastor, before any change to your
accounts or devices. They will help you plan the timing, and timing is the part
people most often get wrong alone.

Where forced marriage or family coercion is involved, contact a service that
specialises in it and do not raise your plans at home. Specialist forced-marriage
services generally operate in office hours. Outside those hours, contact a 24-hour
domestic abuse service, or — where there is immediate risk, including being taken
abroad — the police or emergency services. In the UK the Forced Marriage Unit has an
out-of-hours number: 020 7008 1500, asking for the Global Response Centre.

Where there are children, shared finances or shared housing, you will need legal
advice as well — but safety comes first and paperwork second.

And if what you are carrying after an ending is more than grief — if you cannot
function, or the loss has taken you somewhere dark — that is worth taking to a
suitably qualified professional now rather than waiting to see whether it lifts.

## Continue

Unless immediate protection or time-sensitive help is needed, you do not have to
complete an ordinary ending today or perform it perfectly. If safety is involved,
follow the timing agreed with a specialist rather than the pace suggested by this
lesson. Pain after an ending does not prove the decision was wrong. Stay connected to
safe support and give grief the time you would give a friend.
