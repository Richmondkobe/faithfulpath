# Before You Say Yes to Engagement

This course has been about dating, and it has deliberately stopped short of
engagement. But some of you arrive here in a relationship that has been examined
carefully and is now moving towards a different question — not *should we continue?*
but *are we ready to move intentionally towards marriage?*

This page is about that threshold. It will help you recognise when you have reached
it, and when you have not.

## What engagement is

**A mutually declared intention to marry.** Serious, but not yet marriage — the
marriage covenant is ordinarily entered through vows and recognised within your
church, community and applicable law.

That distinction matters in both directions.

It means engagement is **not a light thing.** Two people who become engaged have told
their families, their church and each other that they intend to marry. Ending an
engagement may carry emotional, financial and communal costs; depending on your
country and your arrangements, questions may also arise about deposits, jointly owned
property or gifts. The legal position varies and should not be assumed. So engagement
is not the way to find out whether you are interested in marriage. Dating should
already have established a genuine, mutual intention. Engagement is then a focused
season of preparation, counsel and deeper conversation.

And it means engagement is **not irreversible.** Important new information may still
arise during that season, and that is precisely why engagement remains breakable. An
engagement can be paused, and it can be ended, and either is far better than a
marriage entered because the invitations were already printed. If you are engaged and
something in this course has troubled you, Lesson 16 is where to take it — and Lessons
6, 10 and 19 where safety applies.

## Essential before engagement

These are not signs. They are conditions. **If any is missing, engagement is not the
next step, whatever else is true.**

**Both people freely intend marriage.** Not one of you wanting it and the other
agreeing. Not one of you wanting it and the other unable to say no. Continuing
requires two freely given yeses; engagement requires the same, with more weight. **A
reluctant yes is not made free merely because it is eventually spoken.**

**Neither person is being pressured or frightened.** If saying *not yet* would expose
you to threats, punishment, spiritual condemnation or family retaliation, do not
complete the engagement questions jointly. Pressure need not be physical to remove
consent; it may be emotional, spiritual, financial or familial.

**No known directional incompatibility is being ignored.** If Lesson 15 placed
something in the incompatible group, or Lesson 16 confirmed it, engagement does not
resolve it. It only makes it public.

**No serious concern is currently being examined.** Do not become engaged while a
significant difficulty is under review. If you are in a slowdown, finish it. If a
serious pattern has only recently been acknowledged, allow enough time to see
responsibility, appropriate help and sustained change. Do not require perfection or
the absence of every struggle; look for honesty, ownership, stability and evidence
that the concern is being handled responsibly.

This does not apply to abuse, coercion or fear. Those are not difficulties to examine
within the relationship; begin with individual specialist support.

**Both understand what engagement is** — a season of preparation for marriage, not an
experiment with the idea of it.

## Signs that you may be near the threshold

With the essentials in place, these are descriptions of what the threshold usually
looks like. They are not a scorecard, and no number of them produces a decision.

**The important questions have been faced.** Not necessarily every tool — a couple who
never needed a slowdown will not have a slowdown review. But you are not moving
towards engagement to silence a known concern, force the relationship to progress, or
find out whether you want marriage.

**You know each other across ordinary life.** Enough varied, ordinary circumstances to
distinguish presented character from repeated character: tiredness, disappointment,
disagreement, family interaction, financial decisions, disrupted plans, everyday
responsibility. You do not need to manufacture tests. The question is whether enough
ordinary life has been shared to support more than an idealised impression.

**Words and observed character broadly agree.** What each of you says about
yourselves, and what the other has seen over time, describe the same person.

**Your relationship is known, not hidden.** Wise, independent people have had enough
access to observe it and permission to speak — and you have listened to concrete
concerns rather than counting supporters and opponents. A group can be enthusiastic
while one perceptive person raises a valid point, or disapprove for reasons of
culture, class or denomination that are not about the relationship at all. Their
counsel informs your discernment; it does not replace the freely made decision of the
two people marrying.

**You have discussed the shape of married life.** Where, with what work, with what
shape of family, with what place for God — and the picture is one you both recognise
and want. If most of your conversations about the future are about the ceremony, you
are further from the threshold than the excitement suggests.

**The relationship can tolerate difficult questions.** Either of you can raise a
concern, ask for time, or say *I'm not sure about that* without punishment, sulking
or a spiritual rebuke. If you cannot, go back to the essentials above.

## The pressures that create a premature engagement

Every pressure this course has named shows up again here, and some show up harder.

**Family.** Parents who want grandchildren, who think you have dated long enough, who
have already begun planning. Their voice belongs in counsel. It does not belong in
the decision — and where family pressure shades into coercion, engagement is not the
way to relieve it.

**Church.** Congregations love a wedding, and some pastors are quicker to bless an
engagement than to ask whether it is wise. If your church has been planning the
wedding since your third month, the enthusiasm is kind, and it is not discernment.

**Age and time.** Fear of time is not a reason to propose. It is a reason to be honest
about time with the person you are with, and to decide with the fear separated from
the evidence.

**Sexual pressure.** Desire is a legitimate part of wanting marriage, and Scripture
does not treat it as shameful. But it is not evidence that this particular
relationship is ready. Engagement is not sexual permission, and a rushed wedding does
not repair weak character, incompatibility or an unsafe relationship. Ask whether you
would still consider this relationship ready if the immediate pressure were removed.

**Momentum.** The relationship has gone on for years. Everyone assumes. Ending it now
would feel like a betrayal of the time invested. The question is never what it has
cost, but what it is. **A long relationship is not owed an engagement.**

**The other person.** Sometimes the push comes from inside the relationship — one
partner ready, the other not, and the ready one pressing. Readiness is allowed to be
unequal. What is not allowed is turning your readiness into their obligation. If you
are the one who is ready, say so once, and then let them answer freely. If you are the
one who is not, say so too, and do not let their disappointment become your decision.

## How to approach the decision

**Talk about marriage before the formal proposal.** A proposal — or the equivalent
formal step in your culture — should not be the first time either person is asked to
consider marriage. The manner and timing may contain surprise; the underlying question
should not.

**Bring it to counsel.** Tell the people in your circle you are thinking about it, and
ask the direct question: *is there anything you have seen that we should look at
before we do this?* Each of you should ask independently, so neither person is
answering for the other or shaping what the other is willing to hear.

**Work through the questions.** The next page is a set of questions to answer before
engagement. They are threshold questions; they do not score and they do not certify.
Do them before the ring, or its equivalent, not after.

**Decide, and say it plainly.** When you have talked, taken counsel and worked through
the questions, decide — both of you — and say so in words. Then the proposal, however
you choose to do it, is a celebration of a decision already made rather than the
decision itself. **That is what a good engagement looks like: not a leap, but a step
you can both see the ground under.**

## Need support?

If you cannot say *not yet* without fear of what would follow, engagement is not a
decision you are currently free to make, and the joint questions on the next page are
not the right tool. Begin with a specialist service for individual guidance — a
domestic-abuse service, or one specialising in forced marriage and family coercion
where the pressure is coming from family or community. Do not raise your hesitation
at home first, and do not arrange mediation.

If a church leader is pressing the decision, seek advice outside that leader's
influence. Seniority in the same structure does not establish independence.

asked of you since Lesson 1. Go back to the lesson that fits, give the other person an
honest horizon, and do not move forward until both of you can do so freely.
