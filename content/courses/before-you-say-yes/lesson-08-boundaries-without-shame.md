# Lesson 8 — Boundaries Without Shame

**Lesson outcome**
By the end of this lesson you should be able to state your own limits in six areas
of a dating relationship plainly and without apology, and tell the difference
between a boundary, a request, an agreement and an attempt at control.

**Key Scripture**
"The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places; indeed, I have a beautiful
inheritance." — Psalm 16:6 (ESV)

The lines in the psalm are the boundaries of an allotted inheritance — a
surveyor's image, not a relational one. But the picture is worth keeping. In
Scripture, boundary lines are not deprivation. They mark out what is yours to
enjoy.

## Core teaching

The word *boundaries* makes some Christians uneasy. It sounds secular, or
selfish, as though love were being replaced by rules. Others have seen it used as
a weapon — *I'm setting a boundary* as a way of ending every conversation and
never being questioned. Both reactions are understandable, and both miss what a
boundary actually is.

This lesson covers the emotional, spiritual, digital, financial, time and
relational limits that shape whether a relationship is healthy long before anyone
touches anyone. Physical boundaries have their own lesson next. It is called
*without shame* because shame is what most often stops Christians from having
boundaries at all, and shame is what is most often used against them when they
do.

**A personal boundary identifies what you will participate in, what access you
will give, and what you will do when a legitimate limit is crossed.** It does not
guarantee another person's compliance. Requests and shared agreements may sit
alongside it, but your boundary concerns the choices available to you.

That matters, because one word gets used for four different things.

- **Boundary** — what I will participate in or do to protect a legitimate limit.
  *I will end the call if I am being shouted at.*
- **Request** — something I ask you to consider, which you may accept or decline.
  *Would you put your phone away while we have dinner?*
- **Agreement** — a commitment we both freely make. *We agree not to post
  photographs of each other without asking.*
- **Control** — an attempt to remove another person's reasonable freedom through
  pressure, monitoring or punishment. *You must give me your passwords so I can
  check whom you contact.*

Not every statement about another person's conduct is controlling. Relationships
require requests and agreements, and a couple who freely agree to phone-free
dinners have not oppressed each other. The question is whether both people keep
the freedom to discuss, decline, or recognise incompatibility without pressure or
punishment.

**Three things a boundary is not.** *Not a wall* — a wall keeps everyone out; a
boundary lets the right things in at the right pace, so that closeness can happen
safely rather than not at all. Where a relationship is unsafe, however, distance
or no contact may be a necessary protective measure rather than an unhealthy
wall. *Not a test* — you do not set one to see whether they will respect it,
though you will certainly learn that. You set it because it is true. *Not a
punishment* — where possible, name important boundaries before a crisis, but you
are allowed to recognise a limit later, revise it, or name it after experience has
shown you it was needed. Timing does not distinguish a boundary from punishment;
purpose, proportion and manner do. A consequence protects your participation or
safety. Punishment aims to cause suffering or secure submission. Ending a call
when shouting starts protects your participation. Refusing affection for days to
make someone give in is punishment.

And boundaries run both ways. The other person may identify and communicate their
limits too. That does not mean every proposed limit is wise or that you must
agree to it — sometimes two legitimate boundaries simply reveal incompatibility,
and sometimes a supposed boundary is an attempt at control. But a relationship in
which only one person may say *this far and no further* is not a relationship with
boundaries. It is a relationship with a ruler.

**Why they are not shameful.** Some Christians have absorbed the idea that a
loving person has no limits — that to say *no*, or *not yet*, or *that's not mine
to share*, is to fail at love. Scripture does not teach this. The Gospels do not
use modern boundary language, but Jesus withdrew from crowds who wanted more of
him (Luke 5:16), declined requests, and let people walk away. Nobody would call
him unloving. Matthew 5:37 — let your yes be yes and your no be no — concerns
truthful speech and unnecessary oaths in its own context, but by application it
reminds us that Christian speech should be plain rather than manipulative.
Someone whose yes and no are clear is easier to love, because they can be known.
Someone who never says no is not more generous; they are less legible, and often
quietly resentful.

Paul tells the Galatians to "bear one another's burdens" and, three verses later,
that "each will have to bear his own load" (Galatians 6:2, 5). The words differ —
a burden is a crushing weight, a load is what a person ordinarily carries.
Together they show that Christian community includes both mutual care and
personal responsibility: help with what is too heavy, but do not take over what is
theirs to carry.

And here is the point that most often changes people's minds. **You can only give
freely what you could have withheld.** A gift that could not have been refused is
not a gift. A closeness that had no limits was not chosen. Boundaries are what
make it possible to give yourself to another person as an act of love rather than
as an absence of alternatives.

**Six kinds.** A map, not a rulebook — the right lines differ between people and
cultures. But every dating relationship needs some line in each of these, and
most of the trouble comes from never having drawn one.

**Emotional.** How much of your inner life you share, and how fast. Whether you
become their only source of support, or they yours. You may care about someone's
feelings without becoming responsible for regulating every emotion or preventing
every disappointment. The Song of Songs repeats a refrain — "do not stir up or
awaken love until it pleases" (2:7). It is poetry rather than a dating timetable,
but it captures the wisdom of not forcing intimacy ahead of its season. Emotional
intimacy can outrun commitment just as physical intimacy can, and leave people
bonded to someone they have not had time to know.

**Spiritual.** Your walk with God remains yours. Praying together is not
inherently dangerous; many couples are strengthened by it. The concern is when
spiritual intimacy is deliberately intensified to create commitment, when private
prayer together replaces community, or when one person's spiritual language begins
to govern the other. Shared devotional life is a powerful accelerator of intimacy,
so make sure each of you keeps direct access to Christian community, trusted
counsel and personal discernment. Neither of you holds spiritual authority over
the other. If you no longer hear from God except through the person you are
dating, something has been crossed.

**Digital.** Phones, passwords, location sharing, messaging at all hours,
expectations about reply speed. Whether photographs may be posted, whether private
messages may be shown to friends, what happens to photographs and messages after
the relationship ends. A dating partner is not entitled to your accounts, and
demanding access can itself be controlling. Voluntary location or content sharing
differs from demanded surveillance, but consent can be withdrawn at any time.
Passwords and account credentials should ordinarily remain private even in a
trusted relationship, because they may also expose other people's messages and
confidential information. Share with the awareness that messages, photographs and
recordings can be copied, stored or distributed beyond your control. Never
pressure anyone to create or send intimate material, never share another person's
content without consent, and treat AI-generated or altered intimate images of
anyone as the serious violation they are. And if you suspect your own device is
monitored, do not simply change passwords or switch off location sharing — sudden
changes can alert a controlling person. Use a safe device and get personalised
advice first.

**Financial.** Who pays for what, and whether it creates obligation. Whether you
lend. Whether you make joint purchases, share accounts or pay each other's debts.
In dating, the wise answer to most of that is *not yet*. Do not merge accounts,
co-sign debt or create significant financial dependency because a relationship is
serious; those decisions belong after marriage, or after proper legal and
financial advice where circumstances differ. Money merged early is very hard to
un-merge, and financial dependence in either direction changes the freedom with
which a relationship can be discerned or ended. Gifts do not purchase access,
affection, sex, commitment or influence over your decisions. Be especially
cautious about any request for money from someone you have not met in person.

**Time.** How much of your week the relationship takes, and what it displaces —
work, sleep, church, friends, family, rest, solitude. Whether you are expected to
be available at every hour, and whether a missed message is treated as neglect. A
relationship that consumes everything is not strong; it is unbalanced, and the
imbalance will show later. The person you are dating should be one part of a full
life, not the whole of a shrinking one.

**Relational.** Your friendships, including with people of the opposite sex. Your
family, and how involved they are. Former partners, and what contact is
appropriate. Privacy protects appropriate personal space; secrecy conceals what
materially affects the other person's safety, consent or ability to decide wisely.
On friendships, be fair in both directions: a partner should not control your
friendships through intimidation, monitoring or unilateral prohibition, and both
people may raise legitimate concerns and negotiate standards they both hold.
Transparency does not mean surrendering all privacy.

**How to set one.** *Know it where you can* — but you will discover some limits
only when discomfort reveals them, and you are still allowed to name a boundary
after something has happened. You are not required to permit repetition simply
because you had not anticipated it. *State it plainly*, neither as accusation nor
apology: *I don't lend money in a relationship* is enough; *I know this might seem
strange, and I hope you don't take it the wrong way…* invites negotiation of
something that was not up for negotiation. *Say what you will do* — *if the
conversation turns to my past before I'm ready, I'll say so and change the
subject* is a boundary; *you're not allowed to ask about my past* is not, though
*please don't ask about my past yet* is a perfectly reasonable request. *Follow
through where you can, and get help where you cannot* — following through is what
makes a boundary meaningful, but difficulty doing so does not make another
person's violation your fault. Fear, financial dependence, trauma and coercion all
make action harder. If you repeatedly cannot act because you fear their response,
seek outside support rather than blaming yourself or attempting a stronger
confrontation. *Watch the response* — acceptance or respectful clarification is a
green flag. Pressure, punishment, sulking, or spiritual reframing is not.

**When a line is crossed.** Name it calmly and specifically: *we agreed we
wouldn't message after eleven, and last night you called at one* — not *you never
respect me*. Weigh both the crossing and the response: severity may make one
incident decisive, and in less serious situations, distinguish a misunderstanding
from a lapse from a pattern. Someone who hears you, apologises and adjusts has
shown you something good. That applies to ordinary failures; coercion,
intimidation and control are not made acceptable by an apology. Allow honest
disagreement — they may think a proposed boundary unreasonable, and they are
allowed to say so. What matters is whether they discuss it respectfully and
refrain from violating it while the disagreement stands. If you cannot agree, the
result may be incompatibility rather than wrongdoing, and that is worth knowing
too. Then act on what you said you would do, if you safely can. That is not
punishment; it is you being the person you said you were.

And if the crossing is one of the things Lesson 6 called serious the first time,
this is no longer a boundary conversation. Boundaries are not a technique for
making an unsafe person behave better.

## Practical tool — Personal Boundaries and Dating Agreement

Two parts. **Part A is yours alone: your own limits, which do not require anyone's
agreement to be valid.** Part B is for the two of you, if you are in a
relationship. Part B is not a contract and cannot be enforced — it is a shared,
written, dated statement of what you have each committed to, so that neither of
you has to guess. Nothing here is scored.

*Before you finish:* pray that the lines you have drawn would be like the lines of

safely grow.

## Next faithful step

Choose one boundary from Part A that is relevant to a real situation in your
life. If it needs to be communicated now and doing so is safe, state it plainly
and record what you said. If no boundary currently needs communicating, write the
sentence you would use and keep it for when the situation naturally arises. Do not
create a test or initiate an unnecessary confrontation to complete this step.

If you fear someone's response, do not use this exercise to discover what they
will do. Seek individual support first.

## Journal reflection

1. Shame attaches to boundaries in three directions: shame for having them, shame
   used against you for keeping them, and shame at yourself for not keeping one.
   Which do you recognise, and where did it come from?
2. Is there a line you would have wanted that has already been crossed? What
   would it take to draw it again from where you are now?

## Need support?

If you cannot maintain a boundary because you fear what the other person will do,
the problem is not your resolve and better boundaries are not the answer. Fear,
financial dependence, trauma and coercion all make following through genuinely
difficult, and none of them makes another person's violation your fault. Begin
with a specialist abuse service for individual guidance rather than a stronger
confrontation. **Find help where you live →**

## Continue

If you are not in a relationship, Part A is the work: know your limits, as far as
you presently can, before another person's preferences begin shaping them.

If you are dating and can speak freely, discuss the boundaries relevant to your
relationship without requiring either person to disclose their private worksheet.

If a line has already been crossed, consider its seriousness before deciding what
comes next. In an otherwise safe relationship, you may communicate or restore the
boundary. If the crossing involved coercion, intimidation, surveillance, violence
or fear of retaliation, do not announce a new boundary and wait to see what
happens; seek individual specialist guidance first.
