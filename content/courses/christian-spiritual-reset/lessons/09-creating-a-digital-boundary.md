# Creating a Digital Boundary

Of the four ordinary questions from Lesson 6, the phone is one of the factors most likely to determine whether your retreat remains protected. You can have three protected days and a good location and still spend them checking. This lesson is about drawing a boundary you can actually keep, one that removes the noise without removing your access to help.

I want to say at the outset that this is not an anti-technology lesson. Your phone will carry the Bible, the map to your location, the number of your support person, and the means of calling for help. The goal is not to reject the device. It is to stop it from being the centre of your attention for a few days, so that something else can be.

## Why it matters more than it seems

Most of us underestimate how much of our inner life is now shaped by a screen. The phone is the first thing many people touch in the morning and the last thing at night. It fills every gap: the queue, the lift, the two minutes before a meeting, the moment of boredom, the moment of discomfort. Those gaps can become places where thoughts surface, spontaneous prayer begins, and a person notices how they actually feel. Constant digital input makes such moments less available.

A retreat is, in large part, the deliberate reopening of those gaps. That is why silence is so central to the sessions ahead, and it is why, for many readers, the phone will be the most persistent avoidable threat to sustained attention. Every time you reach for it, you close the gap again.

Paul was addressing matters very different from digital technology when he wrote in 1 Corinthians 6:12, "All things are lawful for me, but not all things are helpful. All things are lawful for me, but I will not be dominated by anything." Yet the principle invites a useful question: has something permissible gained more control over my attention than I intended? If the thought of turning the phone off produces something close to anxiety, that is worth noticing, not as a reason for shame but as information about what has been holding your attention.

And there is Martha. In Luke 10:38–42, Jesus visits the home of Martha and Mary. Mary sits at his feet and listens. Martha is "distracted with much serving," and when she complains, Jesus answers gently: "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary." Martha's situation was not about technology, and her service was not the problem; the problem was distraction in that moment. But her story gives us a helpful picture of good and legitimate activity crowding out attentive presence. A phone can do something similar during a retreat.

## What the boundary is for, and what it is not for

The boundary has one purpose: to protect your attention for God during the time you have set aside. It is not a test of willpower, a spiritual fast to be proud of, or a way of proving anything to anyone. If you slip and check something, you have not failed; you have noticed, and you can put it down again.

The boundary is also not a boundary against help. Lesson 5 was clear that emergency calling should remain available, and Lesson 6 asked you to keep reception where you can. Choose the arrangement that provides necessary safety without feeding repeated checking. Some readers will rest better with one scheduled check. Others will rest better knowing that a designated contact can call through at any time.

## Two arrangements

There are two fundamentally different ways to do this, and you need to choose one, because they do not combine. A phone in airplane mode cannot ordinarily receive a call, so "airplane mode with an emergency channel" is not an arrangement at all.

| Arrangement | Can calls reach you? | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Focus or Do Not Disturb with selected contacts | Yes | Parents, caregivers, anyone with safety needs |
| Airplane mode with scheduled checks | No, except during checks | Readers who need deep separation and have another route for emergencies |
| Phone held by retreat staff or support person | Indirectly | Retreat centres and supported formats |
| Basic phone for calls only | Yes | Readers who want no apps but need emergency access |

Option A: Remain reachable for emergencies. Keep cellular service on. Use your phone's Focus or Do Not Disturb setting to silence all apps and people except one or two designated emergency contacts. Disable notification badges and previews, and keep the phone face down or in another part of the room. Most phones allow selected contacts to ring through a silenced phone, but settings vary and change with updates, so test it before you leave.

Option B: Disconnect completely. Use airplane mode with Wi-Fi turned off. In this arrangement you will not receive ordinary emergency calls. Turn connectivity back on only during a scheduled check, or arrange for emergencies to be routed through the retreat centre, hotel, host, or another nearby person who can reach you physically.

Whichever you choose, test the arrangement before the retreat. Ask the designated person to call while the setting is active. Confirm that their call rings and that other notifications remain silent. If it fails, fix it before you go, not on Day 1.

## The rest of the arrangement

Before you leave, tell people. Send a short message to the people who normally reach you: family, colleagues, your ministry team, close friends. "I am away from Thursday to Sunday and will not be checking messages. If something urgent comes up, contact [name], who can reach me." That single message removes most of the anxiety, because the people who might need you know what to do, and you know that they know.

Choose one emergency contact and define "emergency." Ideally this is your designated support person, or a family member, who has your location and agrees to contact you only for a genuine emergency. If you have children, the person caring for them is usually the right choice. A genuine emergency may include a serious health or safety problem, an urgent issue involving a dependant, or a situation that cannot reasonably wait until the retreat ends. It does not include ordinary questions, routine work decisions, or matters that someone else can resolve. Adapt the definition to your responsibilities, and say it out loud to the person so there is no ambiguity. Ask them to call rather than send an ordinary message, and agree on what they should do if you do not answer: call again, contact the retreat property, or leave a message marked urgent.

Deal with the Bible problem. Opening a Bible app on a multifunction device places Scripture beside the same apps that usually compete for your attention. If you can, bring a printed Bible. If you cannot, a phone with notifications disabled and the Bible app open will work, but be honest with yourself about how it goes on Day 1, and switch to paper on Day 2 if it is not working.

The same applies to this course. If you are reading it as an ebook and your edition and device allow it, print the retreat session lessons from Module 4 for your personal use. Otherwise, download everything in advance and read on an e-reader or tablet that has no messaging apps, or use a distraction-reducing reading mode. A device limited to the Bible and this course presents far fewer distractions, though paper still offers the clearest separation.

Leave the other screens off. Television, laptop, tablet with email, smartwatch notifications. If you are at home, unplug the television or put the remote in a drawer. This is not about severity. It is about not having to make the decision fifty times a day.

Avoid publicly narrating the retreat while you are living it. Posting can turn private attention toward audience reaction, and it can expose your location. Communicate necessary safety information privately to trusted people. If you want to share a reflection publicly, wait until you have returned and had time to discern what should remain private.

## Prepare the device before you leave

- Download the Bible, this course, maps, any music, and emergency information for offline use
- Disable notification badges and lock-screen previews
- Remove social-media and email apps from the home screen, or temporarily sign out
- Turn off smartwatch notifications
- Write essential contact details and emergency numbers on paper
- Save the retreat address
- Bring a charger or power bank
- Test the emergency-contact arrangement
- Tell the designated person when the boundary begins and ends

## Music, podcasts, and worship

Some readers will want to know whether worship music is allowed. The answer is yes, in a limited way. Module 4 will occasionally suggest a song or a period of listening, and if music helps you enter prayer, use it. But the default of the retreat is silence, and I would ask you not to use music to fill every quiet moment. Silence may initially reveal how accustomed you are to constant input. Mild restlessness can be observed without alarm. If silence triggers panic, flashbacks, dissociation, or severe distress, follow Lesson 5's guidance: shorten the period, keep your eyes open, move gently, contact support, or pause the retreat.

For this guided retreat, leave podcasts, sermons, audiobooks, and additional teaching aside unless a session specifically directs you to one. They are valuable in ordinary life, but this program already provides structured input. The remaining space is for prayer, Scripture, rest, observation, and response.

## What to expect when you stop

If you are used to constant connection, the first day without it may feel strange, and it helps to know what is common.

You may reach for the phone without deciding to. This is habit, not weakness, and it may happen many times on Day 1. Each time, notice it, and return to what you were doing.

You may feel a low-level restlessness that has no obvious cause. Restlessness may ease as the retreat continues, though the timing differs from person to person. Some readers will enjoy the separation quickly; others will continue to find it difficult. Neither response determines the spiritual value of the retreat.

You may find that your mind is louder than you expected. Thoughts, worries, memories, and lists that were held down by the constant stream may surface. The return of ordinary thoughts and unfinished concerns is not necessarily a problem; it may simply mean you are noticing what constant input had obscured. Write down what surfaces, and the sessions ahead will give those thoughts somewhere to go. If the experience becomes severe or destabilising, do not interpret that as spiritual progress you must endure. Follow the safety guidance in Lesson 5.

In my pastoral experience, many people later describe the digital boundary as one of the most valuable parts of the retreat, and some choose to keep a version of it afterwards. Module 5 will help you think about that.

## Adjustments for other formats

One-day retreat. Everything above applies for the day. Tell people, choose your arrangement, and keep it from the moment you begin until you finish.

Three-hour reset. Keep the phone out of sight and block ordinary interruptions. If you must remain reachable for dependants or emergencies, use Option A and allow only the designated contact to ring through.

At-home retreat. This is the hardest format for a digital boundary because ordinary life continues. The realistic version is a boundary around the session time, not the whole week. For each session, put the phone in another room or use Option A for the duration. Between sessions, live normally. Where your responsibilities permit, consider avoiding social media and non-essential news for the week, and keep necessary digital activity deliberate and limited rather than allowing it to spread through the entire day.

## A written agreement

It helps to write the boundary down before you go, because a boundary written on paper is easier to keep than one held vaguely in mind. Copy the following into your journal and fill it in:

For my retreat from ______ to ______, I will use the following arrangement (tick one):

☐ Emergency-call option: cellular service remains on, but Focus or Do Not Disturb allows calls only from ______.

☐ Scheduled-check option: the phone remains disconnected except for checks at ______.

☐ Property-contact option: my emergency contact will call ______, who can reach me at the retreat location.

I tested this arrangement on ______. My emergency contact is ______, and we have agreed what counts as an emergency. I have told ______, ______, and ______ that I am unavailable. I will read Scripture on ______ and this course on ______. I will not post about the retreat while it is happening. If I slip, I will put the phone down and return to what I was doing without guilt.

Signed: ______

If you are retreating with a spouse or group, discuss the shared expectations in advance. Each person should state their own boundary and any legitimate exceptions. Encourage one another without monitoring, shaming, or confiscating devices.
