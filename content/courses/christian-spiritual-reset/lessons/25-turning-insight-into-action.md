# Turning Insight Into Action

## Key Scripture

> "But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves."
>
> — James 1:22 (ESV)

## In brief

Lesson 24 dealt with impressions that need careful testing. This lesson is about the steps you have already identified as low-risk, responsible, and reversible: the Act, Share, and Seek-counsel cards from Session Three, the action and person from Session Ten, and, if you completed Session Nine, your "one faithful thing". A retreat can begin a change, but insight takes root through what you practise afterwards. The work of these two weeks does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be actual.

**Hearing and doing.** James compares a person who hears the word and does nothing to someone who looks in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what they saw. It is a precise description of the post-retreat dip: three days of seeing yourself clearly, then a week in which the image fades because nothing was done with it. What happened on the retreat is the hearing. This lesson is the doing.

**Nehemiah: insight, then a plan.** Nehemiah's burden for Jerusalem came in prayer, and he wept and fasted "for days". He did not resign his post. Months later, when the king asked why he looked sad, he prayed silently and asked for exactly what he needed: leave, letters, timber. He inspected the walls quietly at night before telling anyone, and when he did tell them, the work was divided into sections small enough for families to build. Insight, then time. A plan with specifics. A quiet look at the facts before the announcement. That is the opposite of coming home and announcing everything on the first evening.

Specific requests are easier to answer than general appeals, and a request lets the other person answer honestly. Expect some to say no, and some to be glad they were asked. *Seek-counsel cards:* make the appointment. Not the decision; the appointment. The card is done when the date is in the diary. If you cannot book immediately, take the next access step: identify two providers, join a waiting list, ask for a referral, find out the cost. Do not let an unavailable appointment become no action. If you have been putting off a medical or mental-health appointment, this is the card that matters most. *Entrust cards:* these have no action; that is the point. Read it, pray it, put it back. *Repairs from Session Five:* an apology is an Act card with Lesson 8's cautions. Centre the harm done to the other person, do not pressure them to respond, and do not make contact if it would be unsafe, unwanted, or legally restricted.

**Structural change: the Jethro conversation.** Rest does not fix a structure. A missed rhythm is restarted, not abandoned. An appointment weeks away is still booked, which is more than it was. Doing the word is a habit, and habits are built by resuming, not by never failing.

## Hearing and doing

James 1:22–25 compares a person who hears the word and does nothing to someone who looks in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what they saw. It is a precise description of the post-retreat dip: three days of seeing yourself clearly, followed by a week in which the image fades because nothing was done with it. James's alternative is the person who "looks into the perfect law, the law of liberty, and perseveres, being no hearer who forgets but a doer who acts."

Jesus said the same thing more starkly at the end of the Sermon on the Mount. In Luke 6:46–49, the difference between the house that stands and the house that falls is not what the two builders heard; they heard the same words. It is that one "hears my words and does them" and the other hears and does not. The flood comes to both houses. What happened on the retreat is the hearing. This lesson is the doing.

## Nehemiah: insight, then a plan

Nehemiah is a useful model here, because his story shows how a burden received in prayer became a project without becoming a stunt. In Nehemiah 1, he hears that Jerusalem's walls are broken, and he weeps, fasts, and prays "for days." He does not resign his post. Nehemiah 2 opens some months later; he has been carrying the burden and waiting for the right moment, and when the king asks why he looks sad, he prays silently and then asks for what he needs: leave, letters, and timber. He has clearly thought it through. When he reaches Jerusalem, he inspects the walls quietly at night before telling anyone his plan, and when he does tell them, the work is divided into sections small enough for families to build.

Notice the sequence. Insight, then time. A plan with specifics. A quiet look at the facts before the announcement. Work broken into pieces people could actually do. Nehemiah's situation was not yours, but the shape is transferable, and it is the opposite of coming home from a retreat and announcing everything on the first evening.

## The cards, one kind at a time

The Act cards. Each of these names a next faithful step within your responsibility. This week, choose the one you wrote on Session Ten's action card, and do it. Not all of them; one. If it is a conversation, have it. If it is an appointment, book it. If it is a task you have been avoiding, do the first hour of it. When it is done, take the next Act card, and so on, at a pace of roughly one a week. The point is not speed. It is that the cards leave the pile and become things that happened.

The Share cards. These are the burdens that should not be carried alone, and they will not share themselves. First, decide what kind of sharing the card requires: being heard, receiving emotional or spiritual support, dividing a task, or asking the appropriate leader to redistribute responsibility. Not every shared burden is a task someone else should take over, and you may not have the authority to reassign it yourself.

Then ask. Choose the person the card names and request a short conversation. Say three things: this is the burden; this is how I have been carrying it alone; this is the specific help I am requesting. "I have been managing all the finances on my own and it is wearing me down; would you take over the monthly bills?" "I have been carrying the youth ministry alone and it is no longer sustainable; could we meet with the ministry leader to identify two people who can share the work?" Specific requests are easier to answer than general appeals. A request allows the other person to answer honestly; it is not a spiritual or emotional demand for them to rescue you. Expect that some people will say no, and that some will be glad to have been asked.

The Seek-counsel cards. These named the kind of guidance you need: a doctor, a counsellor, a lawyer, a financial adviser, a safeguarding lead, a domestic-abuse specialist, a pastor. This week, make the appointment. Not the decision; the appointment. The card is done when the date is in the diary. If you cannot book immediately, complete the next available step: identify two suitable providers, join the waiting list, ask your doctor for a referral, contact a helpline, or find out what the service will cost. Do not let an unavailable appointment become no action at all. For urgent safety or medical concerns, do not wait for an ordinary appointment; Lesson 5 applies. If you have been putting off a medical or mental-health appointment, this is the card that matters most, and Lesson 27 will say more.

The Entrust cards. These have no action. That is the point. If you find yourself trying to act on one, notice it; you may have been trying to control the thing again. Read the card, pray the words on it, and put it back. Entrust does not, however, mean neglecting a responsibility, tolerating harm, or refusing necessary help. It means releasing the outcome you cannot control after taking whatever responsible action is actually yours. If new facts show that action is required, reclassify the card rather than using "Entrust" to avoid it.

The repairs from Session Five. If your confession pointed to an apology or a repair, and you copied it to an Act card, it is an Act card like any other, with the cautions from Lesson 8. Centre the harm done to the other person, not your own relief, and do not pressure them to respond on your timetable. An apology does not create an obligation for the other person to forgive, reply, resume contact, or restore trust. Do not contact someone merely to relieve your conscience if contact would be unsafe, unwanted, legally restricted, or likely to burden them further; in such cases, seek qualified guidance about whether and how repair should be attempted. If the matter is serious in the way Session Five described, it is a Seek-counsel card first.

## Structural change: the Jethro conversation

Lesson 4 promised that Module 5 would help with the structural causes of burnout, and this is where it does. Rest, as Lesson 4 also said, does not fix a structure. A person can take three days and walk straight back into the pattern that emptied them, and many do, unless something about the pattern changes.

Go back to Exodus 18. Jethro's advice to Moses had three parts: you are doing too much, some of this can be done by others, and here is how to divide it. Every burned-out person needs a Jethro conversation, with themselves and with whoever has authority to change the workload or shares its consequences: a manager, an elder board, a spouse, a family member, a ministry team, a care coordinator. For caregivers and people with no organisation above them, that may mean extended family, community services, respite care, or professional support. Ask these questions, and write the answers down.

1. What am I doing that only I can do? Be strict. The list is usually shorter than it feels.
2. What am I doing that someone else could do, if they were asked and trusted? This is the Share list, seen structurally.
3. What am I doing that does not need to be done at all, by anyone? Every role accumulates these.
4. What am I doing because I am afraid of what will happen if I stop? Session Eight may have surfaced this.
5. What would this role look like if it were designed for a human being with legitimate limits, rather than depending on one person continually exceeding them?

Then take the answers to the person who can change something, and ask for one structural change. One. A day off that is protected. A task removed. A responsibility shared. A meeting that stops. If the answer is no, ask why and whether another form of relief is possible. A clear refusal is important information about the sustainability of the present arrangement, and it may belong on a "To test" card for Lesson 24.

Expect some resistance, not necessarily from ill will. A system that has grown accustomed to your overfunctioning will have to adjust when you stop. Some resistance may be practical and temporary; some may reveal that the system depends unfairly on your exhaustion. Pay attention to the difference. Neither makes you wrong to have asked.

## When it does not work

Some of this will fail. The person you asked will decline. The structural change will be refused. The appointment will be weeks away. The rhythm will be missed three days running.

None of that undoes the retreat. Session Ten's rhythm card had a line for exactly this: "When I miss it, I will start again the next day without guilt." Apply the same sentence to everything in this lesson. A refused request is information: ask why, consider whether the request was clear and appropriate, explore another person or another form of help, or move the larger question to the "To test" pile. A missed rhythm is restarted, not abandoned. An appointment weeks away is still booked, which is more than it was.

The house in Luke 6 was not built in a day, and the flood did not come on the first afternoon. Doing the word is a habit, and habits are built by resuming, not by never failing.
