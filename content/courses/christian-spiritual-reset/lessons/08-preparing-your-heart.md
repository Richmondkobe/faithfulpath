# Preparing Your Heart

A retreat does not begin when you arrive. It begins in the days before, when you decide whether to come toward it slowly or to crash into it at full speed. The way you approach the preceding week can make a meaningful difference. A little preparation may reduce the restlessness of the first day and help you arrive with greater attention. If you cannot complete this preparation, however, you can still take the retreat honestly and benefit from it. And a restless retreat is not necessarily a failed retreat. Sometimes restlessness is itself part of what needs to be noticed with compassion.

This chapter is about that week. It is short, and it asks for very little each day. But it asks for it deliberately, because your heart, like your body, does not stop moving the moment you tell it to.

## Why you cannot simply stop

Think about what happens when a person who has been running for months finally sits down. The body sits; the mind keeps running. Lists appear. Anxieties that were held off by activity move into the empty space. Many people who go on retreat with no preparation spend the first day, and sometimes the second, simply coming down from the speed they arrived at. That is not wasted, and Chapter 6 said as much. But if you can begin the slowing before you arrive, the retreat itself has more room.

Psalm 131 describes the destination: "I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me." A weaned child can remain with its mother without urgently seeking milk. The image is one of settled nearness: the child is not there only to obtain something but is content to remain. In the same way, the psalmist has learned to rest in God without demanding an immediate answer or outcome. He did not arrive at that stillness by accident; the verb is active. He calmed and quieted his soul. This chapter is about beginning to do the same.

## A simple practice for the week before

For the five to seven days before your retreat, set aside ten minutes a day. Not more, unless you want to. The purpose is to create a small, familiar path into quiet and to notice how your mind and body respond.

| Day | Psalm | Simple focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Psalm 23 | God's care |
| 2 | Psalm 62 | Rest and waiting |
| 3 | Psalm 27 | One central desire |
| 4 | Psalm 42 | Spiritual thirst |
| 5 | Psalm 63 | Longing for God |
| 6 | Psalm 139 | Honest self-examination |
| 7 | Psalm 131 | Quiet trust |

If your retreat is fewer than seven days away, choose from the list. Each day:

Read the psalm twice, the second time more slowly. Do not analyse every verse. Notice which words expose, comfort, challenge, or describe something in you. Allow the passage to bring your present condition into the light.

Sit quietly for up to three minutes. Set a timer so you do not have to watch the clock. Sit without trying to accomplish anything. Notice your breathing, your surroundings, and the phrase you selected from the psalm. If thoughts arise, acknowledge them without judgement and gently return your attention to the phrase. If three minutes is too much, begin with one. If closing your eyes feels uncomfortable, leave them open. If silence feels unsettling rather than calming, shorten it, or take a gentle walk while repeating the line. The purpose is attention, not endurance. It will help you notice what usually happens inside you when external activity becomes quiet.

Pray one sentence about the retreat. Something like: "Lord, I am coming to be with you. Prepare me, and prepare the days." That is enough.

By the end of the week, you may or may not feel calmer. Either way, you will have practised returning to quiet and learned something about how you respond when you slow down. Both are worth having before you begin.

## Noticing your resistance

Somewhere in that week, resistance may appear. It often does. It may arrive as a sudden urgency about tasks that could easily wait. It may arrive as a growing feeling that the retreat is self-indulgent, that this is not the right time, that someone else needs you more. It may arrive as a vague dread you cannot explain.

Resistance is information, not a command. It does not automatically mean you should cancel, and it does not automatically mean you should push through. Ask: What might this resistance be telling me? Is it ordinary reluctance to slow down, fear of what may surface, a practical problem, or a genuine concern about safety and readiness? What preparation or support would help me respond wisely?

Sometimes the answer is a responsibility that needs attention before you go; if so, handle it, and Chapter 2's closing questions already asked you to consider what must be arranged. Sometimes the answer is a real concern about safety, health, or readiness, and Chapter 5's four paths apply. And sometimes the resistance is reluctance to face something the quiet may surface: a grief, a truth about your work, a relationship you have been avoiding, a question about God you have not let yourself ask. If that is what you suspect, write it down and bring the page with you. Do not try to resolve it beforehand. Simply name it, so that when it appears on the retreat, you will recognise it as something you already expected. Respond to what you discover rather than treating every hesitation as spiritual avoidance.

Not every distraction, scheduling problem, or anxious feeling is spiritual opposition. Some are ordinary features of being human, and some require practical or professional attention. You do not need to dramatise the week before the retreat. Notice what is happening, pray honestly, and respond wisely.

Elijah's story reminds us that fear, exhaustion, and the desire to escape can accompany withdrawal. His wilderness experience did not begin as a peaceful spiritual exercise. Yet God met him with care in the condition in which he arrived. Whatever condition you arrive in, that is the God you are coming to.

## The matter of unfinished relationships

Jesus said something about worship that applies to retreat. In Matthew 5:23–24, in the Sermon on the Mount, he tells his listeners: "So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift." The principle is that devotion to God must not become a way of avoiding responsibility for harm we have caused. Where it is safe and appropriate, we should acknowledge the wrong, apologise without excuses, make restitution where possible, and leave the other person free to respond.

Applied to the week before a retreat, this means asking a simple question: is there anyone I have hurt, or am in open conflict with, whom I could reasonably approach before I go? Not every strained relationship. Not old wounds that need the retreat itself to be looked at. But a sharp word you have not apologised for, a debt you have not acknowledged, a misunderstanding you could begin addressing through one honest conversation. If something like that exists, and it is safe and wise to address it, do so before you leave. Make the apology without demanding forgiveness or immediate reconciliation. Do not approach someone merely to make yourself feel lighter before the retreat. Centre the harm done to them, accept that they may not forgive you immediately, and do not pressure them to resolve the relationship on your timetable.

Two cautions, both of which Chapter 5 anticipated. First, Jesus is speaking about a person who has something against you, a wrong you have done. He is not instructing you to chase reconciliation with everyone who has wronged you, and you are not required to contact someone who has harmed you or who is unsafe. Forgiveness and reconciliation are not the same thing, and the retreat's Session Six will hold that distinction carefully. Second, do not use this principle to talk yourself out of going. If a relationship is too complicated to address in a week, it is precisely the sort of thing to bring to the retreat rather than a reason to postpone it.

## Confession before you go

The same logic applies to sin. Some readers carry a specific, known offence that they have not confessed to God, and it sits between them and any real stillness. Chapter 4 mentioned Psalm 32, where the psalmist describes the physical weight of keeping silent about his sin, and the relief when "I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity." 1 John 1:9 gives the promise plainly: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness."

If there is something like that in your life, name it to God in the week before. You do not need to wait for Session Five, which is about a deeper and gentler kind of confession, the kind that surfaces slowly in quiet. This is the other kind: the thing you already know. Confess it now and receive the forgiveness promised in Christ. Bring to the retreat any grief, consequences, repair, or accountability that still needs to be faced. Forgiveness removes condemnation; it does not make every consequence disappear.

If your actions harmed another person, or involve abuse, exploitation, criminal conduct, professional misconduct, or someone's present safety, seek qualified guidance about accountability, reporting, restitution, and appropriate disclosure. Do not use a private retreat or a confidential pastoral conversation to avoid necessary safeguarding or legal responsibility.

Do not, however, turn this into a search. Some readers will comb their lives for hidden sins to explain their dryness, and Chapter 4 already said that dryness is not always caused by sin. Confess what you know. Leave the rest to God. If you find yourself repeatedly confessing the same matter because you cannot feel forgiven, or compulsively searching for hidden sin, do not intensify the exercise. Speak with a pastor or mental-health professional who understands religious anxiety or scrupulosity.

## Praying for the retreat, and asking others to pray

Jehoshaphat's circumstances were very different from yours, a national crisis with an invading army, but the phrase in 2 Chronicles 20:3 captures a deliberate spiritual posture: he "was afraid and set his face to seek the LORD." Setting your face is a chosen act, and it is a good description of what this week is for. You are turning, in advance, toward the days you have set aside.

Pray for them. Pray for the practical things: that the arrangements hold, that the interruptions are few, that your body rests. Pray for the deeper things: that you will be honest, that you will not run, that God will meet you in whatever way he chooses. And ask others to pray too. If you have told a support person about the retreat, ask them to pray for you on each of the days. If you belong to a small group or a church, you might ask one or two people there. Choose someone trustworthy, emotionally steady, respectful of confidentiality, and willing to support without demanding details or interpreting the retreat for you. You do not need to explain why you are going. "I am taking some days to be with God, and I would value your prayers" is enough.

This is not a formality. James 5:16 speaks of the prayer of a righteous person having "great power as it is working." Being prayed for during a retreat is a quiet strength. Chapter 3 showed that Jesus sometimes prayed alone and, on significant occasions, brought trusted disciples nearby. Asking someone to pray connects your solitude to the community you will return to.

## Handing over well

One practical note, because it affects the heart more than people expect. If you are leaving work, ministry, or family responsibilities in someone else's hands, hand them over properly. Write down what needs doing and who is doing it. Tell people when you will return and how genuine emergencies should be handled. Whenever possible, route urgent contact through one designated person rather than remaining available to everyone. Give the person covering for you clearly defined authority, the information they genuinely need, and permission to handle agreed matters without consulting you.

Moses' father-in-law told him that carrying everything alone was "not good," and the remedy was to share the load with people who could bear it (Exodus 18:17–23). A poorly handed-over responsibility will follow you into the retreat as a low hum of worry. A well-handed-over one lets you be absent in fact, not only in location.

## Before you turn the page

1. Which day this week will you begin the ten-minute practice? Write the time and the place.
2. What resistance have you already noticed? Write down, without analysing it, what you think it may be telling you: ordinary reluctance, a practical problem, a safety concern, or something the quiet may surface.
3. Is there a relationship or a known sin that Matthew 5 or 1 John 1 applies to, one you can and should address before you go? If yes, what will you do and when? If no, or if it is not safe, write that down so you are not carrying it as an unanswered question.
4. Who will you ask to pray for you, and what exactly will you say?
