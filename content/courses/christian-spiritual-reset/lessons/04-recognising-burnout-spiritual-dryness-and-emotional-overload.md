# Recognising Burnout, Spiritual Dryness, and Emotional Overload

The first three lessons have used words like tired, dry, and overloaded almost interchangeably. Before you enter the retreat, it will help to separate them, because they are not the same thing and they do not respond to the same care. A person who is burned out needs something different from a person who is spiritually dry, and both need something different from a person who is emotionally overloaded. Many readers will recognise themselves in more than one. That is common. But naming what is actually happening is the first honest act of the retreat.

A word of caution before we begin. What follows is a pastoral description, not a medical or psychological assessment. I am a pastor experienced in counselling, not a doctor. Some of the signs below overlap with depression, anxiety, chronic stress, and physical illness, and this lesson cannot tell you which is which. If you read this and think, this has gone further than tiredness, please take that seriously and read Lesson 5 carefully.

Here is the shape of the lesson in one view. These categories overlap and none of them is a diagnosis.

| Experience | Central difficulty | What may help |
|---|---|---|
| Burnout | Prolonged demands with inadequate recovery or support | Rest, boundaries, delegation, structural change, and possibly professional care |
| Spiritual dryness | God feels distant or spiritual practices feel empty | Honest prayer, Scripture, patient faith, Christian community, and exploring possible contributing factors |
| Emotional overload | More emotion and stress than you can presently process | Reduced demands, safe expression, supportive relationships, gradual processing, and professional help when needed |

## Burnout: when demands outrun recovery

Burnout is a state of deep exhaustion that can develop when prolonged demands continually exceed a person's resources, support, and opportunity to recover. Clinically, the World Health Organization uses the term specifically for chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. In this course I use it more broadly and pastorally, to include similar patterns of exhaustion arising from ministry, caregiving, parenting, and other sustained responsibilities. It often feels as though you have been pouring out for much longer than you have been receiving rest, help, encouragement, or care.

Scripture gives us a vivid picture of an overwhelmed leader in Numbers 11:10–15. Moses, who had confronted Pharaoh and led a nation through the sea, hears the people complaining about food and says to God, "I am not able to carry all this people alone; the burden is too heavy for me. If you will treat me like this, kill me at once." We should not impose a modern diagnosis on Moses, but his words portray a man who had become overwhelmed and could no longer carry his responsibility alone. The burden had become unsustainable, not because Moses lacked faith, but because one person was trying to carry what needed to be shared. Notice what God does. He tells Moses to gather seventy elders and shares the burden among them (Numbers 11:16–17). Earlier, in Exodus 18:13–23, Moses' father-in-law Jethro watches him judge the people from morning to evening and says plainly, "What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out." The remedy was not another demand for greater spiritual effort. God provided a structural answer: shared responsibility.

Common signs of burnout include:

- Dreading things you used to find meaningful, including ministry or service
- Feeling that you have nothing left to give, even to people you love
- Increasing cynicism or numbness toward the people you serve
- Resentment that surfaces as irritability, sarcasm, or withdrawal
- Finding it increasingly difficult to concentrate, complete responsibilities, or believe that your work makes a difference
- Needing more effort to accomplish tasks that once felt manageable
- Going through the motions of prayer and church without any sense of connection
- Physical changes such as persistent headaches, disrupted sleep, low energy, digestive problems, or frequent illness. (These should not automatically be attributed to burnout; new, severe, or persistent symptoms deserve medical evaluation.)
- A quiet fantasy of simply walking away from everything

If this describes you, the retreat will matter, but so will what comes after it. Burnout usually has structural causes, and Module 5 will help you look at them. A person can rest for three days and walk straight back into the pattern that emptied them.

## Spiritual dryness: when God seems absent

Spiritual dryness is different. A person can be well-rested, not overworked, and still find that God feels distant. Prayer feels like effort with no response. Scripture that once spoke now reads like a document. Worship leaves you unmoved. You may still believe everything you believed before, but belief has become a fact rather than a relationship.

Spiritual dryness is a pastoral description, not a clinical diagnosis. It names the experience of feeling distant from God or spiritually unresponsive, but it does not by itself explain why that experience is happening.

The Bible is full of this experience. Psalm 42:1–3 describes a soul that "thirsts for God" while people ask, "Where is your God?" While David is physically in the wilderness of Judah, Psalm 63:1 uses that landscape to express his intense thirst for God, "in a dry and weary land where there is no water." Psalm 13:1 asks, "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" These are not the prayers of people who have abandoned faith. They are the prayers of people who are holding on to faith while feeling nothing, and Scripture preserved them so that we would know the experience is not unusual.

It is important to say what dryness is not. It is not always the result of sin. Christians sometimes assume that if God feels far away, they must have done something to cause it, and they search their lives for the offence. Sometimes there is one; Psalm 32:3–4 describes how unconfessed sin drained the psalmist's strength "as by the heat of summer," and confession restored him. But often there is nothing to find. Job's suffering was not caused by the hidden wrongdoing his friends accused him of, yet he still cried, "Oh, that I knew where I might find him" (Job 23:3). Even when we cannot explain a season of dryness, it can become a place where faith learns to depend less on immediate feeling and more on God's revealed character.

Common signs of spiritual dryness include:

- Prayer that feels one-directional or mechanical
- Reading Scripture without feeling engaged, nourished, or personally responsive to what you are reading
- Feeling nothing in worship, and then feeling guilty about feeling nothing
- Wondering whether earlier experiences of God were real
- Avoiding prayer because it has become disappointing
- A vague sense of distance that you cannot trace to any specific cause

If this is you, the retreat will not force God to feel close again. Nobody can promise that, and you should be suspicious of anyone who does. What it can do is give you unhurried space to be honest about the distance, to sit with the passages above until you realise you are in good company, and to keep turning toward God whether or not the feeling returns. The psalmists did exactly that. Many laments move toward renewed trust even before circumstances change. Others, such as Psalm 88, end without visible relief. Scripture makes room for both experiences, and so does this retreat.

## Emotional overload: when there is too much to carry

The third condition is emotional overload. This is another pastoral description rather than a clinical category. I use it for the experience of feeling that accumulated emotions and demands have exceeded your present ability to process them: grief that was postponed because there was no time, disappointment that was never voiced, anger that was buried because it seemed unspiritual, fear that has been running in the background for months.

Overloaded people often look fine. They are frequently the strong ones. But the weight shows up in unexpected tears, in disproportionate reactions to small things, in an inability to concentrate, in a flatness that has replaced both joy and sorrow. The heart can begin to feel like an overcrowded room. When too much has been stored there without attention, even comfort may be difficult to receive.

Scripture does not pretend otherwise. In Matthew 9:36, Jesus saw the crowds and "had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd." In 2 Corinthians 1:8–9, Paul admits that in Asia he and his companions were "so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself." The apostle who wrote about joy in every circumstance also knew what it was to be crushed. And Psalm 55:22 invites us to "cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you," while 1 Peter 5:7 repeats it: "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you." Both passages invite us to place what we carry into God's care. This does not always mean that the responsibility or the feeling immediately disappears. It means we no longer have to carry it as though we were alone. Overloaded people usually do not believe that. They have carried things so long that putting them down no longer feels like an option.

Common signs of emotional overload include:

- Crying, or being close to it, without an obvious trigger
- Reacting to small problems as if they were large ones
- Difficulty concentrating, making decisions, or remembering things
- A sense of being emotionally flat, as though nothing reaches you
- Avoiding people or conversations because you cannot manage one more thing
- Losses or hurts from months or years ago that still feel raw when touched

For this reader, the retreat's middle sessions will create gentle opportunities for lament, confession, release, and forgiveness. You will not be pressured to resolve everything in three days. Some experiences may need to be approached gradually and with the support of a qualified counsellor, and the retreat will tell you when to pause.

## The whole person

Because you are a whole person, spiritual, emotional, relational, and physical factors can affect one another. A medical condition can make prayer difficult. Grief can disturb sleep. Overwork can make God feel distant. Unconfessed sin can trouble the conscience, but not every troubling symptom is evidence of sin. Wise care asks about the whole person rather than forcing every experience into a single explanation, whether that explanation is purely medical or purely spiritual.

These three experiences can reinforce one another. Burnout may leave little energy for prayer. Spiritual dryness may make grief feel lonelier. Unprocessed emotional pain may intensify exhaustion. The relationship will be different for each person, and sometimes all three occur alongside a medical or psychological condition. If you find yourself somewhere in the middle of all three, that does not mean you are beyond help. It means the retreat needs to be gentle and unhurried, which is why three days is recommended over three hours.

## Where this stops being pastoral

There is a point at which these descriptions stop being adequate. If low mood, emptiness, or loss of interest has been present most days for two weeks or longer, or if these experiences are significantly affecting your work, relationships, sleep, eating, or ability to function, please speak with a doctor or qualified mental-health professional. You do not need to wait until your condition becomes severe before asking for help. What you are experiencing may not be burnout or dryness, or may not be only that, and a retreat is not a substitute for that care.

If you have thoughts of not wanting to be alive, tell a trusted person and contact a qualified mental-health professional or crisis service promptly. If you believe you may act on those thoughts, have formed a plan, or cannot keep yourself safe, do not continue the retreat alone. Contact emergency services or a crisis line in your country now, and ask a trusted person to stay with you. A retreat can wait. Your immediate safety cannot.

Lesson 5 will say more about how pastoral care and professional care work together. It is not optional reading for anyone who recognised themselves in the last two paragraphs.

## A personal check-in

Before you go on, go back through the three lists of signs and mark every line that describes you in the past month. Do it honestly; nobody else will see it. This is not a scored test, and the category with the most marks is not a diagnosis. The purpose is simply to help you notice what deserves attention.

1. Which of the three descriptions had the most marks: burnout, spiritual dryness, or emotional overload? Which had the fewest?
2. How long has this been going on? Weeks, months, or longer? When did you first notice?
3. Is there a specific event you can connect it to (a loss, a move, a conflict, a change of role), or did it build gradually with no obvious cause?
4. Did the section "Where this stops being pastoral" describe you? If so, what support do you need today? Write the name of the person, doctor, counsellor, crisis service, or emergency service you will contact. If you may be in immediate danger, stop here and make that contact now. Keep your answers. Session One of the retreat will ask you to bring them, and you will not have to explain to God what you have not yet admitted to yourself.
