# Finding Help Where You Live

Checked September 2026. Numbers and services change; verify before relying on any of them.

This course is used in many countries, and a list of one country's phone numbers would be useless to most readers. Instead, this lesson tells you what kinds of help exist, how to find them where you live, and what to do if you cannot.

## In an emergency

Emergency numbers vary by country and sometimes by service or region. If you are in immediate danger, may act on thoughts of suicide, cannot keep yourself safe, or have a medical emergency, call the emergency number where you are or go to the nearest emergency department. If you do not know the number, ask someone nearby, a hospital, police station, pharmacy, hotel, neighbour, or trusted person, to help you contact emergency services. Do not delay urgent help while searching for the perfect service.

Common examples include 911 in the United States and most of Canada, 112 throughout the European Union, 999 or 112 in the United Kingdom, and 000 in Australia. These are examples only; verify the number where you live.

Tell a trusted person. Do not remain alone. If it is safe, create distance between yourself and anything you could use to harm yourself.

## Suicide and crisis support

Most countries have a crisis line, staffed by trained people, for anyone who is thinking about suicide or in acute emotional distress. Many are free and available around the clock; some also offer text or chat.

To find yours: search "suicide crisis line [your country]" or "mental health helpline [your country]." Two international directories list crisis services by country: Find A Helpline (findahelpline.com) and Befrienders Worldwide (befrienders.org). The International Association for Suicide Prevention also directs people to Find A Helpline. Verify that the service is current before relying on it.

A crisis line is for acute distress. If your thoughts are persistent but you are not in immediate danger, contact a doctor or mental-health service for ongoing care, as Lesson 27 describes.

## Domestic abuse and sexual violence

Specialist services exist in most countries for people experiencing domestic abuse, coercive control, or sexual violence. They offer confidential advice, safety planning, advocacy, and, in many places, refuge.

Specialist services generally aim to support your safety and choices rather than pressure you, although confidentiality and reporting requirements vary by country and situation.

To find one: search "domestic abuse helpline [your country]" or "sexual violence support [your country]." Public libraries, doctors' surgeries, and women's centres often have information you can take discreetly. If you believe your device is monitored, use a safer device if possible: a trusted person's phone or a device at a library, clinic, workplace, or support service. Do not make sudden changes to browsing history or device settings if doing so might alert the person harming you. A specialist abuse service can help you plan safer communication.

If there is immediate danger, contact emergency services where it is safe to do so.

## Addiction and substance dependence

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, sedatives, or another substance, do not stop suddenly on your own; withdrawal can be medically dangerous. Contact a doctor first. Then, for ongoing support, search "addiction service [your country]" or "drug and alcohol helpline [your country]." Recovery communities such as twelve-step fellowships exist in most countries and can be found online by name. For behavioural addictions, a counsellor with relevant experience and an accountable community are the usual starting points.

## Counselling and mental-health care

How care is accessed varies. In some countries you begin with a family doctor who refers you; in others you can contact a psychologist, therapist, or community mental-health service directly. To find out, search "how to access mental health care [your country]," ask your doctor, or ask a pharmacist.

To find a counsellor or therapist: most countries have a professional register you can search, and searching "registered counsellor [your city]" or "accredited therapist [your country]" will usually find it. Christian counselling associations exist in many countries and can help you find someone who respects your faith. Lesson 27 lists the questions to ask before you begin.

If cost is a barrier, ask about public health provision, charitable counselling services, sliding-scale fees, employee assistance programmes, church-funded schemes, and university training clinics.

## Pastoral support

If you would like pastoral and spiritual guidance outside your own church, Faithful Path Community offers a paid online service called Talk to a Pastor. It includes a private video conversation with Pastor Richmond Kobe and written follow-up. Current fees and booking details are available at faithfulpathcommunity.com. This is pastoral care, not psychotherapy, medical care, or crisis support, and it does not replace the professional services listed above.

## Safeguarding and legal help

If something in this course has raised a concern about the safety of a child or vulnerable adult, or about serious misconduct, contact your church's safeguarding lead, your denomination's safeguarding officer, or the relevant statutory authority in your country. If you need legal advice, search "free legal advice [your country]"; many countries have legal aid or citizens' advice services.

## A last word

Asking for help is not a failure of faith. The people who staff these services are, whether they know it or not, part of how God cares for the people he made. Use them.
