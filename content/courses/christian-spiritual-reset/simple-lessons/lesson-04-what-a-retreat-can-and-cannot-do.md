## One truth to remember

> **A retreat is a good gift, but it was never meant to carry everything.**

## Key Scripture

> “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ.”
>
> — Galatians 6:2 (ESV)

Paul expects Christians to help carry one another’s burdens. Needing help is not unusual or shameful. It is part of ordinary Christian life.

## A retreat cannot…

* replace a doctor, counsellor, treatment programme or crisis service;
* resolve in a few days everything that developed over months or years;
* guarantee that you will feel God’s presence;
* replace the support of other people; or
* make a major decision for you.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About five to six minutes. Read calmly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the transcript” section.)*

A retreat can be a good gift.

But good things can disappoint us, or even cause harm, when we ask them to do more than they were meant to do.

Before you prepare for your retreat, let us set honest expectations.

First, here is what a retreat can do.

It can interrupt your normal pattern. When you step outside your usual routine, you may notice habits and pressures that have become difficult to see.

A retreat can give you unhurried time to rest, pray and become honest with God.

It can help you name what you are carrying. A general feeling of heaviness may become clearer words: grief, fear, anger, disappointment or exhaustion.

A retreat can also begin something good. It may lead you to seek help, have a conversation you have avoided, change an unhealthy pattern or begin a simple rhythm of prayer and rest.

I keep saying that a retreat can do these things. I use that word deliberately.

God is not absent until your retreat begins. Following the right programme cannot create or control a particular experience of God.

Now let us consider five things a retreat cannot do.

First, a retreat cannot replace professional care.

It cannot take the place of a doctor, counsellor, treatment programme, safeguarding service or crisis service. If you are already receiving care, follow the guidance of the professionals who know your situation.

The Safety and Support page explains when to seek help before retreating alone. You can return to that page at any time.

Second, a retreat cannot resolve in a few days everything that developed over months or years.

A few hours or days with God may become an important beginning. You may receive clarity, recognise a pattern or decide to ask for help.

But healing and lasting change often take time. Do not place pressure on yourself to solve everything during one retreat.

Third, a retreat cannot guarantee that you will feel God’s presence.

You may experience peace, tears, conviction or a new sense of direction. You may also experience quietness, distraction or very little emotion.

Some biblical prayers describe a person continuing to call upon God while feeling surrounded by darkness.

A quiet retreat is not necessarily a failed retreat. Your feelings are real, but they are not the only measure of God’s presence or faithfulness.

Fourth, a retreat cannot replace the support of other people.

In today’s Key Scripture, Paul says, “Bear one another’s burdens.” He assumes that Christians will sometimes need help carrying what is heavy.

Your retreat may include private time with God. It may also be taken with appropriate support nearby or as part of a carefully planned retreat with others.

Time with God is not a reason to cut yourself off from safe, wise people.

Fifth, a retreat cannot make a major decision for you.

During the retreat, you may experience strong emotions, ideas or impressions. Write them down, but hold them carefully. A strong feeling is not automatically clear direction from God.

Later in this course, you will learn how to test what you believe you may have heard.

For now, follow one simple rule: do not make a major non-urgent decision during your retreat.

This includes decisions about your marriage, church, employment, finances or where you will live. Give yourself time to return home, rest and seek wise counsel before acting.

Safety is different. If you or another person is in immediate danger, do not delay action because of this rule. Move towards safety and contact the appropriate emergency, safeguarding or professional service.

Treat an impression as a warning—not trustworthy guidance—if it contradicts Scripture, encourages harm, places you above other people, separates you from safe and wise counsel, or pressures you to act immediately without a genuine safety reason.

Do not act on an impression like that. Write it down only if doing so is safe, and discuss it with a mature, trustworthy person or qualified professional.

These boundaries are not meant to discourage you. They are meant to protect you from expecting a retreat to carry something it was never designed to carry.

A retreat can give you space to rest, pray, listen and tell the truth.

It does not have to solve your whole life.

Now consider one question.

What are you hoping this retreat will do for you? <#5#>

Compare your hope with the five boundaries in this lesson. You do not need to remove all hope or expectation. Simply hold it honestly and gently.

Then take one practical step.

Save the name of one safe support person and the emergency number for the place where you will take your retreat.

Keep those details somewhere you can reach quickly, such as a card in your bag or an emergency note on your phone.

If you do not have a safe support person, save the details of a suitable pastor, counsellor, doctor or support service instead.

The course will not ask you to enter those details. They remain with you.

When you have saved them, mark this lesson complete.
