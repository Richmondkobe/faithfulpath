# Session 6 — Forgiving Others and Yourself

*This session is optional. It is not part of the three-day, one-day or three-hour retreat. It has one version.*

---

**Optional session · After your retreat, in the at-home format, or with support**

### Before you begin

**Time needed:** about 60–90 minutes.

**Have nearby:** your Bible, your journal, a pen, one or two cards or small pieces of paper, an envelope and a drink.

**Please read this before you start.**

Forgiveness does not mean denying harm, removing safety boundaries, cancelling justice or restoring unsafe contact.

This session is best done with a pastor, counsellor or trauma-informed professional, especially if the harm was abuse or if thinking about the person brings panic, flashbacks or a feeling of being unreal.

You are never required to forgive on a timetable. You are never required to contact or return to someone who harmed you.

**Consider this session only if:** you are rested and settled, the harm is not still happening, and you want to explore what forgiveness might mean without forcing an outcome.

**Choose to wait for any reason that makes today feel wrong.** You do not need to prove that your reason is good enough.

**If someone checks your belongings or writing,** do not write anything that could put you at risk. You may pray without writing.

**[ Not today — I will wait ]**

*Waiting is a wise choice, not a failure. You may return whenever you choose.*

### Listen

*This parable contains a serious warning. It has sometimes been used to pressure wounded people into immediate forgiveness or unsafe reconciliation. That is not how we use it here. It does not tell you to deny harm, abandon justice or restore contact.*

**▶ Listen — about 7–8 minutes**

**[ Read the transcript ]**

### Pray

**▶ Guided prayer — about 2 minutes**

Use only the lines that fit you. You may leave any blank empty.

### Be still

**Suggested: 10 minutes.** This is deliberately short. Choose less if you need to.

**[ Timer: 5 · 10 · 15 · 20 · 30 minutes ]**

Prayer phrase: **“Into your hands I place this wound.”** Or: **“God sees and God cares.”**

If the silence brings the person too close, or you begin to panic or relive a memory, stop. Sit down, open your eyes, and name where you are and what you can see. This is not failure. It means this session needs support, and it can wait.

### Respond

**Five words that are often confused**

* **Truth:** naming the wrong without making it smaller.
* **Safety:** ending exposure to harm that is still happening.
* **Justice:** reporting, consequences, repayment and protection.
* **Release:** choosing not to pursue personal revenge and placing final judgement in God's hands, even when angry feelings remain.
* **Reconciliation:** a restored relationship. It requires repentance, real change, safety and rebuilt trust. You are never required to restore contact or give someone renewed access to your life. In some situations, reconciliation will not be wise or possible.

*You can begin release before God without the other person's participation. Reconciliation always requires more than one person.*

**Do: place the wound in God's care**

Write this, or pray it without writing. It can be the whole exercise:

> **“God, I place this wound in your care and ask for help with the next faithful step.”**

This is not a lesser prayer. It is a complete one. The next faithful step may be a counsellor, a safety plan, a report, a boundary or simply rest.

**If you are ready and safe, you may also write** *(optional)*:

> “Today, as far as I am able, I choose not to pursue personal revenge. I place final judgement in God's hands. My anger or pain may remain, and I may need to make this choice again. This does not remove safety, truth, reporting or accountability. I ask for help with the next faithful step.”

Use an initial or no details. Do not send it or read it to the person. If you are ready, read it to God, aloud or silently. Then seal it in an envelope and write on the front: **Placed in God's care** and today's date. The envelope is only a reminder of your prayer. Nothing is destroyed.

**If you have been punishing yourself** for something God has already forgiven, you may add a second card: *“God has forgiven ______. Help me stop paying for it with suffering, and show me any repair that is still mine.”*

Receiving God's forgiveness does not mean avoiding consequences, making contact that would be unsafe, or attempting repair without guidance. Some forms of repair should be planned with a qualified person.

**Important:** If the person who harmed you may still be a danger to you or to others, such as children, vulnerable adults or people in their care, forgiveness never replaces safeguarding or reporting. If anyone is in immediate danger, use **Get Help Now**. Otherwise, contact the appropriate specialist or safeguarding service.

### Close

Read these words slowly. Change any line you cannot say honestly.

> I do not have to deny the wrong or give up on justice.
> I choose not to pursue personal revenge, or I ask God to help me make that choice.
> I will keep the boundaries, truth and accountability that are still needed.
> I place what I cannot control into God's care.
> God's mercy never requires me to stay in danger.

☐ I have finished this session.

### What comes next

**Take a break and be gentle with yourself.** Eat, drink, rest or go outside.

If you needed **Get Help Now**, attend to that first, with appropriate support.

If today brought the person too close, contact your support person. You do not need to name the person or share details.

**Continue: return to your course**

## Audio script and transcript

*(About seven to eight minutes including the Bible reading. Read calmly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the transcript” section.)*

Welcome to this optional session.

Before we begin, please hear this clearly. Forgiveness does not mean saying the harm did not matter. It does not mean removing safety or boundaries. It does not mean giving up on justice. And it never means returning to someone who could hurt you.

You are in charge of today. You may stop at any time, and you may choose to wait.

Our Bible passage is Matthew chapter 18, verses 21 to 35, from the English Standard Version.

This parable contains a serious warning. It has sometimes been used to pressure wounded people into immediate forgiveness or unsafe reconciliation. That is not how we will use it here. It does not tell you to deny harm, abandon justice or restore contact. If listening feels unsafe today, you may stop and choose “Not today — I will wait.”

As you listen, notice both the mercy and the warning.

Then Peter came up and said to him, “Lord, how often will my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? As many as seven times?” Jesus said to him, “I do not say to you seven times, but seventy-seven times.
“Therefore the kingdom of heaven may be compared to a king who wished to settle accounts with his servants. When he began to settle, one was brought to him who owed him ten thousand talents. And since he could not pay, his master ordered him to be sold, with his wife and children and all that he had, and payment to be made. So the servant fell on his knees, imploring him, ‘Have patience with me, and I will pay you everything.’ And out of pity for him, the master of that servant released him and forgave him the debt. But when that same servant went out, he found one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred denarii, and seizing him, he began to choke him, saying, ‘Pay what you owe.’ So his fellow servant fell down and pleaded with him, ‘Have patience with me, and I will pay you.’ He refused and went and put him in prison until he should pay the debt. When his fellow servants saw what had taken place, they were greatly distressed, and they went and reported to their master all that had taken place. Then his master summoned him and said to him, ‘You wicked servant! I forgave you all that debt because you pleaded with me. And should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?’ And in anger his master delivered him to the jailers, until he should pay all his debt. So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your heart.”

<#2#>

Peter asked Jesus how many times he should forgive. He suggested seven times, which probably sounded generous. Jesus answered with a much bigger number. He was refusing to turn forgiveness into a scorecard.

Then Jesus told a story. A servant owed a king an enormous debt, far more than he could ever repay. The king forgave all of it. But then that servant refused to forgive a much smaller debt owed to him.

The story holds together great mercy and a serious warning. Jesus does challenge us not to hold on to hatred after receiving so much mercy ourselves. But this warning must never be used to pressure hurt people into saying “I forgive you” on demand. And we do not earn God's grace by forgiving perfectly. The mercy we have received is where forgiveness begins.

Notice also what comes just before this story. Jesus described how to deal honestly with someone who has done wrong, with truth-telling and accountability. So forgiveness in the Bible is not pretending nothing happened.

But those steps are not a plan for dealing with abuse or crime. If harm like that has happened, do not begin by confronting the person privately. Seek specialist or safeguarding help.

One reason forgiveness feels impossible is that we use one word for five different things. Truth means naming the wrong honestly. Safety means ending harm that is still happening. Justice means reporting, consequences and protection. Release means choosing not to pursue personal revenge. It does not require you to stop feeling angry immediately. And reconciliation means a restored relationship.

You can begin release before God without the other person's participation. But reconciliation always requires more than one person. It requires repentance, real change, safety and rebuilt trust. You are never required to restore contact or give someone renewed access to your life. In some situations, reconciliation will not be wise or possible.

So let me say what release is not.

It is not forgetting. Joseph remembered exactly what his brothers had done to him. He said, “You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good.” He remembered, and still chose not to take revenge.

It is not trust. Joseph did not immediately give his brothers renewed access. He observed and tested whether they had changed before the relationship was restored.

It is not giving up safety or justice. You can let go of revenge and still report what happened, keep someone away from your children, or never work with them again. Paul wrote, “Never avenge yourselves.” But that does not stop the proper authorities from doing their job.

It is not a feeling, and it is often not one single moment. It may take time, prayer, grief and support. If the pain returns, it does not mean you failed.

And it does not need to be spoken to the person. Often it should not be.

What about forgiving yourself? You are not the judge in your own case. What you can do is receive God's verdict: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” If you have been punishing yourself, trying to pay for your guilt with suffering, you may ask God to help you stop, while still making any repair that is yours to make.

Finally, you may be thinking of someone who has died, or who will never admit what they did. Release does not need them. It happens between you and God.

In this session, there is one simple prayer you can pray: “God, I place this wound in your care and ask for help with the next faithful step.” That prayer is complete.

Take a slow breath. <#3#> God sees, and God cares.
