# Lesson 23 — Know When to Ask for Help

*Part 4 — Bring It Home · Lesson 23*

**What you will do here:** Notice signs that you may need more than rest, and know who to contact for what.

## If you need help right now

**If you are in immediate danger, think you may act on thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or cannot keep yourself safe,** call the emergency number where you are or go to the nearest emergency department. Tell a safe person and ask them to stay with you or help you reach urgent care.

**If you are having thoughts of suicide or not wanting to be alive but are not in immediate danger,** contact a crisis line or urgent mental-health service now and tell a safe person. The rest of this course can wait.

## One truth to remember

> **Asking for the right help is not a failure of faith. It is wisdom.**

## Key Scripture

> “Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed.”
>
> — Proverbs 15:22 (ESV)

Proverbs treats seeking wise help as normal and good. No one is meant to carry everything alone.

## Signs you may need more help

* Low mood, emptiness or loss of interest on most days for **two weeks or more**, or getting worse.
* Grief that seriously disrupts sleep, eating or daily life.
* Something surfaced that frightens you, or affects your safety or daily life.
* Anxiety, panic, compulsions or low mood keep knocking over your new rhythm.
* You have been resting, taking small steps or seeking support for several weeks, and you are not getting better.

*Do not wait two weeks if things are severe, getting worse quickly, stopping you from managing daily life, or include thoughts of suicide or self-harm. Seek help now.*

## Who to contact for what

| If you need help with… | Contact… |
|---|---|
| Faith questions, spiritual dryness, church hurt, a decision | A safe pastor or spiritual director |
| Grief, relationship difficulties, life changes, moderate anxiety or low mood | A qualified counsellor or therapist |
| Persistent low mood, panic, trauma symptoms, sleep or appetite changes, trouble managing daily life | A doctor or qualified mental-health professional |
| Thoughts of suicide or not wanting to be alive | A crisis line or urgent mental-health service now; emergency services if you may act or cannot stay safe |
| Abuse or coercive control, past or present | A specialist abuse service, contacted from a safe device; emergency services if in danger |
| Alcohol or drug dependence | A doctor and an addiction service. **Do not stop suddenly on your own:** stopping alcohol suddenly when physically dependent can cause dangerous withdrawal and needs professional guidance. |
| Repeated confessing, checking or religious anxiety | A clinician experienced in OCD |
| Serious misconduct or safeguarding concerns | The appropriate safeguarding, legal or professional authority |

*Many difficulties need more than one kind of help. A pastor can be one part of your support, alongside professional care.*

## Open the workbook *(optional)*

**Choosing a counsellor:** check their registration or qualifications, ask about their training and approach, and ask how they respect your faith. Ask about confidentiality and its limits before you begin. If you do not feel respected and heard after a few sessions, you may change. If cost is a barrier, ask about lower fees, charities and public services.

**For pastors and church leaders:** look for help outside your own church, such as your denomination's support, a supervisor, a counsellor with clergy experience or a pastor in another town.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About five to six minutes. Read calmly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the transcript” section.)*

Before we begin, please hear this. If you are in immediate danger, think you may act on thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or cannot keep yourself safe, call the emergency number where you are or go to the nearest emergency department. Tell a safe person and ask them to stay with you or help you reach urgent care.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or not wanting to be alive but are not in immediate danger, contact a crisis line or urgent mental-health service now and tell a safe person. The rest of this course can wait.

Earlier in the course, we said that a retreat cannot replace medical or mental-health care. This lesson says it again, for a different reason.

Before your retreat, the question was whether it was safe to go. Now, the question is what your retreat or reset has shown you.

Quiet time can reveal what busy months had covered. Some people come home realising, perhaps for the first time, that what they are carrying needs more than rest. If that is you, this lesson is not a verdict. It is a map.

Here are some signs that you may need more help.

The low mood has not lifted. If you have felt low, empty or uninterested in life on most days for two weeks or more, or it is getting worse, please speak with a doctor or qualified mental-health professional.

Grief is seriously disrupting your sleep, your eating or your daily life.

Something came up during your retreat that frightens you, or affects your safety.

Anxiety, panic, compulsions or low mood keep knocking over the rhythm you chose.

Or simply this: you have been resting, taking small steps or seeking support for several weeks, and you are not getting better. That is a reason to seek more help, not a reason to blame yourself or simply try harder.

And please do not wait two weeks if things are severe, getting worse quickly, stopping you from managing daily life, or include thoughts of suicide or self-harm. Seek help now.

Many Christians go first to a pastor, because that is the person they know. Pastors are important. But they are not the right first call for everything. On this page, there is a simple table showing who to contact for what. Faith questions may go to a safe pastor. Grief or relationship struggles may go to a counsellor. Persistent low mood, panic or trauma symptoms need a doctor or mental-health professional. And thoughts of suicide need a crisis service straight away.

Most serious difficulties need more than one kind of help. Someone receiving treatment for depression still needs prayer, Scripture and a church that knows them. The mistake is not going to a pastor. It is going only to a pastor for something a pastor is not trained to treat.

Some Christians worry that seeking professional help means their faith is not enough. But receiving healthcare is not a rejection of prayer or trust in God. Prayer, pastoral care, medicine and therapy can work alongside one another.

Some worry about medication. Medication is a medical treatment, not a verdict on your faith. Talk with a qualified prescriber, and please do not start, stop or change medication because of something you experienced on retreat.

And some are afraid that people will find out. Professional care is usually confidential, although there are some legal limits. You can ask about those before you begin.

Proverbs says, “Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed.” Asking for the right help is not a failure of faith. It is wisdom.

Now, a question to think about. Which do you find hardest to believe: that you deserve help, that help exists, or that asking for help is not a failure? <#5#>

And here is your step. Save two contacts: one for pastoral help, and one for professional help, such as a doctor, counsellor or service. If one of the signs already fits you, make the first call, or take the first step towards it, this week.
