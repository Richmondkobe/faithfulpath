## One truth to remember

> **Silence is not emptying your mind. It is gently returning your attention to God.**

## Key Scripture

> “For God alone my soul waits in silence; from him comes my salvation.”
>
> — Psalm 62:1 (ESV)

The psalmist is not silent because he has nothing to say. He chooses to wait for God and trust him.

## Three ways to be quiet

* **Still silence:** Sit quietly with a Bible verse or short prayer.
* **Moving silence:** Walk slowly or move gently without listening to anything.
* **Supported silence:** Keep your eyes open, use gentle background sound or have a trusted person nearby.

*None of these is more spiritual than the others. Choose the one that helps you feel safe and pay attention to God.*

You may carry your phone for safety during moving silence. Simply keep it out of your hand unless you need it.

## Think

**When you imagine being quiet, what do you feel?**

You might feel relief, restlessness, unease, fear or something else.

*[ Optional: write your answer ]*

## If silence becomes distressing

Mild boredom or restlessness is normal. You can return gently to your Bible verse or prayer phrase.

But if you begin to panic, relive a painful memory, feel unreal or become overwhelmed:

1. Stop the silence.
2. Open your eyes and stand up if you can.
3. Name where you are and what you can see.
4. Contact your support person if you need help.

**If you hear a voice as though someone is speaking when no one is there,** especially if it is repeated, commanding, frightening, confusing or affecting your daily life, do not treat it as guidance to obey. Stop the silence and speak with a qualified medical or mental-health professional. If a voice tells you to harm yourself or someone else, or you feel unable to stay safe, use **Get Help Now** and do not stay alone.

## Audio script and transcript

*(About five minutes. Read calmly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the transcript” section.)*

Most of your retreat sessions include a time of silence. Some periods are short. Others are longer.

For some people, silence sounds peaceful. For others, it brings restlessness, unease or even fear.

If silence feels difficult for you, please hear this first: you are not failing. Being uncomfortable with silence is common. It is not a sign of weak faith.

When the noise around us stops, we may begin to notice what is happening inside us. Thoughts, feelings and memories that were hidden by busyness can come to the surface.

Some people fear becoming bored. Some fear their own thoughts. Others worry that they will become quiet and feel nothing from God.

For people who have been through trauma, stillness can sometimes make the body feel alarmed. That response is not a spiritual failure. You do not need to force yourself to remain still when your body is telling you that you feel unsafe.

Here is the most important thing to understand: silence in this retreat is not an attempt to empty your mind.

You are not trying to become blank or stop every thought. You are choosing to give your attention to God, who is already with you.

During the silence, you will usually have something simple to hold: a Bible verse, a short prayer or a question.

Thoughts will come. That is normal.

You do not need to fight them or become angry with yourself. Notice that your attention has wandered, and then gently return to your verse or prayer.

You may need to return many times. That does not mean the silence has failed. Returning is part of the practice.

Silence is not a battle against your mind. It is a gentle, repeated return to God.

There are three ways to be quiet. None is more spiritual than the others.

The first is still silence. You sit quietly with a Bible verse or short prayer. You may close your eyes or keep them open.

The second is moving silence. You walk slowly or move gently without listening to music or another recording. If you need to carry your phone for safety, keep it with you. The aim is simply to stop checking it or holding it at the centre of your attention.

The third is supported silence. You may keep your eyes open, use gentle background sound or have a trusted person nearby. Supported silence may be helpful if complete stillness makes you anxious.

Choose the kind of quiet that helps you feel safe and pay attention to God. You do not have to choose the most difficult option.

You also do not need to begin with thirty minutes.

If silence is new or difficult for you, begin with two minutes. You could choose five minutes if that feels manageable. Ten attentive minutes can also be a complete practice.

If a retreat session suggests a longer silence and you can manage only a shorter time, choose the shorter time. That is enough.

It can help to choose one short prayer phrase.

You might use the words from Psalm 62: “For God alone my soul waits in silence.”

You could pray, “Here I am.”

Or you could use the words, “Be still, and know that I am God.”

When your attention wanders, return gently to your phrase.

Now, let us talk about the difference between discomfort and distress.

Mild boredom or restlessness is normal. You might want to check the time, move your body or think about what you will do later. You can notice that feeling and gently return to your prayer.

But distress is different.

If you begin to panic, feel that you are reliving a painful memory, feel unreal or become overwhelmed, stop the silence.

Open your eyes. Stand up if you can. Move your body. Name where you are, what day it is and what you can see around you. Contact your support person if you need help.

You do not have to continue the exercise. Stopping when you become distressed is a wise response, not a spiritual failure.

If you experience new or severe chest pain, serious difficulty breathing, fainting or another medical emergency, contact the emergency service where you are.

There is one more important safety point.

If you hear a voice as though someone is speaking when no one is there, especially if it is repeated, commanding, frightening, confusing or affecting your daily life, do not treat it as guidance to obey.

Stop the silence and speak with a qualified medical or mental-health professional.

If a voice tells you to harm yourself or someone else, or you feel unable to stay safe, get help straight away and do not stay alone.

Finally, you may be afraid that you will sit in silence and sense nothing from God.

That may happen.

Some retreats include a strong feeling of God’s presence. Others are quiet and ordinary. You may not receive a clear answer or feel anything dramatic.

Your faith does not rest on producing a particular feeling. It rests on who God is.

Waiting quietly before God can still be prayer, even when it feels ordinary.

Now consider this question: When you imagine being quiet, what do you feel? Relief, restlessness, unease, fear or something else? <#5#>

Then choose your first silence: two, five or ten minutes.

Choose one short prayer phrase to bring your attention back to God. If you would like, you can use the timer on this page and try it now.

Begin gently. You have nothing to prove.
