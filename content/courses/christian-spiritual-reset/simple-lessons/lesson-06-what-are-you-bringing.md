## Audio script and transcript

*(About five minutes. Read calmly and naturally. On the course page, place this inside the closed “Read the transcript” section.)*

You have chosen your retreat plan, place and date.

Now there is one more important question.

What are you bringing to God?

In Mark chapter 10, a man named Bartimaeus was sitting beside the road outside Jericho. Bartimaeus was blind.

When he heard that Jesus was passing by, he began to call out for mercy. People told him to be quiet, but he continued calling.

Jesus stopped and asked for Bartimaeus to be brought to him.

Then Jesus asked a question: “What do you want me to do for you?”

The people around Bartimaeus may have thought the answer was obvious. Jesus did not assume the answer or speak for him. He invited Bartimaeus to name what he wanted.

Bartimaeus answered plainly: “Rabbi, let me recover my sight.”

There can be something important about putting our need into words.

God already knows what we are carrying. We are not giving him information he lacks. But speaking honestly in his presence can help us recognise what is happening inside us.

In this lesson, you will write one honest sentence about what you are bringing to God.

There are two common mistakes to avoid.

The first mistake is bringing a long list.

You might think, “I need to find my calling, forgive my father, decide about my job, repair my prayer life and stop feeling anxious.”

Each concern may be real. But together, they can turn the retreat into another demanding project.

In Psalm 27, David had many needs. Yet he wrote, “One thing have I asked of the LORD.”

David gathered many needs into one central desire.

You can do something similar.

Instead of bringing a long list, try to name the burden underneath it.

Your sentence might be:

“Lord, I have been carrying this alone for a long time.”

Or:

“Lord, I am tired of pretending that I am all right.”

Or:

“Lord, I need help facing what I have avoided.”

The second mistake is writing a sentence that sounds spiritual but is not honest.

You might write, “I want to draw closer to God and be renewed.”

There is nothing wrong with those words if they are true.

But perhaps the more honest sentence is, “I am angry about what happened, and I do not know how to pray.”

A polished sentence can sometimes hide the person who is actually coming to God.

God already knows the honest version.

Hannah prayed with such deep distress that the priest Eli misunderstood what he saw and thought she was drunk. She did not hide her pain from God.

You do not need to hide yours.

Sometimes we also bring a hidden hope into a retreat.

We may hope that God will approve a decision we have already made. We may want him to change another person. We may expect a dramatic spiritual experience.

Having those hopes does not stop you from coming to God. But naming them helps prevent them from quietly controlling what you expect to hear.

Your sentence is a request, not a demand.

In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed honestly about what he desired. He said, “Remove this cup from me.” He then placed his desire before the Father’s will.

Following his example does not mean pretending that harm is good. It does not mean remaining in abuse, accepting danger or giving up wise boundaries.

Jesus’s suffering in Gethsemane was connected to his unique saving mission. His prayer must never be used to pressure someone else to remain in a harmful situation.

Being open to God means allowing him to guide you in ways that agree with Scripture, truth, wisdom and safety.

After writing what you are carrying, you may add, “and I am open to your wise leading.”

Add those words only if they are honest. They are not a formula you must say.

What if you cannot find a sentence?

Some people are too tired, numb or confused to know what they need.

If that is you, this prayer is enough:

“Lord, I do not know what I need. I only know I need to stop and be with you.”

Before we finish, you will see an optional workbook section on this page.

It contains a gentle seven-day psalm plan. You can spend a few minutes with one psalm each day before your retreat.

If you cannot manage it, leave it closed. It is not required.

If self-examination tends to become harsh, fearful or obsessive for you, do not use Psalm 139 to search for hidden sins. Read Psalm 23 again and rest in God’s care.

Now consider one question.

Is there something you are hoping this retreat will give you that you have not said plainly? <#5#>

Then take one small step.

Finish this sentence:

“Lord, what I am carrying is…”

If it feels honest, add:

“…and I am open to your wise leading.”

If you do not know what to write, use the ready prayer on this page.

Keep your sentence somewhere private and safe. You will return to it in Session 1.

When you have written or chosen your sentence, mark this lesson complete.
