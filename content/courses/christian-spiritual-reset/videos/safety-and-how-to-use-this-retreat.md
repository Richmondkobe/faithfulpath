---
title: "Safety, and How to Use This Retreat"
lesson: 05-what-a-retreat-can-and-cannot-do
length: "4–5 min"
---

Before you plan a single day of this retreat, I want to speak with you about safety.

Not because I expect something to go wrong, but because silence can sometimes bring more to the surface than a person expected. I would rather you be prepared than surprised.

Let us begin with what a retreat can do.

It can interrupt a pattern you no longer notice. It can give you unhurried space for honesty before God. It can help you put words to a heaviness you have carried without a name. It can restore perspective. And it can become a place where you meet God or grow more attentive to him.

I say "can" deliberately.

God is not summoned by a schedule, and a retreat is not a transaction.

Now, let us be equally clear about what a retreat cannot do.

It cannot replace medical or psychological care. If you live with depression, an anxiety disorder, an eating disorder, an addiction, or the effects of trauma, a retreat may form one part of your care. It is not the care itself.

Luke, who wrote one of the Gospels, was a physician. Paul encouraged Timothy to use a practical remedy for his stomach. Scripture does not ask you to choose between trusting God and receiving appropriate help.

A retreat also cannot repair in three days everything that took years to form. It cannot guarantee that you will feel God's presence. It cannot replace Christian community. And it cannot make a difficult decision for you.

A strong feeling during a retreat is not automatically an instruction from God. Treating it that way has led sincere people into serious mistakes. Anything significant you sense will be written down and tested after the retreat.

There are also circumstances in which a private retreat should wait or should only take place with appropriate support.

If you are having thoughts of not wanting to be alive, do not rely on this retreat as your response. Tell a trusted person and contact a doctor, mental-health professional, or crisis service.

If you believe you may act on those thoughts or cannot keep yourself safe, contact your local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department now. Ask someone to remain with you.

The retreat will still be here when you are safe.

If someone is harming you—physically, sexually, emotionally, financially, or through coercive control—your first priority is safety. A private retreat is not a safety plan. Contact a specialist service from a safe device.

If the abuse happened in the past, a retreat may still be valuable. But hear this clearly: forgiveness is not the same as reconciliation, restored trust, or returning to danger. It never requires contact with the person who harmed you, and it is not something you must complete on a schedule.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, benzodiazepines or other sedatives, or another substance whose withdrawal may require medical supervision, do not use the retreat to stop suddenly. Speak with a doctor or addiction service first.

If silence, stillness, or remembering the past tends to bring flashbacks, panic, dissociation, or the feeling that you are back inside what happened, plan the retreat with a trauma-informed professional. A shorter or supported format may be safer.

Whatever your circumstances, remember one rule for the days ahead:

If a session brings up more than you can safely hold, stop the session.

Not necessarily the entire retreat. Start by stopping the session.

Open your eyes. Stand up and move. Go outside if it is safe. Drink some water. Look around and name where you are, what day it is, and several things you can see and hear.

Remind yourself: "I am here, in the present."

Pray simply, even if the only prayer you have is, "Lord, help."

Contact your support person. If you are in danger, cannot remain safe, or need urgent medical attention, contact emergency services.

You do not have to complete the exercise that brought the reaction to the surface. These sessions are offered, not assigned. A retreat that is paused has not failed.

Elijah slept and ate twice before God spoke to him.

Pausing can be an act of receiving care.

At the end of this lesson, you will choose one of four paths: proceed independently, proceed with support, use an adapted format, or postpone the retreat and seek help first.

Postponing is not failure. For some people, it is the wisest way to prepare.

You will then complete a short safety check. It is not a test, and it is not scored. Your answers are not monitored in real time, and completing the check does not contact anyone on your behalf.

If you need help, you must make that contact yourself or ask a trusted person to help you do it.

Choose your path honestly.

The retreat is for you.

You are not here to serve the retreat.
