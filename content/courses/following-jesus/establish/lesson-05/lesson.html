Salvation: Grace, Adoption and a New Way of Life

People say I am "saved." Saved from what, and saved for what? Is it only about going to heaven when I die?

  • What "justified" means, and why it is a gift received by faith
  • The four parts: Declared Right, Welcomed as Children, Being Made Holy, Kept for Glory
  • Why good works are the fruit of salvation, not its price
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Saved from what, and for what?

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Establish. In Begin, you learned that everyone who trusts Christ becomes a child of God, and in Lesson 4 we saw what Jesus did: he came, lived, died, rose and reigns. Now we go one step further and ask what his work accomplishes for those who trust him.

Christians often talk about being saved. But if you ask what it means, you may get a short answer: "It means I'll go to heaven when I die." That is part of the truth, but only a small part. The Bible's picture of salvation is much bigger. It reaches back to what God has already done, it shapes our lives now, and it looks forward to a future God will complete.

So here is our question: saved from what, and saved for what? Is it only about going to heaven when I die?

2 · Peace with God

Our passage is Romans chapter 5, verses 1 to 11. Listen to verses 1 and 2: "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ. Through him we have also obtained access by faith into this grace in which we stand, and we rejoice in hope of the glory of God."

Notice how many gifts are in these verses: peace with God, access to his grace, a place to stand, and hope. Paul goes on to speak of endurance in suffering, and in verse 5 he says that "God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us."

3 · God acted first

Now listen to verses 6 to 10. "For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly." Verse 8: "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." And verse 10: "For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life."

Let us use the first question from Lesson 1: what does it say? Which words are repeated? Paul says the same thing three times: while we were still weak, while we were still sinners, while we were enemies. God acted first. He did not wait for us to improve. That is grace.

4 · Justified = dikaioō

Our Greek word today is the one translated "justified" in verse 1: dikaioō. It is an important Bible word, and it is simpler than it sounds. Here in Romans, Paul uses the language of justification: God declares a person right with him. It is the language of a verdict.

This does not mean that a believer suddenly becomes a perfect person. God also changes us, but we will see that as another part of salvation. And in verses 10 and 11, Paul adds another picture: we have been reconciled. People who were enemies of God are now at peace with him.

5 · How Paul gets here

Now the background. Paul wrote this letter to a church in Rome made up of Jewish and Gentile believers. Before chapter 5, he has made a long argument. In chapters 1 to 3 he shows that everyone has sinned, Jew and Gentile alike. No one can be declared right by their own record.

Then in chapter 4 he shows that God declares people right by faith, as he did with Abraham, long before the law was given. That is why chapter 5 begins with "Therefore." Paul is now describing the results: what it means to be declared right with God by faith in Christ.

6 · Abraham believed

Paul's key verse about Abraham comes from Genesis 15. God promised Abraham descendants as many as the stars, and the Bible says, "And he believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness."

Now use our third question from Lesson 1: where does this connect in the Bible's larger story? Paul himself takes us back to Abraham. Long before Jesus came, Abraham believed God's promise, and it was counted to him as righteousness. In Romans 4, Paul uses Abraham to show that being counted righteous comes by faith, not by achievement, for everyone, Jew and Gentile, who trusts in Christ.

7 · The short answer

So, saved from what, and saved for what? Here is the short answer. Salvation means that God rescues us from sin and its results, for a new life with him, now and forever.

Saved from what? From the guilt and power of sin, from separation from God, and from final judgement and death. Saved for what? For peace with God, life in his family, growing freedom from sin, good works, and a place in his renewed creation.

Salvation has a past, a present and a future. God has declared us right with him and welcomed us as his children. He is making us holy. And he will keep us for the glory to come. All of it rests on his grace. We will look at it in four parts: declared right, welcomed as children, being made holy, and kept for glory.

8 · Part 1: Declared Right

The first part: declared right. To be justified means that God, the righteous judge, declares a person right with him. Not because our record makes us right, but because of what Christ has done. Paul writes that we are "justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus." And: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."

Imagine standing before a judge with a charge you cannot answer. You have no record you can present to make yourself right. Yet because of what Christ has done, God forgives your sins and declares you right with him. You do not earn this verdict; you receive it by faith, as a gift. And the believer has peace with God.

9 · Part 2: Welcomed as Children

The second part: welcomed as children. God does not only declare us not guilty and send us away. He brings us home. A judge can set a prisoner free, but a judge does not usually take that prisoner into his own family. God does.

Paul writes that God sent his Son "so that we might receive adoption as sons… So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God." In Begin, Lesson 5, we saw that "sons" here is inheritance language that includes every believer, women and men alike. Believers are not servants trying to earn a place in the house. They are children who already belong, "heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ."

10 · Part 3: Being Made Holy

The third part: being made holy. Salvation is not only something God has done for us. It is also something he is doing in us. Paul writes that we "are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another."

Notice the words "are being transformed." This is a process, and it takes a lifetime. The Bible calls it being made holy, or sanctification: the Holy Spirit gradually making us more like Jesus in our character, desires and actions. We take part in this growth, but it is God's work. Paul puts both sides together: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure."

11 · Fruit, not price

So where do good works fit? Paul writes, "For by grace you have been saved through faith," "not a result of works," and then, "created in Christ Jesus for good works." Good works are the fruit of salvation, not its price. They do not buy our salvation or put God in our debt.

But they matter. James writes, "faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead." Real faith shows itself in a changed life, because the Spirit is at work. And holiness cannot be reduced to a church's extra rules about clothing, food or entertainment, as though keeping those rules makes someone right with God. Becoming holy means becoming more like Jesus: in our character, desires, choices, love for God and love for others.

12 · Part 4: Kept for Glory

The fourth part: kept for glory. Salvation also has a future. Paul looks ahead: "much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God… much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life." Peter describes believers as people "who by God's power are being guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time." God will finish what he began. One day our bodies will be raised and all creation will be made new.

God is faithful and powerful to keep his people, and believers are called to keep trusting Christ. The point here is simple: our hope for the future rests on God's grace and keeping power, not on our ability to build a flawless record. So salvation is much bigger than going to heaven when I die. It means peace with God now, belonging to his family now, being changed now, and a sure hope for the future.

13 · Christians differ

Christians differ on two questions here, and we will name them briefly. First, how justification, faith and good works relate. Across historic Christian traditions, salvation is understood as God's grace in Christ rather than something human beings achieve apart from him. Christians differ, however, in how they explain the relationship between justification, faith and works. Romans says a person is justified by faith, while James says faith without works is dead. This course teaches that good works are the fruit of God's saving grace, not the price we pay to receive it.

Second, how holiness grows. Some churches teach that, alongside continuing growth, God may bring believers into a decisive experience of deeper cleansing and wholehearted devotion. Others emphasise sanctification as gradual growth throughout the Christian life, completed only in glory. Both call God's people to holiness, and both look to God himself to make them holy. There is a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

14 · Lupita's story

Let me tell you about Lupita. Her story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Lupita teaches at a primary school in Guadalajara. She grew up in a church with many rules about clothing, music and behaviour, and she believed she had to keep them perfectly to stay saved. Every time she lost her temper with her class, she wondered whether God had finished with her.

At a Bible study in a new church, the group read Romans 5:1: "Since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God." Lupita asked, "Peace? Even when I fail?" The leader explained the difference between being declared right with God through Christ, and being made holy as God continues his work in us. "Your belonging rests on Christ and his grace, not on a flawless record," she said. "Your growth matters, because grace is changing you. But one failure doesn't mean God has thrown you away."

Lupita still wants to grow in patience with her pupils, and she is learning to bring her failures to God without fear. Many believers confuse being declared right with God with becoming perfect. The gospel separates the two without making either unimportant.

15 · What salvation is not

Here are four common mistakes.

"Salvation is only about going to heaven when I die." It has a past, a present and a future, and it begins now.

"I must earn salvation by good works." We are justified by grace, as a gift, received by faith.

"Since I am saved by grace, how I live doesn't matter." Real faith shows itself in a changed life; good works are the fruit of grace.

And "One failure means God has thrown me away." "Since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God."

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Salvation means God rescues us from sin and its results, for a new life with him, now and forever. All of it rests on grace.

Declared right: God declares believers right with him because of Christ, and we have peace with God. Welcomed as children: God adopts believers into his family as heirs. Being made holy: the Spirit is transforming us over a lifetime, and good works are the fruit, not the price. And kept for glory: God will complete what he began, by his grace.

18 · Prayer

Let us pray. Father, thank you that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. Thank you that in him you have declared me right and welcomed me as your child. Holy Spirit, keep changing me to be more like Jesus, and help me to bring my failures to you without fear. Keep me trusting you until you complete what you have begun. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Holiness is beautiful, but it can be twisted. Some people live in constant fear that any failure means God has rejected them. Others belong to churches where "holiness" is used to control what people wear, whom they speak to or how they spend their money, with shame and threats. Leaders should distinguish biblical commands from local customs and must not present human rules as conditions of salvation. If this describes you, remember that you were declared right with God by grace, not by your performance (Romans 5:1). Repeatedly checking whether you are saved, or seeking reassurance again and again, may be connected to religious anxiety; speak with a trustworthy person outside the situation and, where appropriate, seek qualified support. Optional

Go deeper

How justification and good works relate. Across historic Christian traditions, salvation is understood as God's grace in Jesus Christ rather than something human beings achieve apart from him, and genuine faith produces a changed life. Romans says that a person is "justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28), while James says that "faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead" (James 2:17). Christians differ in how they explain the relationship between justification, faith and works. This course teaches that good works are the fruit of God's saving grace, not the price we pay to receive it.

How holiness grows. Some churches teach that, alongside continuing growth, God may bring believers into a decisive experience of deeper cleansing and wholehearted devotion (they often point to 1 Thessalonians 5:23). Others emphasise sanctification as gradual growth throughout the Christian life, completed only in glory (they often point to Philippians 3:12–14). Both call God's people to holiness and look to God himself to make them holy.

God's keeping and the Bible's warnings. In Begin, Lesson 5, we saw that Christians differ over how God's promises to keep his people relate to the Bible's warnings. This lesson does not reopen that question. Its point is that our hope for the future rests on God's grace and keeping power, not on our ability to build a flawless record.
