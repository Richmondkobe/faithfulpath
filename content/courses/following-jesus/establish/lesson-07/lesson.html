Baptism and the Lord's Supper

Do I need to be baptised? I was baptised as a baby. And what is really happening when we take bread and the cup?

About 18 minutesRomans 6:1–4 with 1 Corinthians 11:23–29My Foundations: My church

In this lesson

You will learn

  • What Romans 6:1–4 shows about baptism, and 1 Corinthians 11:23–29 about the Lord's Supper
  • What "buried with" and "remembrance" mean
  • The four parts: Buried and Raised, Publicly His, Remember and Proclaim, Come and Receive
  • Why the Supper is a meal for forgiven sinners, not a test only the strong can pass
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Two gifts, many questions

Welcome to Lesson 7 of Establish. In Begin, you saw that faith becomes visible through baptism, and that God's people gather together. Now we go one step further and look closely at two simple practices Jesus gave his church: baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Few subjects raise as many questions among new believers. Some wonder whether they need to be baptised at all. Some were baptised as babies and are now being told it didn't count. Some feel nervous when the bread and cup are passed around, unsure whether they are allowed to take them, or afraid of taking them unworthily.

So here is our question: do I need to be baptised? I was baptised as a baby. And what is really happening when we take bread and the cup?

2 · Buried with him

We have two passages today. The first is Romans chapter 6, verses 1 to 4. Paul is answering a question: if God's grace is greater than our sin, should we keep on sinning? Listen: "What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it? Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life."

Paul's answer is a strong no, because believers have been joined to Christ. Paul connects baptism closely with that union. He is certainly not presenting baptism as an empty ritual. Nor is he presenting it as something separated from Christ and faith. He uses baptism to show that the Christian belongs to Christ in his death and resurrection, with a new life and a new direction.

3 · On the night he was betrayed

The second passage is 1 Corinthians chapter 11. Listen to verses 23 to 26: "For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, 'This is my body, which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.' In the same way also he took the cup, after supper, saying, 'This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.' For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes."

Let us use the first question from Lesson 1: what does it say? Which words are repeated? Twice Jesus says, "Do this in remembrance of me." The bread speaks of his body given for us. The cup speaks of the new covenant in his blood. And Paul adds that every time we share it, we proclaim the Lord's death until he comes.

4 · Buried with = synetaphēmen

Our first Greek word is in Romans 6 verse 4: synetaphēmen. It means "we were buried with." The first part of the word means "with." Paul does not only say that Jesus was buried. He says that believers were buried with him.

Paul connects baptism with our union with Christ. He describes believers as buried with Christ so that, just as Christ was raised, they too might walk in newness of life: a life lived with Christ and for him.

5 · Remembrance = anamnēsis

Our second Greek word is anamnēsis, translated "remembrance" when Jesus says, "Do this in remembrance of me." It means remembering, or calling to mind.

But notice the setting in which Jesus gives this command. This is not remembering a random fact, like a date or a phone number. God's people had long remembered his saving acts together in worship. At the Lord's Supper, the church remembers Christ and his saving death with thanksgiving.

6 · The Passover meal

Now the background. Jesus gave this meal at Passover. Each year, Israel kept the Passover to remember how God rescued them from slavery in Egypt. God told them, "This day shall be for you a memorial day." Families ate together, and children asked what the meal meant.

Now use our third question from Lesson 1: where does this connect in the Bible's larger story? Jesus himself makes the connection. He told his disciples, "I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer." And Paul writes, "For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed." As Israel remembered God's rescue from Egypt, the church remembers God's greater rescue through the death of Jesus.

7 · A divided meal in Corinth

There is more background in Corinth. The church there was divided. When they met to share the Lord's Supper, some ate and drank well, while poorer believers went hungry and were shamed. Paul writes, "One goes hungry, another gets drunk… do you despise the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing?"

So when Paul warns about eating "in an unworthy manner," he means first of all what the Corinthians were doing: taking the Supper while despising Christ's people and ignoring what his death means. Then he says, "Let a person examine himself, then, and so eat." Self-examination prepares us to come. It is not meant to keep sincere, struggling believers away.

8 · The short answer

So, what are baptism and the Lord's Supper? Here is the short answer. Jesus gave his church two visible gifts: baptism, which marks entry into the Christian life, and the Lord's Supper, also called Holy Communion or the Eucharist, which the church shares again and again. Both point us to Jesus and what he has done. Neither is a way of earning God's favour.

Some churches call them sacraments; others call them ordinances, meaning practices Jesus commanded. Whatever term a church uses, neither should be treated as an empty ritual. Both direct us to God's grace in Christ. We will look at them in four parts: buried and raised, publicly his, remember and proclaim, and come and receive.

9 · Part 1: Buried and Raised

The first part: buried and raised. Baptism identifies us with Christ in his death and resurrection. The old life is buried, and a new life begins.

The New Testament speaks about baptism alongside forgiveness, new life and being clothed with Christ. Forgiveness: "Repent and be baptized… for the forgiveness of your sins." New life: "we too might walk in newness of life." And being clothed with Christ: "as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ." Jesus himself was baptised at the start of his ministry, though he had no sin to turn from. He walked the path his followers would walk.

10 · Part 2: Publicly His

The second part: publicly his. After his resurrection, Jesus told his followers to make disciples of all nations, "baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." So baptism is not an optional extra. It is part of what Jesus commanded. It is a public act of identification with Christ and belonging among his people.

For those coming to faith in the New Testament, baptism normally followed their confession of Christ, usually soon after. When the Ethiopian official believed, he saw water by the road and asked, "See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" Baptism is a step of obedience and belonging, not a reward for spiritual maturity. You do not need to have everything sorted out first. But it should be taken freely, with understanding, and never under pressure.

11 · Part 3: Remember and Proclaim

The third part: remember and proclaim. Paul writes, "For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes."

Notice that the Lord's Supper looks in three directions. It looks back to the cross, where Jesus gave himself for us. It looks around at the family of God, who share it together. And it looks forward to Jesus' return, until he comes. Each time we share it, we are reminded of the gospel, and we tell it again, without saying a word.

12 · Part 4: Come and Receive

The fourth part: come and receive. The Lord's Supper is a meal for forgiven sinners, not for people who think they are good enough. No one comes because they are worthy in themselves. We come because Christ died for us. It is also a meal that joins us to one another: "Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body."

So what does it mean to come in a worthy manner? It does not mean making ourselves worthy. We come trusting Christ, honestly bringing our sin to God, and seeking to love his people. If you are aware of sin, bring it honestly to God, and come trusting the forgiveness Christ has secured. If there is a broken relationship you can safely put right, seek to do so. The Supper is God's gift to strengthen weak believers, not a test only the strong can pass.

13 · Christians differ

Christians differ on two questions here, and we will name them briefly. First, baptism. Many churches baptise only those who personally confess faith, usually by immersion, which pictures burial and resurrection. Other churches also baptise the children of believers. Some understand baptism as a means through which God gives new birth and forgiveness; others understand it as a public sign and confession that follows new birth. These views shape whether someone baptised as a child should be baptised as a believer.

Second, the Lord's Supper. Christians understand Jesus' words "This is my body" in different ways. Some emphasise that Christ is truly present and gives himself to his people through the Supper. Others emphasise the bread and cup as signs through which believers remember and proclaim his saving death. Christians also explain Christ's presence in different ways. This course teaches that both baptism and the Supper are commanded by Jesus, direct us to God's grace in Christ, and are never ways of earning salvation. Ask a trustworthy church leader how your church practises them, and compare what you hear with Scripture. There is a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

14 · Selam's story

Let me tell you about Selam. Her story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Selam is a nurse in Addis Ababa. She was baptised as a baby in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, where her family still worships faithfully. Two years ago, she began reading the Gospels with friends from work, and now she attends a small evangelical church.

One Sunday the pastor announced a baptism service, and a woman beside her whispered, "You must be baptised properly now. Your first baptism didn't count." Selam went home troubled. She did not want to dishonour her parents, and she did not want to disobey Jesus. She asked to meet the pastor. He listened first, and then he opened Romans 6 and Matthew 28 with her. "Our church baptises people when they confess faith for themselves," he said. "Other faithful Christians see your first baptism as complete. I won't rush you. Let's study this together, pray, and talk about how to honour your family whatever you decide."

Selam has not yet decided. She is reading and praying, and she plans to talk with her mother before any step. Meanwhile, she is learning to remember the cross with new gratitude. Her experience is common among people who come to faith from one Christian tradition and settle in another. It needs patient, respectful guidance, not pressure.

15 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are four common mistakes.

"Baptism or the Lord's Supper earns salvation." They point us to Jesus and his grace; they are not ways of earning God's favour.

"They are empty rituals that don't matter." Jesus commanded both, and they are rich with meaning.

"Only perfect people may take the bread and cup." The Supper is a meal for forgiven sinners.

And "1 Corinthians 11 means I should stay away if I struggle." Paul calls us to examine ourselves, and so eat.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Jesus gave his church two visible gifts that point to him.

Buried and raised: baptism identifies us with Christ in his death and resurrection. Publicly his: baptism is a public act of belonging to Jesus, taken freely and without pressure. Remember and proclaim: the Lord's Supper looks back to the cross, around to God's family, and forward to his return. And come and receive: the Supper is a meal for forgiven sinners; we examine ourselves, and so eat.

In your name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Baptism and the Lord's Supper point us to God's grace in Christ, but they can be misused. Warning signs include being pushed to be baptised before you understand or are ready; being told that you are not saved, or that a family member who has died is lost, only because of how or when someone was baptised; being refused the Lord's Supper as a punishment for asking questions or disagreeing with a leader; or being made so afraid of 1 Corinthians 11:29 that you stay away for years. Church discipline should be rare, careful, fair and aimed at restoration, never a tool for control. If this is happening, speak with a trustworthy person outside the situation, and see the Support Page "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority." In some families and countries, baptism brings serious risk, such as threats, loss of home or income, or legal danger. Your safety matters to God: seek wise advice and make a safety plan before a public step. If you are in recovery from alcohol, have a health condition or allergy, or find immersion difficult or unsafe, you may ask a trustworthy church leader privately about grape juice, gluten-free bread or another appropriate way to take part or be baptised.

Optional

Go deeper

Who is baptised, and how. Many churches baptise only those who personally confess faith, usually by immersion, which pictures burial and resurrection (they often point to Acts 2:41; 8:36–38; Romans 6:4). Other churches also baptise the children of believers (they often point to whole households being baptised in Acts 16:15, 33; to the promise "for you and for your children" in Acts 2:39; and to Colossians 2:11–12). Churches also differ over immersion, pouring or sprinkling.

What baptism does, and baptism again. Some churches understand baptism as a means through which God gives new birth and forgiveness (they often point to Acts 2:38 and 1 Peter 3:21, and understand the "washing of regeneration" in Titus 3:5 in connection with baptism). Others understand it as a public sign and confession that follows new birth (they often point to Acts 10:44–48, where the Spirit was given before baptism). These views shape whether someone baptised as a child should be baptised as a believer. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that baptism is commanded by Jesus and that salvation rests on God's grace in Christ.

Baptism "in Jesus' name". Acts speaks of baptism "in the name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 2:38; 10:48; 19:5). Most churches understand this as baptism by Jesus' authority and into belonging to him, and they baptise with the words Jesus gave in Matthew 28:19. Some churches baptise using only the name of Jesus. Because Lesson 2 treats the Bible's teaching about the Father, the Son and the Spirit as central, this course follows Matthew 28:19, while welcoming people from those churches warmly and inviting them to examine the Scriptures together.

How Christ is present in the Lord's Supper. Christians receive Jesus' command, "Do this in remembrance of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24–25), but understand his words "This is my body" (Matthew 26:26) in different ways. Some emphasise that Christ is truly present and gives himself to his people through the Supper, though they explain that presence in different ways. Others emphasise the bread and cup as signs through which believers remember and proclaim Christ's saving death; many of them also speak of receiving Christ spiritually, by faith. Churches also differ over how often they share it, who may take it, and whether they use wine or grape juice. Ask a trustworthy church leader how your church approaches these matters, and compare what you hear with Scripture.
