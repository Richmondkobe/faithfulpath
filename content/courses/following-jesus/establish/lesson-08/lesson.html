Prayer, Worship and Fasting

I have started praying on my own. But why do Christians pray together, sing together and sometimes fast? Do I have to do all of that?

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Is it for everyone?

Welcome to Lesson 8 of Establish. In Begin, you started a simple prayer life: coming to the Father honestly, using the pattern praise, surrender, ask, forgive, protect, and keeping on praying. Perhaps you now pray most mornings on your own. Now we go one step further.

Christian life involves more than private prayer. Christians gather to pray together. They sing. Sometimes they fast. If you are new, all of this can feel strange, or even overwhelming. Is it for everyone, or only for very spiritual people?

So here is our question: I have started praying on my own. But why do Christians pray together, sing together and sometimes fast? Do I have to do all of that?

2 · They lifted their voices together

We have two passages today. The first is Acts chapter 4. Peter and John had just been arrested and warned never to speak about Jesus again. When they were released, they went back to the other believers. What did the church do? Luke writes, "they lifted their voices together to God and said, 'Sovereign Lord, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them.'"

And they asked for something surprising: "And now, Lord, look upon their threats and grant to your servants to continue to speak your word with all boldness." And God answered: "when they had prayed, the place in which they were gathered together was shaken, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and continued to speak the word of God with boldness."

Let us use the first question from Lesson 1: what does it say? Notice what they did not ask for. They did not ask only for safety, and they did not pray for their enemies' destruction. They asked for courage to keep speaking about Jesus. The shaking of the place was a special moment. But the passage gives us a pattern we can follow: God's people pray together, depend on the Holy Spirit, and ask God for courage to speak about Jesus.

3 · Seen by your Father

Our second passage is Matthew chapter 6, verses 16 to 18. Jesus says: "And when you fast, do not look gloomy like the hypocrites, for they disfigure their faces that their fasting may be seen by others. Truly, I say to you, they have received their reward. But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, that your fasting may not be seen by others but by your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you."

Which words are repeated? Seen by others, and seen by your Father in secret. Jesus is not forbidding churches from fasting together; the early church did so. He is warning against fasting to look spiritual.

4 · Together = homothymadon

Our first Greek word is in verse 24: homothymadon. It is translated "together," and it means together, with one accord.

Luke uses this word several times in the early chapters of Acts for people acting together, or with one accord. Here, the believers lift their voices to God together. Their prayer shows their shared purpose: they bring the same threat before God and ask him for boldness. Praying together is God's people seeking him together.

5 · Boldness = parrēsia

Our second Greek word is parrēsia. It is translated "boldness," and it means boldness, or open, plain speech.

The word appears three times in this chapter. In verse 13, the leaders saw the boldness of Peter and John, "and they recognized that they had been with Jesus." In verse 29, the church prays for boldness. And in verse 31, they receive it. So boldness here is not a loud personality. It is the courage to speak plainly about Jesus, and it is what the church asked for and received.

6 · Praying Psalm 2

Now the background. Peter and John had healed a man who could not walk, and they had spoken about Jesus in the temple. The leaders arrested them and "charged them not to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus." This was a real threat to a small, young church.

Now use our third question from Lesson 1: where does this connect in the Bible's larger story? The believers themselves make the connection. In their prayer, they quote Psalm 2: "Why did the Gentiles rage, and the peoples plot in vain? The kings of the earth set themselves… against the Lord and against his Anointed." They saw their situation in the light of God's word, and they prayed Scripture back to God. You can do the same.

7 · Not for an audience

There is one more piece of background. Our verses about fasting come from the Sermon on the Mount. In Matthew chapter 6, Jesus talks about three practices: giving, prayer and fasting. He begins with a warning: "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them."

His concern in each is motive. These practices are for God, not for an audience. That is true of prayer and worship as well as fasting.

8 · The short answer

So, do I have to do all of that? Here is the short answer. Praying together, worshipping together and fasting are not extra burdens for super-spiritual people. They are ways God's people give him focused attention, respond to his grace, and seek him, together and in private.

We will look at it in four parts: pray together, worship in spirit and truth, fast in secret, and keep a rhythm.

9 · Part 1: Pray Together

The first part: pray together. Praying alone is good and necessary. But from the beginning, Christians also prayed together. Paul urges "that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people," including leaders and those in authority. So believers pray for one another, for their leaders, for their communities, and for people to come to know Jesus.

Praying with others can be very simple. You might pray one sentence aloud. You might read a written prayer together. You might pray silently while others speak, agreeing in your heart. All of these count. And no one should ever be pressured to pray aloud.

10 · Part 2: Worship in Spirit and Truth

The second part: worship in spirit and truth. Jesus once spoke with a Samaritan woman who asked which mountain was the right place to worship God. Jesus moves her attention beyond the argument over the right mountain. True worship is centred on the Father, and Jesus says, "the hour is coming, and is now here, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth." As John's Gospel unfolds, we learn that true knowledge of the Father comes through Christ.

Worship includes a whole life offered to God. Paul writes, "present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship." Your work, your relationships and your choices can all be worship. Worship also includes God's people gathering to praise him, give thanks, hear his word, pray and encourage one another, "singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God." Singing teaches us truth, and it stirs our hearts to thank God. True worship focuses on God, not on performance.

11 · Part 3: Fast in Secret

The third part: fast in secret. Jesus speaks about fasting as a practice his hearers already knew, and elsewhere he says that his disciples would fast after he was taken from them. The early church also fasted at important times. In Scripture, fasting normally means voluntarily going without food for a limited time in order to seek God.

Christian fasting is humble: done for God, not to impress others. It is not a way to earn God's favour or force his hand; he is a Father, not a machine. And it is joined with justice and mercy. Through Isaiah, God rebuked people who fasted while mistreating their workers: "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness… to let the oppressed go free?" Fasting from food is not safe for everyone, and the notes below this lesson explain why. If fasting from food is not safe for you, you can still practise focused self-denial by setting aside something else, such as social media or entertainment, and giving that time to prayer. Choosing a safe way to fast is wisdom, not weak faith.

12 · Part 4: Keep a Rhythm

The fourth part: keep a rhythm. Healthy spiritual practices are sustainable. Many new believers start with great enthusiasm, try to do everything at once, burn out, and then feel guilty. God is not asking for heroic bursts. He is inviting you into a relationship.

A simple rhythm might look like this. Daily: personal prayer and Bible reading. Weekly: gathered worship with your church. Regularly: praying with others, perhaps in a small group. And occasionally: fasting, where it is safe. The rhythm serves the relationship; it is not a scorecard. If you miss something, simply begin again.

13 · Christians differ

Christians differ on two questions here, and we will name them briefly. First, styles of worship. Some churches follow set patterns of written prayers, readings and seasons. Others prefer spontaneous prayer and newer songs. Many churches welcome expressive worship, such as lifting hands, clapping and shouting praise. Others value reverent quiet and reflective silence.

Second, patterns of fasting. Some churches keep regular fast days or seasons; others call occasional fasts, or encourage personal or partial fasts. This course teaches that Scripture gives principles for worship more than a single style. What matters is that worship honours God, is true to Scripture, builds others up, and, whether expressive or quiet, is done decently and in order. And fasting should direct us towards God, repentance, justice and love of neighbour, never become a competition over food or endurance. There is a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

14 · Minh's story

Let me tell you about Minh. His story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Minh repairs motorbikes in Ho Chi Minh City. He became a Christian last year and prays every morning before opening his shop. His small church announced three days of fasting and prayer, and a friend told him, "Real believers eat nothing for three days." On the second day, working in the heat, Minh felt dizzy, burned his hand on an engine, and went home ashamed. On Sunday, during loud worship, he stood silently while others raised their hands, wondering whether God was disappointed with him.

He talked with an older couple who led the church prayer meeting. They read Matthew 6 together. "Jesus cares about your heart, not how much you can endure," the wife said. "Because you work physically in the heat, don't try another food fast while working. You could set aside your phone in the evening and use that time to pray." Her husband added, "And you don't have to raise your hands to worship. Sing the words you mean, or pray them quietly. God sees you."

Minh now joins the Wednesday prayer meeting, where he prays one or two sentences aloud when he is ready. On the next church fast, he set aside his evening phone time and prayed for his customers and his family. Many new believers feel they must copy the most intense believers around them. They need to learn a safe, honest and sustainable way to seek God.

15 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are four common mistakes.

"Only very spiritual people pray together, worship and fast." These are normal practices for all of God's people.

"I must pray aloud, sing loudly or raise my hands." Silent prayer, quiet worship and one sentence all count.

"Fasting earns answers or forces God's hand." God is a Father, not a machine.

And "I must do everything at once." Begin with a simple rhythm, and keep going.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. These practices are not burdens for the few, but gifts for all God's people.

Pray together: believers seek God together, for one another and for the world, and simple prayer counts. Worship in spirit and truth: a whole life offered to God, and gathered praise that focuses on him, not on performance. Fast in secret: humbly going without food for a time to seek God, where it is safe, and with justice and mercy; and where it is not safe, choosing another safe practice of focused self-denial. And keep a rhythm: sustainable habits that serve the relationship, not a scorecard.

18 · Prayer

Help me to seek you humbly, safely and steadily, and when I miss a day, help me simply to begin again. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Fasting from food is not safe for everyone. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have diabetes or another medical condition, take regular medication, are under 18, do heavy physical work, or have ever struggled with eating, do not fast from food without medical advice. You can still seek God by setting aside something else, such as social media or entertainment, and giving that time to prayer. If fasting makes you dizzy, faint, confused, weak or otherwise unwell, stop the fast, attend to your immediate health needs and seek medical advice where appropriate. If fasting has become connected to controlling your eating, your body or your weight, please speak with a doctor or a trustworthy person, and seek specialist support. Prayer, worship and fasting can also be misused: being told God will only answer if you fast longer or give money; being pressured into long fasts or all-night meetings when you are exhausted or unwell; being publicly shamed for not praying aloud or not worshipping expressively; or emotional intensity being used to push people into decisions about money, marriage or leaving their family. If this is happening, speak with a trustworthy person outside the situation, and see the Support Page "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority."

Optional

Go deeper

Styles of worship. Some churches follow set patterns of written prayers, readings and seasons (they often point to the patterned worship of the Psalms). Others prefer spontaneous prayer and newer songs. Many churches welcome expressive worship such as lifting hands, clapping, dancing and shouting praise (they often point to Psalm 47:1, Psalm 149:3 and 1 Corinthians 14:26). Others value reverent quiet and reflective silence (they often point to Ecclesiastes 5:1–2 and Habakkuk 2:20). This course teaches that Scripture gives principles for worship more than a single style: worship honours God, is true to Scripture, builds others up, and, whether expressive or quiet, is done "decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).

Patterns of fasting. Some churches keep regular fast days or seasons; others call occasional fasts, or encourage personal fasting. Some practise partial fasts, sometimes called a "Daniel fast". The Bible does not name or command a "Daniel fast"; it is a modern term for a partial fast inspired by Daniel's limited diet at particular times (Daniel 1:12; 10:3). This course teaches that fasting should direct us towards God, repentance, justice and love of neighbour, not become a competition over food or endurance. Ask a trustworthy church leader how your church approaches these matters, and compare what you hear with Scripture.

Fasting in the early church. The church sometimes joined fasting with prayer at important moments: in Acts 13:1–3, while worshipping and fasting, the church was directed by the Holy Spirit to set apart Barnabas and Saul; in Acts 14:23, Paul and Barnabas prayed and fasted as they appointed elders. Jesus said that after he was taken away, "then they will fast" (Mark 2:20). For practical help, see the Life Guide "Fasting: A Practical Guide".
