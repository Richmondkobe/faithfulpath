God's Kingdom and Our Christian Hope

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Where is history going?

Welcome to Lesson 10, the last lesson of Establish. In Begin, you heard Jesus announce the good news of God's kingdom. In Lesson 4, we saw that Jesus rose and reigns, and in Lesson 5, that God keeps his people for glory. Now we go one step further and ask where his reign is leading.

Wars, disasters and injustice fill the news. People we love grow ill and die. Online, some voices claim to know exactly when the world will end. It is natural to wonder whether there is any real hope. The Bible's answer is not fear and not guesswork. It is a King and a kingdom.

So here is our question: the world seems to be getting worse. Where is history going? What happens when we die, and when Jesus returns?

2 · Your kingdom come

Our main passage is the prayer Jesus taught his followers, in Matthew chapter 6, verses 9 and 10: "Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven."

Let us use the first question from Lesson 1: what does it say? Notice the direction of this prayer: God's name honoured, his kingdom coming, and his will being done "on earth as it is in heaven." Christian hope is not the abandonment of God's creation. It is the final triumph of God's rule and will.

3 · Grieve with hope

We will read two more passages with it. The first is 1 Thessalonians chapter 4. Paul wrote to new believers in Thessalonica who were grieving fellow Christians who had died. He did not tell them not to grieve. He wrote: "that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope. For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep."

Paul's comfort rests on Jesus' own resurrection. And the passage ends, "Therefore encourage one another with these words." Its purpose is comfort and hope, not a detailed timetable.

4 · Kingdom = basileia

Our first Greek word is one you met in Begin: basileia, "kingdom." When the New Testament speaks of the kingdom of God, the emphasis is first of all on God's royal rule and reign, not simply a territory with borders.

Jesus began his ministry with this announcement: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel." So when we pray "Your kingdom come," we are asking for God's rule to be welcomed and obeyed, in our lives and in the world.

5 · Coming = parousia

Our second Greek word is parousia, translated "coming" in verse 15: "we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord." It means an arrival, or a presence.

In the wider Greek world, the word could be used for the visit or arrival of an important person, such as a ruler. The New Testament uses it for the return of Jesus. It is the coming of the King.

6 · A call to faithful endurance

Now the background. Our third passage is Revelation 21:1 to 5. Revelation uses vivid symbolic images. It was written to churches facing pressure, suffering, temptation and compromise, calling them to faithful endurance. You do not need to understand every image in Revelation to grasp its clear and certain hope.

Now use our third question from Lesson 1: where does this connect in the Bible's larger story? John writes, "Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth." His words echo the prophet Isaiah, where God says, "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth." The hope at the end of the Bible is the hope God had already promised through the prophets.

7 · The short answer

So, where is history going? Here is the short answer. God's kingdom has already come in Jesus, and we live under his rule now. One day Jesus will return, raise the dead, judge the world with justice, and make all things new.

We will look at it in four parts: the kingdom has come, live it now, he will return, and all things new.

8 · Part 1: The Kingdom Has Come

The first part: the kingdom has come. People could see it in Jesus. He forgave sins, healed the sick, welcomed outsiders and set people free from evil. "If it is by the finger of God that I cast out demons," he said, "then the kingdom of God has come upon you."

Yet the kingdom did not arrive all at once. Jesus compared it to a mustard seed, small at first but growing into a large plant, and to yeast working through dough. Christians often describe this as already and not yet. God's kingdom has truly begun, but it is not yet complete. Evil, sickness and death are still with us. They do not have the last word.

9 · Part 2: Live It Now

The second part: live it now. If the King has come, his people live under his rule now. Jesus taught us to pray, "Your kingdom come," and to "seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness." Paul says the kingdom is a matter of "righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit." It looks like honest work, forgiveness, mercy and justice, care for the poor and the grieving, and telling others about the King.

We do not build God's kingdom by our own efforts. Jesus told Pilate, "My kingdom is not of this world." His kingdom does not come from the world's systems of power. No political party, nation or earthly leader is God's kingdom or can claim Christ's authority for itself. And nothing done for him is wasted: "in the Lord your labor is not in vain."

10 · Part 3: He Will Return

The third part: he will return. As Jesus ascended, angels told his disciples, "This Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven." His return will be personal and visible. When he comes, the dead in Christ will rise, and believers will always be with the Lord.

No one knows when. Jesus said, "Concerning that day and hour no one knows." So Christians live ready, not calculating dates, and any teacher who sets a date contradicts Jesus. Christ will judge with perfect justice; every wrong will be seen and dealt with justly. For those who belong to him, "there is therefore now no condemnation." And believers who die are with the Lord, though our final hope is not to remain as spirits without bodies, but bodily resurrection when Christ returns.

11 · Part 4: All Things New

The fourth part: all things new. Christian hope is not floating forever in the clouds. It is resurrection and a renewed creation. Jesus' resurrection is "the firstfruits" of a great harvest. As he rose bodily, so will his people. And creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption.

Revelation shows the end of the story: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God. He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more." And the one on the throne says, "Behold, I am making all things new." This is the blessed hope that sustains believers through suffering and loss.

12 · Christians differ

Christians differ on two questions here, and we will name them briefly. First, the order of end-time events. Revelation chapter 20 speaks of a thousand-year reign, and Christians understand it in different ways: as a future reign of Christ on earth after he returns, as a picture of Christ's present reign, or as a future time of widespread kingdom blessing before he returns. They also differ over the timing of when believers will be gathered to meet the Lord.

Second, the nature of final judgement. Many Christians believe that those who finally reject God will experience eternal conscious punishment; others understand it as irreversible destruction. Both take judgement seriously. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians confess that Christ will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead. This course teaches that he will return personally, raise the dead, judge with perfect justice and make all things new; that no one knows the day or the hour; and that salvation is found in Christ alone. There is a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

13 · Wei Ling's story

Let me tell you about Wei Ling. Her story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Wei Ling is an accountant in Kuala Lumpur. Her father, a quiet believer, died last year, and she still cries when she passes his favourite food stall. Recently a cousin sent her online videos claiming that Jesus would return within two years and that recent earthquakes proved it. One video urged viewers to give their savings to a ministry "before it is too late." Wei Ling began sleeping badly, checking the news late at night, and wondering whether her grief meant weak faith.

Her small group leader read 1 Thessalonians 4 with her. "Paul does not tell you to stop grieving," he said. "He tells you to grieve with hope. Because your father trusted Christ, you have strong reason for hope. Jesus died and rose again, and those who belong to him will be raised to new life when he returns." Then he showed her Matthew 24:36. "No one knows the date. Jesus does not want us anxious about calculations. He wants us faithful and ready, loving people now."

Wei Ling stopped watching the videos and set a limit on late-night news. She did not give her savings away. She has begun visiting a widow from her church every Saturday, and she prays "Your kingdom come" with new meaning. Fear-driven end-times teaching spreads quickly online, and believers need to be anchored in clear, hopeful Scripture.

14 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are four common mistakes.

"Christians should calculate when Jesus will return." No one knows the day or the hour.

"Heaven means floating forever as spirits." Our final hope is bodily resurrection and a renewed creation.

"This world does not matter, because it will all burn." God is making all things new, and nothing done for him is wasted.

And "Christians should not grieve." We grieve, but with hope.

15 · This week

This week, your assignment is to live in kingdom hope.

First, read Matthew 6:9 and 10, 1 Thessalonians 4:13 to 18, and Revelation 21:1 to 5. Second, write one short line for each of the four parts, in your own words. You can use the book to help. Third, pray "Your kingdom come" each day for one situation. And if it is appropriate and safe, do one act of kingdom service, such as visiting someone who is grieving, lonely or sick. Ask what would help, rather than assuming.

And fourth, follow the seven-day reading plan below this lesson, and complete "My Foundations in Following Jesus." This assignment belongs to the My Foundations area called my hope.

16 · Completing Establish

This is the last lesson of Establish. Before you move on, take time to look back over all ten lessons, with your mentor or group, or on your own. On the completion page you will find "My Foundations in Following Jesus," with the five areas you have been building: my beliefs, my life with God, my life in the Spirit, my church, and my hope.

These are reflection points, not requirements for earning God's acceptance or permission to continue. You may move into the next stage while still working through some of them. Simple words are enough. Thank God for what he has done in you through these weeks.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. God's kingdom has come in Jesus, we live as his people now, Jesus will return, and God will make all things new.

The kingdom has come: God's rule has arrived in Jesus; it is already begun but not yet complete. Live it now: seek his kingdom first, in righteousness, peace, mercy and witness; nothing done for him is wasted. He will return: personally and visibly, at a time no one knows, to raise the dead and judge with justice, and there is no condemnation for those in Christ. And all things new: resurrection bodies, a renewed creation, and God living among his people, with no more death or tears.

Thank you for the hope we have in Jesus, and for all you have taught us through these lessons. Comfort those who grieve, and keep us faithful and ready, loving people now, until he comes. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Teaching about the end can be misused. Warning signs include leaders or groups predicting dates; urging people to sell property, give large sums, stop medical treatment, leave their family or move to a special place because "the end is near"; or using fear of hell or of coming disaster to control people. Do not make an urgent financial, medical, family or relocation decision because of an online prophecy or end-times claim. Pause, test the teaching by Scripture and seek independent advice, and see the Support Page "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority." If fear about death, judgement or the end of the world keeps you from sleeping, working or enjoying life, talk with a trustworthy believer, and consider seeking help from a doctor or counsellor; seeking help is not a lack of faith. Grief is normal, and Christian hope does not remove it. If you are grieving, especially for someone whose faith you were unsure about, you may leave them in the hands of a God who is perfectly just and merciful: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). If grief remains overwhelming, consider grief support or counselling.

Optional

Go deeper

The thousand years and the order of end-time events. Revelation 20:1–6 speaks of a thousand-year reign. Some Christians believe Christ will return before a reign on earth (they often point to Revelation 20:1–6). Others understand the thousand years as a picture of Christ's present reign between his first and second comings (they often point to John 5:24–29 and Revelation 20:4). Others understand the thousand years as a future period of widespread kingdom blessing before Christ returns (they often point to passages describing the growth and influence of God's kingdom, such as Matthew 13:31–33). Christians also differ over when living believers will be "caught up" to meet the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:17). Some believe this gathering is a separate event before a time of great distress, followed later by Christ's visible return (they often point to 1 Thessalonians 1:10 and Revelation 3:10). Others believe it is part of Christ's one public return (they often point to Matthew 24:29–31 and 2 Thessalonians 2:1–4). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians confess that Christ will return in glory, raise the dead, judge the living and the dead, and make all things new.

The nature of final judgement. Many Christians believe that those who finally reject God will experience eternal conscious punishment, shut out from the joy of God's kingdom (they often point to Matthew 25:46 and Revelation 20:10–15). Others understand the final punishment as irreversible destruction rather than everlasting conscious suffering (they often point to Matthew 10:28 and 2 Thessalonians 1:9). Both take judgement seriously, and both affirm that God is perfectly just and merciful and that salvation is found in Christ alone. Ask a trustworthy church leader how your church approaches these matters, and compare what you hear with Scripture. For more help, see the Life Guide "Understanding End-Times Views".

Believers who die. Paul said that to be "away from the body" is to be "at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8), and that to "depart and be with Christ" is "far better" (Philippians 1:23). Our final hope is bodily resurrection when Christ returns, and life with God in the renewed creation. If God seems slow, Peter explains that what may seem like delay reflects God's patience and gives room for repentance (2 Peter 3:9).
