1 · How can I understand what a passage really means?

Welcome to Establish, the second stage of Following Jesus. In Begin, you learned that the Bible is breathed out by God, and you began to read it every day: pray, read or listen, notice and ask, and pray it back. Now we are going one step further. This lesson slows down the step called notice and ask, so that you can understand a passage carefully and apply it faithfully.

Here is why it matters. People who all say they believe the Bible sometimes use it to prove very different things. One preacher quotes a verse to promise wealth; another quotes the Bible to warn against loving money. Someone sends you a verse on social media that seems to say exactly what they already believed.

So here is our question: how can I understand what a passage really means? By the end of this lesson, you will have four simple questions to use on any passage of the Bible.

2 · The book of the Law is opened

Our passage is Nehemiah chapter 8. Let us read it together. Verse 1: "And all the people gathered as one man into the square before the Water Gate. And they told Ezra the scribe to bring the Book of the Law of Moses that the LORD had commanded Israel."

Verse 2 says that the crowd included men, women and all who could understand. Verse 3: "And he read from it facing the square before the Water Gate from early morning until midday, in the presence of the men and the women and those who could understand. And the ears of all the people were attentive to the Book of the Law."

Ezra stood on a wooden platform made for the occasion. Verse 5: "And Ezra opened the book in the sight of all the people, for he was above all the people, and as he opened it all the people stood."

3 · "They gave the sense"

Now listen to verses 7 and 8. A group of teachers called Levites "helped the people to understand the Law, while the people remained in their places. They read from the book, from the Law of God, clearly, and they gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading."

Notice two things that belong together. First, the word itself, read clearly. Second, help to understand it. The people did not only hear the words. Teachers moved among them and explained, so that they understood. That is what this lesson is about: not only hearing God's word, but understanding it.

4 · The word the passage repeats

Today we need only one Hebrew word, and the passage itself shows us why. Nehemiah 8 keeps returning to the Hebrew root bîn, which has to do with understanding or discerning. Different forms of that word appear again and again through the chapter. In verse 2, the crowd includes "all who could understand." In verse 3, "those who could understand." In verse 7, the Levites "helped the people to understand." In verse 8, "the people understood the reading." And in verse 12, they rejoiced "because they had understood the words."

Repeated words can show what a passage emphasises. Here, the goal of that great day was not only that the Law was read, but that God's people understood it.

And the phrase translated "they gave the sense" is, literally, something like "putting insight" into the reading. The teachers did not add a new message. They helped the people grasp the meaning of the message that was already there.

5 · Home from exile

Now the background. Several generations earlier, Jerusalem had been destroyed and many of God's people had been taken into exile in Babylon. The exile had come because the nation had turned away from God and ignored his word.

Now many had returned home. Under Nehemiah's leadership, they had just rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem. But walls were not enough. They needed to know again the God who had brought them back, and to live as his people. So they gathered and asked for God's word.

And as they understood it, they were deeply moved. Verse 9 says, "For all the people wept as they heard the words of the Law." They saw how far they had fallen short.

6 · Understanding brings joy

But the leaders told the people not to grieve on that holy day. They said, "the joy of the LORD is your strength." And verse 12 tells us that the people went away to eat and drink, to share with others, and "to make great rejoicing, because they had understood the words that were declared to them." Notice the reason for their joy: they had understood.

Centuries later, in the book of Acts, we see the same healthy pattern in the town of Berea. The believers there "received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so." They listened eagerly to the apostle Paul, and then they checked what he said against Scripture. We read the Bible personally, and we also read it with God's people, and we test what we hear by the Scriptures themselves.

7 · What Christians believe about the Bible

Before we learn the four questions, let us remember what Christians believe about the Bible. In Begin, Lesson 6, we saw that "All Scripture is breathed out by God." Peter adds that "men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit." So the Bible has real human writers, with their own languages and styles, and it is also God's word. Because it is God's word, it is trustworthy, and it carries God's authority for what we believe and how we live.

Its main message, salvation through Jesus Christ, is clear enough for ordinary people to understand. "The testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple." That does not mean every passage is equally easy. Even Peter admitted that some things in Paul's letters "are hard to understand."

So we read the Bible with prayer, asking the Holy Spirit to help us, just as the risen Jesus "opened their minds to understand the Scriptures."

8 · The short answer

So how can I understand what a passage really means? Here is the short answer. Read carefully, read in context, read with Jesus at the centre, and read with God's people.

We will turn that into four questions. What does it say? What did it mean? Where does it point? And how should we respond? Let us take them one at a time.

9 · Question 1: What It Says

The first question is simply: what does the passage actually say? It is surprising how often we decide what a verse means before we have read it carefully. So slow down and notice. Who is speaking, and to whom? What happens, or what is being said? Which words or ideas are repeated? You have already done this today, in Nehemiah 8.

And then ask: what comes before and after? That question matters most. A single verse lifted out of its paragraph can be made to say almost anything. So read the whole paragraph, and if you can, the whole chapter.

10 · Question 2: What It Meant

The second question is: what did this passage mean in its original setting? The Bible was written in real places, to real people, long ago. So before we ask what it means for us, we ask what it meant there. Who first received it, and what was happening?

Two things help here. First, notice the kind of writing. The Bible contains stories, poems, laws, prophecies, wise sayings, letters and parables, and we read each kind differently. A proverb, for example, tells us what is usually wise, not what is guaranteed in every case.

Second, tell apart description, command and lasting significance. Description is what happened, and the Bible honestly records sin and failure; not everything it describes is approved by God. A command tells us what God commanded people to do. We ask who received it, in what setting, and how it applies to God's people now. Lasting significance asks what the passage teaches God's people beyond its first setting. For example, God told Israel to build a wooden ark for his covenant. We do not build arks, but we learn that God was present with his people, and that they were to treat his presence with reverence.

11 · Question 3: Where It Points

The third question is: how does this passage fit into the whole story of the Bible? In Begin you saw that the Bible tells one great story that finds its centre in Jesus. After his resurrection, Jesus explained to his disciples "that everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled."

This does not mean forcing Jesus into every detail of every verse. It means asking: how does this passage connect to God's promises? How does it relate to the gospel? What does it show about life as God's people in Christ?

Paul writes that the Old Testament was "written for our instruction," but we read it as people who live after Jesus has come. Think of Nehemiah 8. God's people had been brought home and gathered around God's word. As we read that passage within the whole Bible, we know that God's work did not end there. The story continues towards Jesus, who brings God's saving promises to fulfilment and who, after his resurrection, opened his disciples' minds to understand the Scriptures.

12 · Question 4: How We Respond

Only after the first three questions do we ask the fourth: how should we respond? James warns against hearing without doing: "But be doers of the word, and not hearers only."

A response might be one truth to believe, one action to take, one prayer to pray, or one question to carry.

But here is the key: application flows from the meaning, not from what we wish a verse said. If we skip the first three questions, we often end up using the Bible to support what we already wanted. Paul urged Timothy to be "a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly handling the word of truth."

13 · Christians sometimes interpret differently

Faithful Christians do not agree about every difficult passage. That should not frighten us. It is one reason we read carefully. We pray. We compare Scripture with Scripture, letting clearer passages help us with harder ones. We listen to the wider church, not only to ourselves. And we remain willing to be corrected.

On how Christians describe the Bible, and how Old Testament law applies today, you will find a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

14 · Rosa's story

Let me tell you about Rosa. Her story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Rosa runs a small sewing business in Lima and attends a women's Bible group at her church. For years she kept a card on her sewing machine with Philippians 4:13: "I can do all things through him who strengthens me." She believed it promised that her business would grow if she had enough faith. When sales fell, she felt confused and ashamed.

One evening, the group leader asked them to read Philippians 4:10 to 13 using the four questions. Rosa noticed verse 11 for the first time: "Not that I am speaking of being in need, for I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content."

The group talked about the setting. Paul was writing from prison, thanking a church for a gift. He had known both plenty and hunger. "So 'all things' includes being hungry," Rosa said slowly. "It's about strength to be faithful in any situation. It isn't a promise of success."

Rosa's business is still struggling, and she is still praying and working hard. But she has a new card on her machine, with Philippians 4:11 to 13, and she now encourages the other women to read verses in their setting.

15 · What understanding the Bible is not

Here are four common mistakes.

"Bible study is only for scholars." The four questions are for everyone, and listening to the Bible counts fully.

"Every verse is a personal promise for me to claim today." Some promises were made to particular people. Ask what the passage meant first.

"Everything the Bible describes, God approves." The Bible honestly records sin. Tell description, command and lasting significance apart.

And "Only our leader can tell us what the Bible means." Healthy teachers help people read the Bible for themselves, and they welcome questions, just as Paul welcomed the Bereans checking the Scriptures.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Read carefully, read in context, read with Jesus at the centre, and read with God's people.

What it says: observe carefully, and read what comes before and after. What it meant: ask what it meant in its original setting, and tell description, command and lasting significance apart. Where it points: see how it fits into the whole story that leads to Jesus. And how we respond: one truth to believe, one action to take, one prayer to pray or one question to carry, because application flows from the meaning.

You will use these four questions in every lesson of Establish, and in the reading plan each week.

18 · Prayer

Let us pray. Father, thank you for your word, breathed out by you and given to us. Open my mind to understand the Scriptures, as Jesus did for his disciples. Help me to read carefully, to listen with your people, and to be willing to be corrected. Make me a doer of your word, and not a hearer only, and may understanding your word bring me joy, as it did for your people long ago. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Be careful of any group or leader who claims that only they can interpret the Bible, that it has secret meanings only they can unlock, or that you must not read it for yourself or ask questions. Healthy teachers welcome questions (Acts 17:11). If this is happening to you, seek help outside that group: see “I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority”.

Some Bible passages describe violence, abuse or great suffering. You may pause, leave a passage for now, and talk with someone you trust.

Optional

Go deeper

Words used to describe the Bible. Some churches use words such as "inerrant" or "infallible" to describe the Bible, and they define them in different ways. This course does not build its teaching on any one of these terms. It keeps to what the Bible says about itself: it is "breathed out by God" (2 Timothy 3:16); "your word is truth" (John 17:17); and God's word "shall not return to me empty" (Isaiah 55:11).

How Old Testament law applies today. Christians agree from Scripture that the Old Testament is God's word; that Jesus came "not to abolish… but to fulfill" the law (Matthew 5:17); that some commands, such as the food laws and the animal sacrifices, are fulfilled in Christ (Mark 7:19; Hebrews 10:1–14); and that the Old Testament still instructs God's people (Romans 15:4). Churches differ over how some particular commandments apply today. In each case, ask what the passage meant in its original setting and how it is fulfilled in Christ.
