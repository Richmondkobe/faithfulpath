Jesus: His Life, Cross, Resurrection and Reign

I know Jesus died and rose again. But who exactly is he, and why does all of his life matter, not only the cross?

About 18 minutesPhilippians 2:5–11My Foundations: My beliefs

In this lesson

You will learn

  • The shape of Jesus' story in Philippians 2:5–11: down to the cross, then up to the throne
  • What "he emptied himself" means, and what it does not mean
  • The four movements: He Came, He Lived, He Died, He Rose and Reigns
  • How Jesus' humility shapes the way we treat one another
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Who exactly is Jesus?

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Establish. In Begin, you learned the good news about Jesus, and that he is Lord. And in Lesson 3, we saw that God promised hope from the very beginning. Now we go one step further and ask how God kept that promise: in Jesus Christ.

Many Christians can say, "Jesus died for my sins and rose again." That is true, and it is the heart of the gospel. But sometimes Jesus' life between his birth and his death gets skipped over, as if it were only a long introduction to the cross. And sometimes people admire Jesus as a great teacher or example without realising who he really is.

So here is our question: who exactly is Jesus, and why does all of his life matter, not only the cross?

2 · The way down

Our passage is Philippians chapter 2, verses 5 to 11. These verses may have been sung or recited by early Christians, and they tell the whole story of Jesus in a few lines. Listen to verses 5 to 8.

"Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross."

3 · The way up

Now verses 9 to 11: "Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."

Let us use the first question from Lesson 1: what does it say? Notice the shape of the passage. It moves down: from equality with God, to becoming human, to becoming a servant, to death on a cross. Then it moves up: God exalts him and gives him the name above every name.

And notice why Paul tells this story. Just before, he asks the church to act with humility, counting others more significant than themselves. Then he says, "Have this mind among yourselves." Knowing Jesus' story is meant to shape how we treat one another.

4 · "He emptied himself"

Our one Greek word today is in verse 7: ekenōsen, which means "he emptied himself." People have sometimes taken this to mean that when the Son became human, he stopped being God, or gave up being God.

But Paul explains what he means, in the same sentence: Christ took "the form of a servant," and was "born in the likeness of men." He did not stop being God. The eternal Son humbled himself by taking our humanity and a servant's place.

5 · Philippi and the cross

Now the background. Philippi was a Roman colony, a city that took pride in its Roman connections. The believers there knew very well what the cross meant.

Crucifixion was a slow, shameful Roman execution. It was not a symbol on a necklace. That is why Paul's words are so striking: "even death on a cross." The one who was in the form of God went all the way down, to the most shameful death that people of the Roman world could imagine. Jesus truly suffered and truly died.

6 · "Every knee shall bow"

There is another piece of background that is easy to miss. In Isaiah 45, the LORD says, "To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance." In Isaiah, those words belong to the one true God.

In Philippians 2, Paul takes these words and says them about Jesus: at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord. In Begin, Lesson 4, you learned that Lord, kyrios, is the title Jesus' followers gave him. Here we see how high that title reaches. This is the third question from Lesson 1, where it points: Isaiah's words about the LORD find their fulfilment in Jesus, to the glory of God the Father.

7 · The short answer

So, who exactly is Jesus, and why does all of his life matter? Here is the short answer. Jesus is the eternal Son of God who became fully human. His whole life matters, because his whole life is God's rescue in action.

The Son of God came to be one of us. He lived the faithful life we have failed to live and showed us God's kingdom. He died for our sins. He rose from the dead and now reigns as Lord. We will look at this in four movements: he came, he lived, he died, and he rose and reigns.

8 · Movement 1: He Came

The first movement: he came. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us." In Lesson 2, we saw that the Son is fully God. Here is the wonder: the eternal Son of God became a real human being. He was born as a baby, grew up in an ordinary family, worked with his hands, ate, slept, wept and felt tired.

Jesus is fully God and fully human. He is not God pretending to be human, and he is not a man who became God. And because he shared our life, he understands us. Hebrews says that we have "one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin." When you are tempted, tired or hurting, you are not praying to one who is distant from human weakness.

9 · Movement 2: He Lived

The second movement: he lived. Jesus' life was not a waiting period before the cross. Everything he did mattered. He announced the kingdom: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand." And he showed what God's kingdom looks like. Peter later said, "He went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him."

Jesus taught with authority. He healed the sick. He forgave sins. He welcomed people others pushed away: tax collectors, lepers, women with bad reputations, children. He confronted evil and hypocrisy. And through it all, he obeyed his Father perfectly.

His life shows us two things. It shows us who God is: "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father." And it shows us what a truly human life looks like: complete trust in God and complete love for others. Jesus lived the faithful life that we have failed to live.

10 · Movement 3: He Died

The third movement: he died. Why did he die? The Bible gives several pictures. Each shows something true, and together they help us see what the cross achieved.

In our place: "He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities." Peter writes that Christ suffered once for sins, "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God." Jesus took the penalty our sins deserved. A ransom: Jesus said he came "to give his life as a ransom for many." A ransom is a price paid to set captives free.

Victory: on the cross, God "disarmed the rulers and authorities." The cross defeated the powers of evil. And reconciliation: "in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself." The cross brings enemies back into friendship with God. Jesus' death is also an example of faithful love, but it is far more than an example. It dealt with our sin and opened the way back to God.

11 · Keeping the cross clear

Three truths keep us from misunderstanding the cross. First, the Father was not unwilling. The cross was not a loving Son persuading an angry, unwilling Father to forgive us. "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." The Father loved us, and the Son willingly gave himself for us.

Second, the Son was not an unwilling victim. Jesus said of his life, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord."

And third, the death of Jesus can never be blamed on Jewish people as a group, then or now. Jesus and all his first followers were Jewish. The early believers prayed that Herod and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, had gathered against him. Christians confess something much more personal: Jesus gave himself willingly because of the sins of all humanity, including our own.

12 · Movement 4: He Rose and Reigns

The fourth movement: he rose and reigns. The story does not end at the cross. On the third day, Jesus rose bodily from the dead. Paul does not present the resurrection as merely a spiritual experience or a feeling among Jesus' followers. He presents it as a real event, names people who saw the risen Jesus, and says that most of them were still alive when he wrote.

Paul is very direct about how much depends on this: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins… But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead."

Forty days later, Jesus ascended to heaven. He now reigns at the Father's right hand, "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion." And he is not distant from his people: "he always lives to make intercession for them." Jesus is praying for those who belong to him. The one who humbled himself to the cross is the risen Lord before whom every knee will finally bow.

13 · Christians differ

Christians differ on two questions here, and we will name them briefly. First, how the Bible's pictures of the cross fit together. Across historic Christian traditions, Jesus' death and resurrection stand at the centre of God's saving work, and all the pictures we have seen are in Scripture. Christians explain how they fit together in different ways. Some churches emphasise Jesus bearing the penalty for sin in our place as the centre that holds the others together; others give more emphasis to his victory over sin, death and evil. This lesson keeps all the biblical pictures together.

Second, healing and the cross. Some churches teach that physical healing is included in Christ's saving work and pray expectantly for healing now; others teach that the cross guarantees the body's complete healing at the resurrection, while God may also heal now according to his wisdom. Whatever the view, continuing illness is never proof that a person lacks faith, and prayer for healing should go together with medical care, not replace it. There is a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

14 · Seo-yeon's story

Let me tell you about Seo-yeon. Her story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Seo-yeon is an accountant in Seoul. She had attended church since her twenties and thought of Jesus mainly as the perfect example of how to be good. When her grandmother died, she found that "be good like Jesus" gave her nothing to hold on to. At the funeral she kept asking herself, "Is death simply the end?"

A few weeks later, her small group read 1 Corinthians 15. She noticed how Paul listed the people who saw Jesus alive, and how firmly he said that the resurrection either happened or Christian faith is futile. "I always thought of Jesus as a teacher," she said. "I didn't realise he actually defeated death." The group leader showed her Philippians 2 and the whole story: he came, he lived, he died, he rose and reigns.

Seo-yeon still grieves her grandmother deeply, and that is right. Christian hope does not require her to stop grieving. But she has begun reading the Gospels again, this time paying attention to who Jesus is, not only how he behaved.

15 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are four common mistakes.

"Jesus was only a good teacher or example." He is the eternal Son of God, who died and rose to save us.

"Jesus stopped being God when he became human." He took on our humanity without losing his divine nature.

"The Father was angry and the Son was loving." The Father loved us, and the Son willingly gave himself for us.

And "The resurrection is a nice symbol." Jesus truly rose bodily from the dead, and everything depends on it.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Jesus' whole life is God's rescue in action.

He came: the eternal Son became fully human, without ceasing to be God. He lived: he announced and showed God's kingdom, and lived the faithful life we have failed to live. He died: in our place, as a ransom, in victory over evil, to reconcile us to God. And he rose and reigns: he rose bodily, ascended, prays for his people and rules as Lord.

18 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, eternal Son of God, thank you for coming to be one of us. Thank you for your faithful life, and for showing us the Father. Thank you for giving yourself for me on the cross. Thank you that you are risen and reign, and that you pray for your people. Give me your mind, so that I may serve others with humility. To the glory of God the Father. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Jesus' suffering is sometimes misused to tell people they should silently accept abuse. This is a serious misuse. Christ freely chose his unique saving suffering; no one may use his cross to impose suffering on someone else. Seeking safety, reporting wrongdoing and setting boundaries are not failures to follow Jesus. Speak with a trustworthy person outside the harmful situation, and see the Support Page "I am in danger or being abused." If teaching about healing has left you feeling guilty because you or someone you love is still ill, please hear this: illness is not proof of weak faith.

Optional

Go deeper

How the pictures of the cross fit together. Across historic Christian traditions, Jesus' death and resurrection stand at the centre of God's saving work, and all the pictures in this lesson are found in Scripture. Christians explain how these pictures fit together in different ways. Some churches emphasise Jesus bearing the penalty for sin in our place as the centre that holds the others together (they often point to Isaiah 53:5–6 and 2 Corinthians 5:21). Others give more emphasis to Christ's victory over sin, death and evil (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14–15). This course keeps all the biblical pictures together.

Healing and the cross. Some churches teach that physical healing is included in Christ's saving work, and encourage believers to pray expectantly for healing now (they often point to Isaiah 53:4–5 and Matthew 8:16–17). Others teach that the cross guarantees the complete healing of the body at the resurrection, while God may also heal in this life according to his wisdom (they often point to 1 Peter 2:24 in its context and 2 Corinthians 4:16–5:4). Whatever their view, continuing illness is never sufficient evidence that a person lacks faith, has failed to claim a promise, or is outside God's favour. Prayer for healing should go together with appropriate medical care, not replace it.
