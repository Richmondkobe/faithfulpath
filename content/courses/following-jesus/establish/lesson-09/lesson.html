The Church: Belonging, Leadership, Service and Care

I belong to a church now. Who is in charge, what is expected of me, and what should happen when something goes wrong?

About 18 minutesEphesians 4:11–16 with 1 Peter 5:1–4My Foundations: My church

In this lesson

You will learn

  • Why Christ gives leaders to his church, from Ephesians 4:11–16
  • What good leaders are and are not, from 1 Peter 5:1–4
  • The four parts: Led by Christ, Shepherded With Care, Built Up Together, Corrected and Protected
  • Why respect for leaders is not blind obedience, and how a healthy church protects people
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Who is in charge?

Welcome to Lesson 9 of Establish. In Begin, you saw that following Jesus means belonging to his family: belonging, gathering together, caring for one another, and finding a healthy church. Now we go one step further and look inside the family of God.

Once you belong, new questions come. Who leads the church, and how much authority do they have? What is expected of me? And what happens when something goes wrong: when people disagree, when someone sins, or when someone is harmed? Knowing how a healthy church works helps you to honour good leaders, to serve well, and to recognise when something is wrong.

So here is our question: I belong to a church now. Who is in charge, what is expected of me, and what should happen when something goes wrong?

2 · To equip the saints

We have two passages today. The first is Ephesians chapter 4. Paul lists people Christ has given to the church: "And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ."

Let us use the first question from Lesson 1: what does it say? Notice the purpose. Leaders are not given so that they can be important, or so that they can do all the work. They are given to equip everyone else, so that the whole church grows to maturity and is not tossed to and fro by false teaching. And verse 16 says the body grows "when each part is working properly," so that "it builds itself up in love."

3 · Shepherd the flock

The second passage is 1 Peter chapter 5. Peter was an apostle, but he calls himself "a fellow elder." Listen to how he describes good leadership: "shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you; not for shameful gain, but eagerly; not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock. And when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory."

Willing, not forced. Eager, not greedy. An example, not a tyrant. And leaders will answer to the chief Shepherd, Jesus himself. This passage gives believers a clear picture of what to expect from leaders, and it gives leaders a clear standard to live by.

4 · Equip = katartismos

Our Greek word today is in Ephesians 4 verse 12: katartismos. It is translated "to equip," and it means equipping, preparing, making ready.

Christ gives these leaders "to equip the saints for the work of ministry." In the New Testament, the word saints does not mean a few especially holy people; it means every believer. The picture is not of leaders doing all the ministry while everyone else watches. God's people are equipped to serve, and the whole body is built up.

5 · Paul and the Ephesian elders

Now the background. Paul had spent about three years in Ephesus. Later, on his way to Jerusalem, he called the elders of the Ephesian church to meet him, and he gave them a farewell charge.

He told them, "Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock… to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood." He warned them that "fierce wolves will come in among you," and told them to guard the flock. Notice two things. The church belongs to God, not to its leaders. And leaders must watch over their own lives as well as protecting the people in their care.

6 · Shepherds in Israel

Now use our third question from Lesson 1: where does this connect in the Bible's larger story? In the Old Testament, Israel's leaders were often called shepherds. Through the prophet Ezekiel, God rebuked shepherds who used the people for themselves: "Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" And God promised, "I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out."

Jesus takes up this picture when he says, "I am the good shepherd." And Peter calls him "the chief Shepherd." So church leaders are under-shepherds. The flock belongs to God, and every leader answers to Jesus, the good shepherd who laid down his life for the sheep.

7 · The short answer

So, who is in charge, what is expected of me, and what should happen when something goes wrong? Here is the short answer. Jesus is the head of the church. He gives leaders to serve and equip his people. Every member has a part to play. And a healthy church corrects gently and protects the vulnerable.

We will look at it in four parts: led by Christ, shepherded with care, built up together, and corrected and protected.

8 · Part 1: Led by Christ

The first part: led by Christ. Paul writes, "he is the head of the body, the church." Jesus is the chief Shepherd. Every church leader, however senior, serves under him and under his word. Church leaders have real responsibility. But no leader owns God's people, and no leader stands above Scripture.

When James and John asked Jesus for the most important positions, he said, "Whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all. For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." In Christ's church, greatness is measured by service.

9 · Part 2: Shepherded With Care

The second part: shepherded with care. When Paul describes elders or overseers, he focuses mostly on character and faithfulness: self-controlled, gentle, not quarrelsome, not a lover of money, faithful in family life, well thought of by outsiders, and able to teach. The Bible's emphasis is not charisma, impressive gifts or success, but character.

Acts chapter 6 gives a helpful example. When some widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution of food, the church chose trustworthy people "full of the Spirit and of wisdom" to make sure their needs were handled fairly. Good leadership pays attention to people who are easily missed.

10 · Respect, not blind obedience

How should believers relate to faithful leaders? Paul asks believers "to respect those who labor among you… and to esteem them very highly in love because of their work." Hebrews tells us to remember our leaders, consider their way of life, and imitate their faith.

But respect is not blind obedience. The Bereans checked even Paul's teaching against Scripture, "examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so." Leaders must not demand unquestioning obedience, or claim final authority over personal decisions Scripture leaves to Christian wisdom and conscience, such as whom to marry, where to work or where to live. Faithful leaders teach God's word, give counsel and equip believers to follow Christ. They do not take Christ's place in another person's life.

11 · Part 3: Built Up Together

The third part: built up together. Leaders equip; every member serves. The body grows "when each part is working properly." There are no spectators in God's family. Believers serve with the gifts God has given them: welcoming, teaching, encouraging, giving, helping and caring. And they build one another up by speaking the truth in love.

Giving is part of this too. Believers support the church's work through generous, cheerful giving: "God loves a cheerful giver." Paul arranged for others to travel with the money he collected, "for we aim at what is honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man." So a healthy church should handle money openly and honestly, with appropriate accountability, rather than leaving church funds under one person's unchecked control.

12 · Part 4: Corrected and Protected

The fourth part: corrected and protected. Every church will face sin, conflict and harm, because every church is made up of imperfect people. A healthy church corrects with gentleness: "If anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness." The aim is repentance and restoration where possible, not humiliation.

For ordinary personal sin and conflict, where it is safe and appropriate, Jesus gives a process that normally begins by speaking privately with the person, then involving one or two others if needed, and only then the church. But suspected abuse, criminal behaviour and safeguarding concerns are different. They should never be forced through this private-confrontation process; they go directly to the proper people. And a healthy church protects the flock and holds its leaders accountable. Crimes and abuse are not matters for the church to handle alone.

13 · Christians differ

Christians differ on three questions here, and we will name them briefly. First, how churches are governed. Some churches are led by bishops, some by elders or a council, and some make major decisions as a congregation. Second, women in church leadership. Many churches recognise women as pastors and leaders. Other churches affirm women's ministry in many forms, but understand the role of elder or pastor to be reserved for qualified men. Christians who take Scripture seriously reach different conclusions about these passages.

Third, tithing and giving. Some churches teach giving a tenth of income as the standard for believers; others teach generous, proportional and cheerful giving without a fixed percentage. This course teaches that, in every form of church government, leaders should be accountable and no one should be beyond correction; that women and men are equally made in God's image and equally gifted by the Spirit for service; and that giving is a glad response to God's generosity, never a way to buy blessing, and no one should be shamed or pressured into giving. There is a short note in Go deeper, below this lesson.

14 · Esther's story

Let me tell you about Esther. Her story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Esther is a primary school teacher in Douala. After a year in her church, she joined the children's ministry team. Over several weeks, she noticed that a popular volunteer often kept one child behind after class. One Sunday, she saw a message on his phone asking the child to keep their chats secret.

Esther felt sick. He was liked by everyone, and she was new. She wondered whether speaking up would be "touching the Lord's anointed." But she went to the church's safeguarding lead, as her training had taught her. The lead listened carefully, thanked her, and recorded exactly what she had seen. She acted at once to prevent any further unsupervised contact, and promptly contacted the proper authority. Church leaders did not question the child or run their own investigation. The senior pastor later told the team, "We will always choose the safety of a child over the reputation of an adult or the church."

Esther still serves in children's ministry, and she now helps train new volunteers. Many believers hesitate to raise concerns about respected people. A healthy church makes it safe to speak up, and acts to protect the vulnerable.

15 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are four common mistakes.

"Church leaders are bosses who must be obeyed without question." Leaders serve under Christ and his word; respect is not blind obedience.

"All leaders are untrustworthy." Most leaders serve faithfully; honour them and pray for them.

"Members just attend; leaders do the ministry." Leaders equip; every member serves.

And "Matthew 18 means abuse should be handled privately." Matthew 18 is for ordinary conflict; abuse and safeguarding concerns go to the proper people.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Christ leads his church through servant leaders, every member serves, and a healthy church corrects gently and protects the vulnerable.

Led by Christ: Jesus is the head; no leader owns God's people or stands above Scripture. Shepherded with care: leaders equip, serve and set an example; character matters most, and respect is not blind obedience. Built up together: every member serves and gives, and money is handled openly, with accountability. And corrected and protected: gentle correction seeking restoration; Matthew 18 for ordinary conflict; abuse reported, never hidden; and leaders accountable.

18 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you are the head of your church and our good shepherd. Thank you for the leaders who serve and care for us; give them wisdom, humility and courage. Help me to serve with the gifts you have given me. Make our church a place of truth, love and safety, where the weak are protected and everyone can grow. In your name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Most church leaders serve faithfully, but some misuse authority. Warning signs of spiritual abuse include leaders who cannot be questioned or corrected; demands for obedience in personal decisions such as marriage, work, travel or where you live; secrecy about money; public shaming of those who do not give or who disagree; cutting people off from family or friends; and threats that God will punish you if you leave. Some leaders misuse Psalm 105:15 ("Touch not my anointed ones") to silence concerns; in that psalm, God warns kings not to harm his covenant people, and it does not forbid questioning, reporting or correcting leaders. Abuse of any kind, including sexual misconduct by a leader or any harm to a child or vulnerable adult, is not a private church matter and must never be covered up. Suspected abuse of a child or vulnerable adult must be referred promptly according to local law and safeguarding procedure. If you are an adult being harmed, you can seek support and choose your reporting options; see the Support Pages "I am in danger or being abused" and "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority." No one should be threatened, shamed, removed or publicly identified for raising a safeguarding concern in good faith. Leaving a church is sometimes right, especially when there is abuse, serious and persistent false teaching about core Christian truth, or ongoing control. You do not need a leader's permission to leave, and leaving an unsafe church is not leaving God.

Optional

Go deeper

How churches are governed. Some churches are led by bishops (they often point to the oversight given to Timothy and Titus in 1 Timothy 1:3 and Titus 1:5). Some are led by elders or a council (they often point to Acts 14:23 and 20:17). Some make major decisions as a congregation (they often point to Acts 6:3–5 and Matthew 18:17). This course teaches that, in every form, leaders should be accountable, and no one person should be beyond correction.

Women in church leadership. Many churches recognise women as pastors and leaders. They point to the Spirit being poured out on both sons and daughters for ministry (Acts 2:17–18), to women such as Priscilla and Phoebe serving significantly in the early church (Acts 18:26; Romans 16:1–2), and to other women Paul names among his fellow workers (Romans 16). Other churches affirm women's ministry in many forms but understand the role of elder or pastor to be reserved for qualified men, pointing especially to 1 Timothy 2:11–14 and 3:1–7. Christians who take Scripture seriously reach different conclusions about these passages. This course teaches that women and men are equally made in God's image (Lesson 3) and equally gifted by the Spirit for service.

Tithing and giving. Some churches teach giving a tenth of income as the standard for believers (they often point to Malachi 3:8–10 and Matthew 23:23). Others teach generous, proportional and cheerful giving without a fixed percentage (they often point to 1 Corinthians 16:2 and 2 Corinthians 9:6–8). This course teaches that giving is a glad response to God's generosity, not a way to buy blessing, and that no one should be shamed or pressured into giving. Ask a trustworthy church leader how your church approaches these matters, and compare what you hear with Scripture.

Accountability and civil authorities. Serious accusations against leaders must be taken seriously and handled fairly (1 Timothy 5:19–21). The words about "two or three witnesses" in 1 Timothy 5:19 must never be used to refuse to listen to a disclosure or to block a safeguarding report; a concern may need to be reported and assessed even when only one person has spoken. The Bible recognises that God has given civil authorities a role in restraining and punishing wrongdoing (Romans 13:1–4).
