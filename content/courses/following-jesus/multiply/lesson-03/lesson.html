Lesson 3 of 10 · Book chapter 33

Telling Your Story Honestly

My story isn't dramatic. And some parts of my past are painful or private. What should I say if someone asks why I follow Jesus?

About 17 minutesMark 5:18–20 and Acts 26Getting ready: A first conversation plan

In this lesson

You will learn

  • Why a testimony is simply telling what the Lord has done (Mark 5:18–20)
  • How Paul told his story honestly before King Agrippa, and left him free (Acts 26)
  • The four parts: What He Has Done, Before Meeting Since, Honest Not Exaggerated, Point to Jesus
  • How to tell a short, true story, including a quiet one, without sharing what is painful or private
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Is my story enough?

Welcome to Lesson 3. At the end of Begin, you were invited to tell your story in your own words. And in Lesson 2, you learned to be ready with reasons when someone asks about your hope. Now we are going one step further.

Many believers feel nervous when asked to share their testimony. Some think their story is too ordinary, because there is no dramatic moment to describe. Others have stories with painful parts they do not want to share. And some have heard testimonies that seemed exaggerated, or focused more on the speaker than on Jesus.

So here is our question: My story isn't dramatic. And some parts of my past are painful or private. What should I say if someone asks why I follow Jesus?

2 · Go home and tell them

Our first passage is Mark chapter 5, verses 19 and 20: "And he did not permit him but said to him, 'Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how he has had mercy on you.' And he went away and began to proclaim in the Decapolis how much Jesus had done for him, and everyone marveled."

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Notice what Jesus asks this man to tell: how much the Lord has done for him, and how he has had mercy on him. The story is about the Lord's mercy.

3 · Paul before King Agrippa

Our second passage is Acts chapter 26. Paul was on trial before King Agrippa, and he was given permission to speak. He spoke respectfully, and he told his story honestly, including his violent past: "I myself was convinced that I ought to do many things in opposing the name of Jesus of Nazareth." Then he described meeting Jesus, and he connected his story to Jesus' death and resurrection.

Agrippa replied, "In a short time would you persuade me to be a Christian?" Acts records no conversion. Paul openly said he wished Agrippa would become as he was, "except for these chains". But he did not manipulate or pressure him. A story can be faithfully told even when the listener does not believe.

4 · Witness = martys (recalled)

You met our Greek word in Lesson 1: martys, a witness, a person who tells what they have seen and know. It appears again in our passage. In Acts 26, Paul tells Agrippa what the risen Jesus said to him on the road: "I have appeared to you for this purpose, to appoint you as a servant and witness to the things in which you have seen me."

So a testimony is not a performance. It is a witness telling what Jesus has shown them. Paul's witness to the risen Christ was unique. But every believer can tell, simply and truthfully, how the same Jesus has met them.

5 · Sent home to the Decapolis

Now the background. Jesus had crossed the Sea of Galilee to the country of the Gerasenes, in the region called the Decapolis. It was largely non-Jewish; Mark mentions a large herd of pigs feeding nearby. The man had lived among the tombs, tormented and alone.

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? After Jesus set him free, the townspeople were afraid and asked Jesus to leave. The man begged to go with Jesus. But Jesus sent him home, to people who knew his past. Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus often told people to say nothing. Here, he sends this man to tell his own town, as an early witness in a non-Jewish region.

His story was personal, local and focused on the Lord's mercy. One more thing: this passage describes a particular work of evil spirits. It does not teach that mental illness or other conditions are caused by demons.

6 · Paul's defense in Caesarea

Our second passage takes place in Caesarea, on the coast. Paul had been held there as a prisoner for two years. King Agrippa, a ruler who knew the Jewish customs and Scriptures, came to hear him, and Paul was given permission to speak.

Paul began, "I think myself fortunate that it is before you, King Agrippa, I am going to make my defense today." The word for making a defense comes from the same family as the word you met in Lesson 2, apologia. And notice how Paul made his defense: by telling his story.

He told it in three parts: his life before Christ, how he met Jesus on the road to Damascus, and how his life has changed since. We will come back to that simple shape in a moment.

7 · The short answer

So, what should you say if someone asks why you follow Jesus? Here is the short answer.

Tell what the Lord has done for you. Use a simple shape if it helps. Tell the truth, without exaggerating your past or your present. And let Jesus, not you, be the hero of the story.

That gives us four parts: What He Has Done; Before, Meeting, Since; Honest, Not Exaggerated; and Point to Jesus.

8 · Part 1: What He Has Done

The first part: What He Has Done.

Jesus said to the freed man, "tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how he has had mercy on you." A testimony is simply that: telling what Jesus has done.

A man healed of blindness was questioned by religious leaders who wanted him to criticise Jesus. He could not answer all their theological questions. But he said, "One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see." And the psalmist says, "Come and hear, all you who fear God, and I will tell what he has done for my soul."

You do not need to be a theologian to tell your own story. The apostles proclaimed what they had uniquely seen and heard of Christ. Our testimony rests on their witness, and tells how the same Jesus has met us.

9 · Part 2: Before, Meeting, Since

The second part: Before, Meeting, Since.

When Paul spoke before Agrippa, he told his story in three parts: before, his life before Christ; meeting, how he met Jesus; and since, how his life has changed.

This simple shape helps many people. But not everyone has a dramatic turning point. If you grew up in a Christian home, or came to faith gradually, another shape may fit better: how faith began, how it became my own, and what Jesus means to me now.

Timothy had known the Scriptures from childhood. Paul wrote of "your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well." Quiet stories are real stories.

These are helpful shapes, not required formulas. You may combine them, leave parts out, or tell only the part that fits the question you were asked.

10 · Part 3: Honest, Not Exaggerated

The third part: Honest, Not Exaggerated.

Paul says, "Having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth." A testimony must be truthful. That means not making the past sound worse than it was, not claiming that life is now perfect, and not promising others the same outcomes.

Honesty includes ongoing struggles and unanswered questions. You may say, "I still struggle with worry, but Jesus has been patient with me."

Paul called himself the foremost of sinners, but his focus was on God's mercy: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost. But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience." That is not an invitation to compete over whose past was worst. Paul did not dwell on sensational details, and our stories should never glorify sin or describe it in graphic detail.

And you never have to share what is painful or private, or anything private about someone else. You can tell a true story without the details: "I went through a very hard time, and Jesus met me there."

11 · Part 4: Point to Jesus

The fourth part: Point to Jesus.

Paul wrote, "For what we proclaim is not ourselves, but Jesus Christ as Lord, with ourselves as your servants for Jesus' sake." The hero of a testimony is Jesus, not the speaker.

A good story leaves listeners thinking about Jesus, not about the speaker's past or achievements. It ends simply, often with one sentence about what Jesus means to you now. Then it leaves the listener free to ask more, or to say nothing. "Let your speech always be gracious."

Here is a useful test. If someone remembers only one thing from your story, would it be something about Jesus?

12 · Christians differ

Christians differ on one question here: is a remembered moment of conversion expected?

Some Christians emphasise a clear moment of turning to Christ that a believer can usually remember. They often point to Paul on the Damascus road in Acts 9, and to the Philippian jailer in Acts 16, who asked, "What must I do to be saved?"

Others emphasise that many people come to faith gradually, or grow up trusting Christ from childhood, without a single remembered moment. They often point to Timothy's sincere faith, and to Lydia, whose heart the Lord opened as she listened.

Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that what matters most is present trust in Christ, not how dramatic one's story is or whether one remembers an exact date. Christians also differ over how often testimonies are shared in church services; you will find that under Go deeper on the lesson page.

13 · Rami's story

Let me tell you about Rami. His story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Rami is a university student in Amman, Jordan. He grew up in a Christian family and has always gone to church. When his youth leader asked him to share his testimony at a youth evening, he panicked. A friend had once spoken about leaving a gang, and the room had been silent with amazement. "My story is boring," Rami said. "I've always been a Christian."

His youth leader, Hanna, read Mark 5 and 2 Timothy 1 with him. "Jesus told that man to tell what the Lord had done for him," she said. "Timothy learned the Scriptures from childhood. Your story matters because it is true."

Together they used the shape: how faith began, how it became my own, and what Jesus means to me now. Rami remembered the year his father lost his job, when church members brought food and prayed with the family, and he first prayed honestly on his own. Before including that, he asked his father's permission, and his father agreed. Rami also admitted that he still has doubts some days, and that Jesus has been patient with him.

Rami spoke for three minutes, mostly about Jesus' faithfulness. Afterwards a younger student told him, "My story is like yours. I thought it didn't count." Rami now has a thirty-second version for when someone asks why he goes to church. Many believers think their stories are too ordinary, when honest, quiet stories often help others most.

14 · What a testimony is not

Here are six common mistakes about telling your story.

"A real testimony must be dramatic." No. Quiet and gradual stories are real stories.

"I must remember the exact date I became a Christian." No. What matters is present trust in Christ.

"I must share every painful detail." No. You never have to share what is private or traumatic.

"A good testimony shows how much better my life is now." No. Honest stories include ongoing struggles.

"Following Jesus will bring you what it brought me." No. We tell what God has done, without promising others the same outcome.

And "My story is about me." No. The hero is Jesus.

15 · This week

This week, your assignment is to prepare a short, honest story that points to Jesus.

First, read Mark 5:1 to 20 and Acts 26:1 to 29. Second, write one short line for each of the four parts, in your own words. You can use the book to help.

Third, if you are comfortable doing so, write your story: a short, honest account that takes about three minutes to tell. Use either shape: before, meeting, since; or how faith began, how it became my own, and what Jesus means to me now. End with one sentence about Jesus. Then write a thirty-second version. Leave out painful details you do not wish to share, and anything private about other people. If you are still exploring, you may write where you are now.

Fourth, if you wish, practise your short story with your mentor or a trusted believer, and listen to theirs. If someone asks about your faith this week, you may share a short part of your story and let them respond freely.

And fifth, follow the seven-day reading plan below this lesson. If you miss a day, simply begin again the next day. Your thirty-second story will help you later with the third part of My Disciple-Making Plan: a first conversation plan.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We tell what Jesus has done, using a simple shape, honestly and without exaggeration, so that people see him.

What he has done: a testimony is telling how the Lord has had mercy on you. Before, meeting, since: or how faith began, how it became my own, and what Jesus means to me now; quiet stories are real stories. Honest, not exaggerated: truthful about the past and the present, with no promises of the same outcome. And point to Jesus: he is the hero, so end simply, and leave the listener free.

17 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, thank you for your mercy to me. Thank you for every part of my story, the quiet parts and the hard parts, and for meeting me there. Help me to tell the truth about what you have done. Keep me from exaggerating, and from making the story about me. And may the people who hear it think of you. In Jesus' name. Amen.

For groups, or with a friend

Talk about it

  1. What makes a story about faith helpful or unhelpful to listen to?
  2. In Acts 26, how does Paul tell his story, and how does Agrippa respond?
  3. What helps Christians tell their stories honestly and humbly? Share your own story only if you wish, and without anything private about other people.

Pause and seek help

You never have to share painful, private or traumatic parts of your story. If remembering or writing about your past brings up overwhelming feelings, stop, and consider talking with a trusted person or qualified counsellor (Grow, Lesson 4). Ask permission before sharing anything private about someone else; if someone harmed you, you may speak of God's help without naming them, and safeguarding concerns go through proper routes, not testimonies. Agreeing to speak in church does not mean agreeing to recording or online posting, and you may say no if anyone asks you to make your story more dramatic or changes it in ways that are not true (Support Page: "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority").

Optional

Go deeper

Public testimonies in church. Many churches regularly invite believers to share testimonies in services, to give God glory and encourage others (they often point to Psalm 66:16). Others share testimonies less often, preferring to keep the focus on the preaching of Scripture and to avoid drawing attention to individuals (they often point to 2 Corinthians 4:5 and Proverbs 27:2). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that telling what God has done can honour him, and that testimonies should be truthful, humble and freely given.

Is a remembered moment of conversion expected? The full Christians differ box from the book, with the passages each view uses (summarised on Slide 12). See also Begin, Lessons 3 and 5.

Stories of healing or provision. Be careful and honest. Do not claim that a medical condition has been cured without proper confirmation, do not stop treatment because of a testimony, and do not suggest that others will receive the same outcome if they have enough faith (Establish, Lesson 6; Grow, Lesson 11).

Where following Jesus is dangerous. Think carefully before telling your story to new people, and never record or post it online without considering the risks to you and others. Stories shared online can be permanent. Permission should be freely given for each use, and you may withdraw it before publication wherever possible.

If you are still exploring. You may not yet have a story of faith, and that is fine. You are welcome to think about where you are now in your journey, and what questions you still have. No one will ask you to tell a conversion story or to say publicly whether you have become a Christian.
