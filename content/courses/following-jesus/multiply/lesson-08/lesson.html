Reading Scripture and Praying With Someone

I'd like to read the Bible with a friend, but I'm not a teacher. How do I lead a conversation without preaching at them? And how do we pray together without it feeling awkward?

About 15 minutesLuke 24:13–35 and Acts 16:13–15Look back: Lessons 3 to 7

In this lesson

You will learn

  • How Jesus walked, asked, listened and opened the Scriptures on the road to Emmaus (Luke 24)
  • How the Lord opened Lydia's heart through a simple conversation by the river (Acts 16)
  • The four parts: Walk Alongside, Open the Scriptures Together, Ask, Don't Lecture, Pray Simply Together
  • How to lead a short Bible conversation and pray together, always inviting and never requiring
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · I'm not a teacher

Welcome to Lesson 8. In Establish, you learned four questions for reading Scripture: What It Says, What It Meant, Where It Points, and How We Respond. In Begin, you learned to pray using Praise, Surrender, Ask, Forgive and Protect, and in Establish, to pray together. This lesson brings those together, so that you can lead a short, simple Bible conversation, and pray with someone in a way that is gentle, invitational and safe.

Many believers would love to read the Bible with a friend, a new believer or a family member, but they hold back. They are not teachers. They worry they will be asked something they cannot answer. And praying aloud with someone else can feel awkward.

So here is our question: I'd like to read the Bible with a friend, but I'm not a teacher. How do I lead a conversation without preaching at them? And how do we pray together without it feeling awkward?

2 · The road to Emmaus

Our first passage is Luke chapter 24. Two disciples were walking away from Jerusalem, grieving and confused after Jesus' death. "Jesus himself drew near and went with them", though they did not recognise him.

He asked them questions and listened. He also corrected them firmly: "O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken!" Listening does not mean avoiding truth. Then, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself."

As he broke bread with them, "their eyes were opened, and they recognized him." They said, "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?" And they went straight back to Jerusalem to share the news.

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Christ opens the Scriptures to confused disciples, and shows how they point to him.

3 · By the river

Our second passage is Acts chapter 16. In Philippi, Paul and his companions went to a place of prayer by the river, and "sat down and spoke to the women who had come together."

Then Luke says: "One who heard us was a woman named Lydia, from the city of Thyatira, a seller of purple goods, who was a worshiper of God. The Lord opened her heart to pay attention to what was said by Paul."

Notice the ordinary setting, a conversation rather than a performance, and God's initiative in opening hearts.

4 · Opened = dianoigō

Our Greek word today is the one translated "opened" in Luke 24:32: dianoigō. It means to open.

Luke uses this word again and again in these passages. On the road to Emmaus, the disciples' eyes were opened. Jesus opened the Scriptures to them. A little later, he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures. And in Philippi, "the Lord opened her heart."

So the word ties our two passages together. Jesus opens the Scriptures, and the Lord opens Lydia's heart. Our part is simpler: we open the Bible, we ask good questions, and we trust God to work through his word.

5 · Walking away

Now the background. This happened on the evening of the resurrection. Two disciples were walking about seven miles from Jerusalem to a village called Emmaus. They had heard that the tomb was empty, but they did not understand.

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? Jesus began with a question: "What is this conversation that you are holding with each other as you walk?" He listened to their disappointment before he explained the Scriptures.

The passage shows Christ opening the Scriptures and showing how they point to him. His way of walking, questioning, listening and explaining also gives us qualities to imitate. It is not a fixed sequence that every conversation must follow.

6 · A place of prayer

Philippi was a Roman colony in Macedonia. On the Sabbath, Paul and his companions went outside the gate to the riverside, where they thought there was a place of prayer.

Lydia came from Thyatira, in Asia. She was a seller of purple goods, which were valuable, and she was a worshipper of God. She was already praying when Paul arrived. The Lord opened her heart through a simple conversation.

Afterwards Lydia and her household were baptised, and she offered hospitality to Paul and his companions.

7 · The short answer

So, how do we read the Bible and pray with someone? Here is the short answer.

Walk alongside the person, listening first. Open the Bible together and look for Jesus. Ask simple questions rather than giving lectures. And pray together simply, always inviting and never requiring.

That gives us four parts: Walk Alongside, Open the Scriptures Together, Ask, Don't Lecture, and Pray Simply Together.

8 · Part 1: Walk Alongside

The first part: Walk Alongside.

On the road to Emmaus, Jesus first drew the disciples into conversation with a question: "What is this conversation that you are holding with each other as you walk?" He listened to their disappointment before explaining the Scriptures.

Reading Scripture with someone begins with relationship. Notice how they are. Hear their questions. Meet them where they are. A few minutes of genuine conversation before opening the Bible is not wasted time. It is part of the care.

9 · Part 2: Open the Scriptures Together

The second part: Open the Scriptures Together.

Jesus "interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself." We are not Jesus, but we can open the Bible together and look for him.

Keep it simple. Choose a short passage, often from a Gospel. Mark is a good place to start. Read it aloud, or listen to it, perhaps twice. And let the text lead the conversation.

When God's law was read in Nehemiah's day, the Levites "gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading." And like the Bereans, who examined the Scriptures daily, read with eagerness, and check what is said against Scripture.

A simple prayer before reading can help: "Open my eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of your law."

10 · Part 3: Ask, Don't Lecture

The third part: Ask, Don't Lecture.

Jesus taught through questions as well as explanation. Simple questions help people discover truth for themselves, and keep the conversation two-way.

The four questions from Establish work well: What does it say? What did it mean in its original setting? Where does it point? And how do we respond? Other helpful questions include: "What stands out to you?", "What do we learn about God or Jesus?" and "What is one thing you want to remember or do?"

It is fine to say, "I'm not sure. Let's look at it more, or ask someone." If you do not know the background or meaning, do not guess. Read the surrounding verses, use trustworthy study notes, or ask a mature Christian. And do not turn every object, number or character into a hidden picture of Jesus.

Christians differ over how to read Scripture together: some use structured questions like these, and others prefer slower, more reflective reading. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that Scripture should be read in context, centred on Christ and applied humbly. Keep it short: usually twenty to forty minutes is enough.

11 · Part 4: Pray Simply Together

The fourth part: Pray Simply Together.

The early believers "lifted their voices together to God", and James urges believers to "pray for one another". Praying together can be very simple: a sentence or two each, silent prayer, or a short written prayer such as the Lord's Prayer. Jesus warned against heaping up "empty phrases". And when we do not know what to pray, "the Spirit helps us in our weakness."

Always invite; never require prayer aloud. You might ask, "Would you like to pray, or shall I pray briefly for us?"

Before mentioning someone's health, family, finances or private circumstances in prayer, ask what they are comfortable including. And always ask before any touch.

Christians differ over praying aloud, silently or in tongues together. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that praying together should be understandable and considerate, never pressured, and that no style of prayer is more spiritual than another.

Lydia's story reminds us that it is God who opens hearts through his word, often quietly. Our part is simple and faithful.

12 · Isaac's story

Let me tell you about Isaac. His story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Isaac is a taxi driver in Kampala, Uganda. His friend Moses, a mechanic, had started coming to church, but said he found the Bible confusing. Isaac offered to read with him on Saturday mornings at a roadside café near Moses's workshop. Isaac felt unqualified. He had never led a Bible study.

His small group leader suggested he use the four questions from Establish, and start with Mark's Gospel. On the first Saturday they read Mark 2, about the paralysed man lowered through the roof. Isaac asked, "What stands out to you?" Moses said, "His friends didn't give up." They talked about what Jesus did and said, and what it shows about him.

When Moses asked a question about forgiveness that Isaac could not answer, Isaac said, "Good question. Let's ask Pastor Okello, or look it up in a trustworthy Bible resource, and come back to it."

At the end, Isaac asked, "Would you like to pray, or shall I pray briefly for us?" Moses preferred Isaac to pray, so Isaac prayed two sentences, thanking God for Jesus and asking for help for Moses's workshop, which Moses had mentioned.

They still meet most Saturdays, sometimes with another friend. Isaac has learned that he does not need to be a teacher. He needs to be a friend who opens the Bible and asks good questions.

13 · What this is not

Here are six common mistakes about reading Scripture and praying with someone.

"Only trained teachers can read the Bible with others." No. A friend who opens the Bible and asks good questions is enough.

"I must have every answer." No. "I'm not sure; let's find out" is honest.

"Bible reading should be a lecture." No. Questions keep it a conversation.

"Every detail of a passage is a hidden picture of Jesus." No. Read in context, and see how it fits the larger story.

"Real prayer is long and aloud." No. Short, simple and silent prayers are real prayers.

And "Bible reading and prayer replace professional help." No. They go alongside it when it is needed.

15 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We walk alongside, open the Scriptures together, ask rather than lecture, and pray simply together.

Walk alongside: relationship first, and listen before opening the Bible. Open the Scriptures together: a short passage, read aloud, looking for Jesus, and checking everything by Scripture. Ask, don't lecture: simple questions, no guessing, and "I'm not sure" is fine. And pray simply together: invite, never require, and ask before mentioning private matters or any touch.

16 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you walked alongside your disciples and opened the Scriptures to them. Open my eyes to see you in your word. Make me a good listener and a simple friend. Teach us to pray together, freely and gladly. You open hearts; help me to be faithful. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Some passages can stir up painful memories, for example passages about violence, abuse, family, illness or judgement. Choose passages with care, especially with new believers, and let the other person pause or change the passage. If someone becomes distressed, stop and listen; offer support and, where needed, help them find qualified help (Grow, Lesson 4). Prayer together must never become pressure or control. Warning signs include insisting that someone pray aloud, praying "at" someone to correct them, using prayer to announce what God wants for their life, laying hands on them without permission, or making them feel spiritually inferior because of how they pray. If this happens to you, you may say no. Meet in safe and appropriate settings, such as public places, homes with others present, or online with agreed boundaries, and follow your church's safeguarding procedures, especially with children, young people or vulnerable adults (Lesson 9). Where reading the Bible together is dangerous or restricted, seek experienced local advice and protect the other person's privacy (Support Page: "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority").

Optional

Go deeper

Ways of reading Scripture together. Some Christians use structured questions, such as the four from Establish, so that anyone can lead (they often point to Nehemiah 8:8 and Acts 17:11). Others prefer slower, more reflective reading, listening for what God may be saying through a passage read several times (they often point to Psalm 119:18 and Colossians 3:16). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that Scripture should be read in context, centred on Christ and applied humbly, and that any personal impression must be tested by Scripture (Grow, Lesson 10).

Praying aloud, silently or in tongues together. Some Christians are comfortable praying aloud together, and some may pray in tongues (they often point to Acts 4:24 and 1 Corinthians 14:15). Others prefer quieter or written prayers, especially with new believers or people of other backgrounds (they often point to Matthew 6:5–8 and 1 Corinthians 14:16, 23). (See also Begin, Lesson 7 and Establish, Lesson 6.) In shared prayer, no one should dominate, overwhelm or exclude another, and Paul's teaching about interpretation and order in 1 Corinthians 14 should be followed. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that praying together should be understandable and considerate, never pressured, and that no style of prayer is more spiritual than another.

Prayer and privacy. Permission to pray is not automatically permission to share details with others, and it is never permission to lay hands on someone; always ask before any touch.
