Explaining the Gospel Clearly

If someone asked me what Christianity is really about, I'm not sure I could explain it clearly. What is the gospel, and how do I share it without sounding scripted or pushy?

About 18 minutesRomans 10:9–17 and Acts 8:26–40Getting ready: A first conversation plan

In this lesson

You will learn

  • Why people need to hear the message, and why faith comes through the word of Christ (Romans 10:9–17)
  • How Philip listened first and began with the Scripture in front of him (Acts 8:26–40)
  • The four parts: Start Where They Are, Tell the Good News, Invite a Response, Leave Them Free

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · What is it really about?

Welcome to Lesson 4. In Lesson 3, you learned to tell your own story. A testimony says, "This is what Jesus has done for me." The gospel says, "This is what God has done in Jesus Christ for sinners."

In Begin, you met the good news in four parts. And in Establish, you saw that Jesus came, lived, died, rose and reigns. Now we are going one step further: explaining that good news simply and accurately, in your own words.

Many believers love Jesus but feel unsure how to explain the heart of the Christian message. Some worry that they will get it wrong. Others have seen gospel presentations that felt scripted, pushy or disrespectful, and they do not want to repeat them.

So here is our question: If someone asked me what Christianity is really about, I'm not sure I could explain it clearly. What is the gospel, and how do I share it without sounding scripted or pushy?

2 · How will they hear?

Our first passage is Romans chapter 10: "If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved… How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching?… So faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ."

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Notice the chain of questions. People cannot call on someone they have not believed in, and they cannot believe in someone they have never heard of. And notice where faith comes from: through the word of Christ.

3 · Beginning with this Scripture

Our second passage is Acts chapter 8. The Spirit led Philip to a single traveller, an official from Ethiopia, who was reading the prophet Isaiah in his chariot. Philip heard him reading and began with a question: "Do you understand what you are reading?" The man replied, "How can I, unless someone guides me?" and he invited Philip to sit with him.

Then Luke says: "Then Philip opened his mouth, and beginning with this Scripture he told him the good news about Jesus."

The official then asked his own question: "See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" And he went on his way rejoicing. Philip did not create the interest, and he did not pressure the response. This is an example, not a formula for every conversation.

4 · Told him the good news = euangelizō

Our Greek word today is the one translated "told him the good news" in Acts 8:35: euangelizō. It means to announce good news. Paul uses the same word in our first passage, when he quotes Isaiah: "How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the good news!"

Think of a messenger running to a city with news of a victory. The messenger does not invent the news, or argue people into it, or sell it. The messenger announces what has happened.

So when we share the gospel, we are not selling something. We are announcing what God has done in Jesus Christ.

5 · A letter to Rome

Now the background. Paul wrote to the church in Rome before he had ever visited it. A central concern of the letter is that salvation through Christ is for everyone, Jew and Gentile alike. Just before our verses, he says, "there is no distinction between Jew and Greek… everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved."

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? If the message is for everyone, then messengers matter. That is why Paul quotes Isaiah 52, where a messenger runs across the mountains to announce that God reigns.

Paul is describing how God works through the message. He is not giving a technique that guarantees results.

6 · On the desert road

Our second passage takes place on the desert road that went down from Jerusalem towards Gaza. The traveller was a court official of the queen of the Ethiopians, in charge of all her treasure. He had come all the way to Jerusalem to worship, and now he was going home.

Reading aloud was common in the ancient world, and Luke tells us that Philip heard the official reading Isaiah. It was Isaiah 53: "Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter and like a lamb before its shearer is silent, so he opens not his mouth." The man asked who the prophet was speaking about.

So Philip did not begin with a prepared speech. He began with the man's own question, and with the Scripture already in front of him, and from there he told him about Jesus.

7 · The short answer

So, what is the gospel, and how do we share it without being scripted or pushy? Here is the short answer.

Start by listening to where the person is. Tell the good news of who Jesus is and what he has done. Invite a response simply and without pressure. And leave the person free, trusting the Holy Spirit to work.

That gives us four parts: Start Where They Are, Tell the Good News, Invite a Response, and Leave Them Free.

8 · Part 1: Start Where They Are

The first part: Start Where They Are.

Philip paid attention to what was already in front of the man, rather than beginning with a prepared speech. Paul, speaking in Athens, began with an altar the people already knew: "I found also an altar with this inscription: 'To the unknown god.'"

Explaining the gospel begins with listening: understanding the person's questions, background and words. You might ask, "What do you already believe about Jesus?" or "What do you think Christianity is about?"

Avoid church jargon. Words such as "saved", "sanctified" or "washed in the blood" may mean nothing to a listener, or something different from what you intend. Use everyday words, and explain any biblical word you use.

Respect the person's culture and beliefs, even when you disagree. And do not assume from someone's church background, or lack of one, whether they trust Christ. Listen to what the person actually believes.

9 · Part 2: Tell the Good News

The second part: Tell the Good News.

The gospel is the good news about Jesus. A simple way to explain it is the four parts from Begin.

Who Jesus is: the promised King, fully God and fully human. What Jesus announced: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel." What Jesus did: "Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures… he was buried… he was raised on the third day." His resurrection shows who he is and his victory over death.

And why this is good news, and how we respond. We have all turned away from God, and we cannot rescue ourselves. In love, God offers forgiveness, reconciliation and new life as a gift through Jesus. "By grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God." We respond by turning to God, trusting Jesus, confessing him as Lord, and beginning to follow him. These responses do not earn salvation; they receive what Christ has done.

Keep Jesus at the centre, as Paul did: "Jesus Christ and him crucified." Be honest about sin and about grace. Never promise that following Jesus brings wealth, health or an easy life. And you do not have to say everything in one conversation. Often a few clear sentences, given in answer to a real question, are enough.

10 · Part 3: Invite a Response

The third part: Invite a Response.

The gospel calls for a response. "If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved." Jesus called people to "repent and believe", and the New Testament connects this with baptism and joining God's people.

An invitation can be simple: "Would you like to follow Jesus?" "Would you like to read one of the Gospels with me?" Or, "What questions or concerns do you still have about trusting Jesus?"

Choose an invitation that fits where the person is. For many people, the next step is not a decision today, but reading about Jesus for themselves. And remember: salvation rests on Christ, not on saying a prayer perfectly.

11 · Part 4: Leave Them Free

The fourth part: Leave Them Free.

"Faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ." And Jesus promised that the Holy Spirit would "convict the world concerning sin and righteousness and judgment." So Christian witness depends on the Spirit's work, not on our ability to pressure someone into a response. Our part is to explain faithfully and kindly. God gives the growth.

People must be free to say yes, no or "not yet", and to keep asking questions. A "no" does not end a friendship. Do not raise the invitation again unless the person later returns to the subject or clearly shows renewed interest.

And a "yes" is the beginning of a journey, not the end of our care. If someone says they want to follow Jesus, do not leave them with only a prayer or a card. With their agreement, offer to keep meeting, help them connect with a healthy church where possible, and do not announce their decision to others without their permission. You will learn more about this in Lesson 7.

12 · Christians differ

Christians differ on one question here: should people be invited to pray a prayer of commitment, or to respond publicly?

Many churches invite people to pray a prayer of commitment, or to respond publicly at the end of a service. They often point to Romans 10:9 to 13, and to Peter's call on the day of Pentecost in Acts 2.

Others emphasise repentance, faith, baptism and joining the church, without a set prayer or a public invitation. They often point to Acts 2:38, and to the Ethiopian official's baptism in Acts 8.

A prayer can express genuine repentance and faith, but the prayer itself does not save. So leaders should neither give false assurance because someone repeated certain words, nor withhold assurance because someone cannot remember praying a set prayer.

Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that salvation is by grace through faith in Christ, and that no response should be pressured or treated as a formula. Christians also differ over whether to learn a prepared outline or to explain the gospel conversationally; you will find that under Go deeper.

13 · Giulia's story

Let me tell you about Giulia. Her story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Giulia is a hairdresser in Naples, Italy. One of her regular clients, Paolo, grew up going to church at Christmas and Easter but rarely thinks about faith. One afternoon he noticed that Giulia seemed calmer than she used to be. "You said something last month about Jesus changing your life," he said. "What do you mean?"

Giulia had practised a short explanation with her small group. First she asked, "What do you already believe about Jesus?" Paolo said he knew the Christmas and Easter stories but had never understood why they mattered.

In a few minutes, in everyday words, Giulia explained who Jesus is, that he announced God's kingdom, that he died for our sins and rose again, and that God offers forgiveness and new life as a gift, not a reward. "For me," she said, "this became real when I stopped trying to earn God's love and simply trusted Jesus." Then she asked, "Would you be interested in reading one of the Gospels with me sometime? No pressure at all." Paolo said he would think about it. "That's completely fine," Giulia said, and went back to talking about his holiday.

A month later Paolo asked whether they could read Mark's Gospel over coffee once a month, and they have begun. Giulia has never pressed him for a decision, and she speaks respectfully of the church he grew up in. Whether or not another conversation follows, Giulia has explained the good news clearly and left Paolo free.

14 · What the gospel is not

Here are six common mistakes about explaining the gospel.

"The gospel is a way to improve your life or get rich." No. It is the good news of what God has done in Christ.

"I must say everything in one conversation." No. A few clear sentences in answer to a real question are often enough.

"Saying a particular prayer guarantees salvation." No. Salvation rests on Christ, received by faith.

"People must decide immediately." No. People are free to say yes, no or not yet.

"If they say no, I should keep pressing." No. A no does not end friendship, and we do not keep repeating the invitation.

And "I can be disrespectful about other beliefs to make the gospel clear." No. We explain the gospel with gentleness and respect.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We start where people are, tell the good news clearly, invite a response, and leave people free.

Start where they are: listen first, use everyday words, and respect the person. Tell the good news: who Jesus is, what Jesus announced, what Jesus did, and why this is good news and how we respond. Invite a response: a simple invitation that fits where the person is, remembering that salvation rests on Christ, not on a perfect prayer. And leave them free: the Spirit convicts; yes, no and not yet are all respected; and a yes begins a journey.

Pause and seek help

The gospel is urgent and good, but it must never be shared through pressure: fear used to force decisions, deadlines, repeated invitations after a no, emotional manipulation, or help, gifts or opportunities offered in return for a response. Take special care with people in crisis, grief, illness, hospital, prison or extreme need; offer comfort and care first, and speak about faith only if they wish. Where someone depends on you for work, grades, housing, healthcare, money or essential help, do not start a conversation about faith within that relationship (Lesson 1). Never ask a child to keep conversations, prayer or baptism secret from parents or guardians; if involving the family might put a child at risk, seek safeguarding advice at once (Support Page: "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority").

Optional

Go deeper

Prepared outlines and conversational approaches. Some Christians find it helpful to learn a short gospel outline or illustration so that they can explain the message clearly (they often point to 1 Corinthians 15:1–4 and 2 Timothy 4:2). Others prefer a conversational approach that starts with the person's questions and adapts the explanation each time (they often point to Acts 8:30–35 and Acts 17:22–31). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that the message must centre on Christ crucified and risen, explained clearly, honestly and lovingly.
