Responding to Questions With Truth and Humility

People ask me hard questions: Why does God allow suffering? Aren't all religions the same? How can you trust the Bible? I freeze or get defensive. How should I respond?

About 17 minutesColossians 4:5–6 and Acts 17:16–34Look back: Lessons 2 and 4

In this lesson

You will learn

  • How gracious speech answers each person, not a script (Colossians 4:5–6)
  • How Paul listened, reasoned and spoke clearly in Athens, and left the response to his hearers (Acts 17)
  • The four parts: Listen to the Question, Answer With Grace, Honest About Limits, Point Back to Jesus
  • How to say "I don't know" honestly, care for people in pain, and invite them to look at Jesus
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Hard questions

Welcome to Lesson 5. In Lesson 2, you learned to be ready with reasons, and in Lesson 4, to start where people are. In Grow, you learned to be quick to listen, and to doubt honestly. Now we are going one step further, and bringing those together.

When people learn that we follow Jesus, they often ask questions. Some are curious. Some are testing us. Some are carrying deep pain. And some questions are hard: about suffering, other religions, the Bible, or the church's failures. It is easy to freeze, to get defensive, or to try to win an argument.

So here is our question: People ask me hard questions. Why does God allow suffering? Aren't all religions the same? How can you trust the Bible? I freeze or get defensive. How should I respond?

2 · Gracious speech

Our first passage is Colossians chapter 4, verses 5 and 6: "Walk in wisdom toward outsiders, making the best use of the time. Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how you ought to answer each person."

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Gracious speech, seasoned with salt, is wise, fitting and helpful: truthful without being needlessly harsh. And notice the words "each person". An answer should respond to the individual in front of us, rather than repeat a script.

3 · Paul in Athens

Our second passage is Acts chapter 17, when Paul was in Athens. Luke says Paul's "spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols." Yet he did not shout or mock. He reasoned in the synagogue, and in the marketplace every day with whoever was there.

When he spoke at the Areopagus, the city's council, he took his hearers seriously. He began with an altar they already knew, "To the unknown god", and he quoted their own poets: "as even some of your own poets have said, 'For we are indeed his offspring.'" Then he spoke clearly about God the Creator, repentance, judgement and Jesus' resurrection.

The responses varied. "Some mocked. But others said, 'We will hear you again about this.'" And some believed. Respect did not require agreement, or avoiding difficult truth. And Paul left the response to them.

4 · Reasoned = dialegomai

Our Greek word today is the one translated "reasoned" in Acts 17:17: dialegomai. It can mean to reason or discuss. Luke uses it for what Paul did in the synagogue in Thessalonica too: "he reasoned with them from the Scriptures."

The same word can also describe someone speaking at length. So we learn the give-and-take from the setting. In Athens, Paul reasoned "in the marketplace every day with those who happened to be there", and some of the philosophers "conversed with him". That was a real conversation, with questions heard and answered.

So Paul did not avoid people's questions, and he did not simply preach at them. He reasoned with them.

5 · Written in chains

Now the background. Paul wrote Colossians while he was in prison. Just before our verses, he asks the church to pray "that God may open to us a door for the word", and he ends the letter, "Remember my chains."

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? The "outsiders" were the neighbours, colleagues and officials around the young church who did not yet follow Christ. Paul cared about how believers spoke to them.

And "seasoned with salt" recalls the salt you met in Lesson 2. Our words, like our lives, should be distinct, wholesome and good to receive.

6 · The city of philosophers

Our second passage takes place in Athens, a famous centre of learning, full of temples and statues to many gods. Paul talked with people in the synagogue, and every day in the marketplace, the busy public square where people met, traded and debated.

There he met philosophers from two well-known schools, the Epicureans and the Stoics. Some dismissed him: "What does this babbler wish to say?" Others thought he was introducing foreign gods, because he was preaching Jesus and the resurrection. So they brought him to the Areopagus, the city's council, to explain.

Paul took his hearers seriously. He used sources they recognised, and then he clearly challenged their understanding of God.

7 · The short answer

So, how should we respond to hard questions? Here is the short answer.

Listen to the question before you answer. Answer truthfully and graciously. Be honest about the limits of what you know. And point people back to Jesus, inviting them to look at him for themselves.

That gives us four parts: Listen to the Question, Answer With Grace, Honest About Limits, and Point Back to Jesus.

8 · Part 1: Listen to the Question

The first part: Listen to the Question.

James tells believers to be "quick to hear, slow to speak", and that humble listening should shape how we respond to people's questions. Proverbs says it directly: "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame."

Before answering, try to understand what the person is really asking, and why. Some questions are intellectual: "How do we know the Bible is reliable?" Others come from grief, injustice or harm by Christians: "Why did God let my brother die?" or "Why should I trust a church after what happened to me?" The same words can carry very different questions.

A helpful first response is often another question: "What makes you ask?" "What have you heard about that?" or "Can you tell me more about what you believe?" Listening shows respect. And it helps you answer the question the person is actually asking, rather than the one you expected.

9 · Part 2: Answer With Grace

The second part: Answer With Grace.

Paul says, "Let your speech always be gracious." And the Lord's servant must be "kind to everyone… correcting his opponents with gentleness."

Believers can give real reasons for their hope. For example, the testimony of eyewitnesses to real events: Luke says he carefully investigated what was handed down by those who were "eyewitnesses", and Paul lists appearances of the risen Jesus to many witnesses, "most of whom are still alive" when he wrote. Paul even says that if Christ has not been raised, Christian faith is empty. You can also speak of the trustworthiness of Scripture, which you learned about in Establish, and of the honest way the Bible speaks about suffering, through lament and the hope of resurrection.

Answers should be truthful, humble and kind. Winning an argument is not the goal; helping the person see Jesus more clearly is. "A soft answer turns away wrath." When you speak about other religions or beliefs, describe them in a way their own followers would recognise as fair. Do not ask one person to defend everything their whole tradition believes. Ask what they believe, and let them correct your understanding.

Christians differ over how much to rely on evidence and argument, and over how to describe other religions: some emphasise the differences, and others also emphasise points of contact. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that salvation is found in Christ, that the Holy Spirit is the one who convicts, and that believers should speak truthfully about Christ and respectfully about people of other faiths.

10 · Part 3: Honest About Limits

The third part: Honest About Limits.

No one has every answer. It is honest and wise to say: "I don't know, but I'd like to find out." "Christians think about that in different ways." Or, "I don't know, and I may not be able to give a complete answer." Only promise to find out if you mean to come back to the conversation.

Some questions, such as why God allowed a particular tragedy, have no complete answer this side of eternity. Admitting limits is honest and respectful. And when a question comes from deep pain, listening, compassion and practical care matter more than arguments.

If a question touches your own unresolved doubts or pain, you may pause the conversation. You do not need to defend an answer you do not yet understand, or pretend to be certain. With the person's agreement, you might recommend a good resource, or invite a wiser believer into the conversation.

Questions are not the enemy of faith. Scripture says, "have mercy on those who doubt." And no one has to answer every question before they can trust Jesus.

11 · Part 4: Point Back to Jesus

The fourth part: Point Back to Jesus.

Many questions eventually lead back to the central one: who is Jesus? When Nathanael doubted, asking, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?", Philip simply said, "Come and see." When Thomas doubted the resurrection, Jesus met him with evidence and grace: "Put your finger here, and see my hands… Do not disbelieve, but believe." And Thomas answered, "My Lord and my God!"

Christians believe that Jesus is "the way, and the truth, and the life", and that "there is salvation in no one else." We can say this with humility and respect for others.

A conversation may end with an invitation to look at Jesus directly, for example by reading one of the Gospels together, as you saw in Lesson 4.

12 · Baraka's story

Let me tell you about Baraka. His story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Baraka is a pharmacy student in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. His closest friend at university, Hamisi, is a devout Muslim. One evening Hamisi asked, "How can Christians say that God has a Son? We honour Jesus as the Messiah and a prophet, but we do not believe God has a Son or that Jesus was crucified. Why do Christians believe those things?" Baraka felt nervous and started to argue, then stopped himself.

He remembered Proverbs 18:13, and said, "That's an important question. Can you tell me more about what you believe about Jesus?" Hamisi explained, in his own words. Baraka listened carefully and thanked him.

Then he explained gently that Christians do not mean that God produced a child physically. "Son" describes the eternal relationship of the Son with the Father; Jesus is the eternal Word who shares God's nature and became truly human. Baraka also explained that Christians believe Jesus willingly gave himself for our sins, and that God raised him from the dead. So the cross is not God failing to protect Jesus, but his saving love and his victory over sin and death.

When Hamisi asked how God can be Father, Son and Holy Spirit, Baraka could not answer well. He said honestly, "I'm not sure how best to explain that. Can I think about it, talk with my pastor, and come back to you?" Hamisi smiled and agreed. Baraka asked his pastor, read Establish, Lesson 2 again, and came back with a clearer explanation.

Hamisi still disagrees, but the two friends respect each other, and they sometimes read passages from the Gospels together. Baraka has learned to describe Hamisi's faith in ways Hamisi would recognise as fair. Baraka has learned that he can speak clearly about Jesus while listening honestly and treating Hamisi with respect.

13 · What answering well is not

Here are six common mistakes about answering questions.

"The goal is to win the argument." No. The goal is to help the person see Jesus more clearly.

"I must have an answer to every question." No. "I don't know" is honest and respectful.

"People in pain need my explanation." No. They first need listening, compassion and care.

"Respect means never saying anything difficult." No. Paul was respectful, and he still spoke clearly.

"I can describe other religions however I like." No. Describe them as their own followers would recognise.

And "Questions and doubts are shameful." No. Questions are welcome, and doubt is not necessarily unbelief.

Let us bring it together. We listen to the question, answer with grace, are honest about our limits, and point back to Jesus.

Pause and seek help

Some questions come from deep pain: grief, illness, abuse, or harm caused by Christians or a church. In these moments, listening, compassion and practical care matter more than arguments; do not defend wrongdoing by Christians or churches, and if someone has been harmed, help them find appropriate support. If a conversation becomes hostile, threatening or unsafe, you may end it calmly, online or in person; ending an abusive exchange is not a failure to witness. Where speaking about religion, or changing religion, is restricted or dangerous, seek experienced local counsel. Hard questions can stir up your own doubts too; talk with a trusted believer or mentor (Grow, Lesson 11) (Support Page: "I am in danger or being abused").

Optional

Go deeper

How to use evidence and argument. Some Christians emphasise giving reasons and evidence, such as historical reasons for the resurrection or reasons for believing in God (they often point to Acts 17:2–3, Acts 26:25–26 and 1 Peter 3:15). Others emphasise that faith comes mainly through hearing the gospel and the Spirit's work, and are cautious about relying on argument (they often point to 1 Corinthians 2:1–5 and Romans 10:17). Many hold both together. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that reasons can help remove obstacles, that the Holy Spirit is the one who convicts, and that arguments must always be offered with gentleness and respect.

How to speak about other religions. Christians agree that salvation is found in Christ (John 14:6; Acts 4:12). They differ over how to describe other religions. Some emphasise the differences and errors they see (they often point to 1 John 5:20–21 and 1 Corinthians 8:4–6). Others also emphasise points of contact, truths that other traditions may recognise (they often point to Acts 14:17 and Acts 17:22–28). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that believers should speak truthfully about Christ and respectfully about people of other faiths, never mocking or misrepresenting them.

Online and hostile conversations. Online, you may mute, block or report someone who becomes abusive; if threats are made, save evidence where it is safe to do so and seek advice. You do not have to keep arguing.

If your doubts come after harm in a church. Take time, and seek safe people. Doubt is not necessarily the rejection of God (Grow, Lesson 11).
