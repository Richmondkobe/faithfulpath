Living as a Witness in Everyday Life

Most of my life is work, family and neighbours, not church events. Can ordinary life really point people to Jesus?

About 16 minutesMatthew 5:13–16 and 1 Peter 3:15–16Getting ready: A first conversation plan

In this lesson

You will learn

  • What it means to be salt and light, and why the aim is God's glory, not our reputation (Matthew 5:13–16)
  • How to give a brief, gentle answer when someone asks about your hope (1 Peter 3:15–16)
  • The four parts: Salt and Light, Faithful at Work and Online, Open Your Table, Ready With Reasons
  • How simple, safe hospitality and honest everyday work can point people to Christ
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Ordinary life

Welcome to Lesson 2. In Lesson 1, you saw that every disciple is sent, and that we are witnesses where we live, who love without pressure. In Grow, you learned to work as for the Lord. Now we are going one step further.

Most of us spend far more time at work, at home, with neighbours and online than at church events. It is easy to think that witness only happens at special outreach meetings. Yet the people who know us best see how we work, how we speak, how we treat others, and how we respond to pressure.

So here is our question: Most of my life is work, family and neighbours, not church events. Can ordinary life really point people to Jesus?

2 · Salt and light

Our first passage is from the Sermon on the Mount, Matthew chapter 5: "You are the salt of the earth… You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden… In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven."

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Notice that Jesus does not say, try to become salt and light. He says, you are. And notice where the light points: people see the good works, and they give glory to the Father.

3 · A reason for the hope

Our second passage is 1 Peter chapter 3, verses 15 and 16: "In your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect, having a good conscience."

Notice the order again. It begins in the heart, with Christ honoured as Lord. Then someone asks, and we answer. And the way we answer matters as much as what we say: with gentleness and respect.

4 · Defense = apologia

Our Greek word today is the one translated "defense" in 1 Peter 3:15: apologia. An apologia is a reasoned answer or defence. It was often used in a court setting, though not only there. Here in 1 Peter, it is an answer given when someone asks. Luke uses the same word when Paul stands on the steps in Jerusalem and says to the crowd, "hear the defense that I now make before you."

So an apologia does not mean saying sorry. And it is not an attack. It is a thoughtful, honest explanation of why we believe what we believe.

Notice too who starts the conversation. Peter says, "to anyone who asks you." The defense is an answer to a question. And it is given with gentleness and respect.

5 · On the hillside

Now the background. Salt and light come straight after the Beatitudes, where Jesus describes the people of his kingdom: the poor in spirit, the merciful, the peacemakers, the persecuted. So the good works he speaks of are not a show. They flow from that kind of humble, merciful character.

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? In an ordinary home of that time, one clay lamp lit the whole house. Jesus says, "Nor do people light a lamp and put it under a basket, but on a stand, and it gives light to all in the house." A lamp is not for admiring. It is for helping everyone see.

And a few verses later, Jesus warns, "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them." So Jesus is not telling his disciples to perform for an audience. He calls them to live openly as his people, so that God is honoured.

6 · Scattered and slandered

Our second passage comes from a letter Peter wrote to believers scattered across five provinces, in what is now Turkey. They faced suspicion and false accusations. Peter says people "speak against you as evildoers". Yet he expects their good conduct to raise questions, and even to put false accusations to shame.

That is why he says, be prepared. The questions often come because people have been watching how believers live.

Long before, God told Judah's exiles in Babylon to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you… and pray to the LORD on its behalf." Their situation was unique. But it gives us a helpful picture: God's people seeking the good of the places where they live, rather than withdrawing from them.

7 · The short answer

So, can ordinary life really point people to Jesus? Here is the short answer.

Be salt and light where you already live. Work and speak faithfully, at work and online. Open your table with simple, safe hospitality. And be ready to give a gentle reason for your hope when someone asks.

That gives us four parts: Salt and Light, Faithful at Work and Online, Open Your Table, and Ready With Reasons.

8 · Part 1: Salt and Light

The first part: Salt and Light.

"You are the salt of the earth." Salt was valuable in the ancient world, and it had many uses, including seasoning and preserving food. Whatever particular use Jesus had in mind, the picture calls his people to remain distinct. And the picture of light makes the result clear: their good works are seen, and people glorify God.

So Christians are called to be distinctive in kindness, honesty, generosity and hope. Paul says believers "shine as lights in the world" when they live without grumbling or disputing.

The aim is God's glory, not our own reputation. That is why Jesus warned against practising righteousness to be seen.

And you do not have to be perfect to bear witness. Honesty about your failures, and a willingness to apologise, can point to Christ as clearly as your successes.

9 · Part 2: Faithful at Work and Online

The second part: Faithful at Work and Online.

Paul urged believers "to live quietly, and to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands… so that you may walk properly before outsiders." As you learned in Grow, honest, careful and respectful work is part of our witness. So are being on time, being fair, keeping promises, and owning our mistakes.

So is the way we speak and post online. Paul told the Lord's servant not to be "quarrelsome but kind to everyone… correcting his opponents with gentleness." Paul was not writing about social media, but the same Christlike restraint should shape how we speak when disagreements move online. Before you post, it helps to ask: Is it true? Is it gracious? Is it necessary? Is it respectful? And do not share another person's story, photo, prayer request or private message without their permission.

At work, we respect reasonable workplace rules and our colleagues' freedom. Faithful witness never means neglecting our duties or pressuring people. And as you saw in Lesson 1, take particular care where you hold power over others. Christians differ over how visibly faith should be expressed in a particular workplace. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that colleagues' freedom must be respected, and that no one should use workplace power to promote their faith.

10 · Part 3: Open Your Table

The third part: Open Your Table.

Peter says, "Show hospitality to one another without grumbling." And Hebrews says, "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers."

Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners. When the religious leaders complained, he said, "I have not come to call the righteous but sinners to repentance." He told his followers to invite people who "cannot repay you." And Jesus also received hospitality from others.

Hospitality does not need a large home or expensive food. It can be a cup of tea, a shared meal, a welcome at the door, or remembering someone's name.

Hospitality should be safe, suitable for the culture, and genuine, not a strategy. Guests should never be expected to pray, join in worship or talk about religion in return. If you pray before a meal, explain it briefly, and let guests join, observe or decline without embarrassment.

11 · Part 4: Ready With Reasons

The fourth part: Ready With Reasons.

Everyday faithfulness may lead people to ask questions. "Why are you so calm?" "Why did you apologise?" "Why do you go to church?"

Peter says to be prepared. A brief, honest answer is often enough: what Jesus means to you, in a sentence or two. For example: "I follow Jesus. He has forgiven me and given me hope, and I'm trying to live his way." Then stop, and let the other person decide whether to ask more.

It is fine to say, "I don't know, but I'd like to find out," if you mean to come back to it. You will learn more about answering questions in Lesson 5. And Lessons 3 and 4 will help you tell your story and explain the gospel more fully.

12 · Jia-hao's story

Let me tell you about Jia-hao. His story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Jia-hao leads a small IT team in Taipei, Taiwan. He and his wife, Mei-lin, live in an apartment block where neighbours rarely speak. Jia-hao used to argue about politics and religion online, and once he blamed a team member for an error that was partly his own.

After studying Matthew 5 with his small group, he made some changes. He apologised to his team member and deleted several angry posts. And he decided not to raise faith with the people he supervises unless they raised it themselves.

Jia-hao and Mei-lin also began inviting neighbours for simple dumplings on the first Saturday of each month, with several neighbours present. An elderly widower, Mr Lin, started coming regularly. One evening he asked why they always paused before eating. Jia-hao said briefly, "We thank God, because we believe Jesus loves us and gives us everything we have. You're welcome to join or just listen." Mr Lin nodded and changed the subject.

At work, a colleague from another department, Wen, asked Jia-hao why he had apologised to his team member. Jia-hao explained that following Jesus had made him want to be honest about his mistakes, and left it there.

Mr Lin still comes to dinner and sometimes asks questions. Wen occasionally asks about church. Jia-hao and Mei-lin keep loving their neighbours whether or not anyone becomes a Christian. Everyday integrity and simple hospitality may open doors that organised programmes do not.

13 · What everyday witness is not

Here are six common mistakes about everyday witness.

"Kindness and hospitality are tactics for conversion." No. They are genuine love, whether or not faith is ever discussed.

"Good works are for being seen." No. The aim is God's glory, not our reputation.

"Witness at work means I must always talk about faith." No. Faithful work, honesty and respect come first.

"I must be perfect to bear witness." No. Honesty about failure can also point to Christ.

"Hospitality means opening my home to anyone." No. Hospitality should be safe, with wise limits.

And "I must have a full answer to every question." No. A brief, honest answer is often enough.

14 · This week

This week, your assignment is to let your ordinary life point to Christ.

First, read Matthew 5:1 to 16 and 1 Peter 3:13 to 17. Second, write one short line for each of the four parts, in your own words. You can use the book to help.

Third, write one or two sentences you could say if someone asked why you follow Jesus. Keep it short and honest, in your own words. You may never need to use it this week, and that is fine.

Fourth, if it is appropriate and safe, offer one simple act of hospitality or neighbourliness, such as sharing food, inviting someone for tea, or welcoming a newcomer. Let it be genuine care, with nothing expected in return. Ask what would help, rather than assuming. If this is not possible or safe, pray for your neighbours.

And fifth, follow the seven-day reading plan below this lesson. If you miss a day, simply begin again the next day. Your one or two sentences will help you later with the third part of My Disciple-Making Plan: a first conversation plan.

15 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We are salt and light, faithful at work and online, open-handed with our tables, and ready with gentle reasons for our hope.

Salt and light: distinctive kindness, honesty and hope, for God's glory, not our reputation. Faithful at work and online: honest work and gracious words that are true, gracious, necessary and respectful. Open your table: simple, safe, genuine hospitality, with nothing expected in return. And ready with reasons: a brief, honest answer when someone asks, with gentleness and respect.

Show me one simple way to welcome someone this week, with nothing expected in return. Make me ready to give a gentle reason for my hope when someone asks. And may people see your goodness, and give glory to you. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Hospitality should be safe for you and your guests: think about who will be present, especially children and vulnerable adults, and you may set limits on who comes into your home and how often. Where someone depends on you for work, grades, housing, healthcare, money or essential help, do not start a conversation about faith within that relationship (Lesson 1). If you face discrimination, harassment or threats because of your faith, preserve evidence where it is safe to do so and seek confidential advice about your options. If someone repeatedly asks for money, pressures you or makes you feel unsafe, it is right to set limits and seek counsel (Support Page: "I am in danger or being abused").

Optional

Go deeper

Expressing faith at work. Christians agree that believers should not be ashamed of Christ or the gospel (Mark 8:38; Romans 1:16), but they differ over how visibly faith should be expressed in particular workplaces. Some speak naturally about church, or pray quietly before meals where this is allowed. Others are more careful because of their role, workplace rules, local culture or the vulnerability of colleagues. Matthew 5:16, 1 Peter 3:15–16 and Colossians 4:5–6 call believers to visible goodness, readiness and gracious speech without prescribing one method. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that colleagues' freedom must be respected, and that no one should use workplace power to promote their faith.

Eating and hospitality across faiths and cultures. Some Christians freely eat with neighbours of other faiths and accept food offered in their homes, unless it is clearly presented as an act of worship (they often point to 1 Corinthians 10:27 and Luke 5:29–30). Others are more cautious about food connected with religious offerings, to protect their own conscience or that of others (they often point to 1 Corinthians 8:9–13 and 10:28–29). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that sharing meals with people of every background is good, that believers worship God alone, and that they should respect others' consciences. (See also Begin, Lesson 4 and Grow, Lesson 6.)

Safe hospitality. Avoid isolated situations that feel unsafe or that break safeguarding guidelines, and consider meeting new acquaintances in public places first. If you live alone, hospitality can take place with friends or in a shared space. Hospitality never requires giving anyone unrestricted access to your home, children, money or personal information.

If you face hostility because of your faith. Use formal reporting routes where they are trustworthy. Respond without retaliation, and do not put yourself or others in danger.

If you are still exploring. You may have Christian friends, neighbours or colleagues whose lives have made you curious. This lesson describes what Christians hope their everyday lives will show. Your questions are welcome. No one will ask you to share faith with others or to say publicly whether you have become a Christian.
