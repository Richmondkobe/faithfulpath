Walking With a New Believer

Someone I know has just started following Jesus and has asked me to help them. Where do I start, and how do I help without controlling them or getting out of my depth?

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Where do I start?

Welcome to Lesson 7. In Grow, you saw that faith is sustained through life together. In Lesson 4, you learned to leave people free. This lesson shows how to walk alongside a new believer through the basics of following Jesus, with gentleness, encouragement, simple teaching and healthy boundaries, always pointing them to Christ and his church.

It is a joy when someone begins to follow Jesus. It can also feel daunting when they ask you to help them. You may wonder what to teach, how often to meet, and what to do if they face problems you cannot solve. You may also have seen mentoring go wrong: a mentor who became controlling, or a new believer who became dependent on one person.

So here is our question: Someone I know has just started following Jesus and has asked me to help them. Where do I start, and how do I help without controlling them or getting out of my depth?

2 · Gentle, like a nursing mother

Our first passage is 1 Thessalonians chapter 2. Paul writes: "But we were gentle among you, like a nursing mother taking care of her own children. So, being affectionately desirous of you, we were ready to share with you not only the gospel of God but also our own selves, because you had become very dear to us."

And a few verses later: "For you know how, like a father with his children, we exhorted each one of you and encouraged you and charged you to walk in a manner worthy of God."

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Paul uses a mother's picture and a father's picture to show tenderness and encouragement together. And notice that they shared not only the gospel, but their own lives.

3 · Taken aside

Our second passage is Acts chapter 18. Apollos was "an eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures… fervent in spirit". But "he knew only the baptism of John."

Then Luke says: "When Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately."

They did not dismiss his gifts, and they did not correct him in public. They took him aside. Afterwards the believers encouraged him and wrote to support his ministry.

4 · A young church in Thessalonica

Now the background. Paul's ministry in Thessalonica was cut short by opposition. The believers sent Paul and Silas away by night. So Paul writes to a young church, reminding them how his team had lived among them.

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? Paul contrasts his team with teachers who sought money or praise. They did not flatter, and they did not use people. Instead, they "worked night and day" so as not to be a burden, and their conduct was "holy and righteous and blameless."

Walking with others depends on character and trustworthiness, not only on teaching.

5 · Coming alongside

Priscilla and Aquila were a married couple, tentmakers who had left Rome. They worked alongside Paul, and later met Apollos, a gifted teacher from Alexandria, in Ephesus.

Apollos was not simply a brand-new believer. He already knew the Scriptures and taught accurately about Jesus, though his understanding was incomplete. So his story is not a complete model of helping new believers. But it shows an important principle. Priscilla and Aquila came alongside someone respectfully, and helped him understand the way of God more accurately. Good help is humble, discreet rather than secret, and helps people flourish.

We see the same spirit in Barnabas. When Saul came to Jerusalem as a new follower of Jesus, the believers were afraid of him. "But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles." A new believer often needs, above all, someone who will stand alongside them.

6 · The short answer

So, where do we start? Here is the short answer.

Care for a new believer gently and patiently. Encourage them to walk worthy of God, without taking control of their life. Learn the basics of following Jesus together. And point them to Christ and his church, not to yourself.

That gives us four parts: Gentle Like a Mother, Encourage Like a Father, Teach the Basics Together, and Point Them to Christ and His Church.

7 · Part 1: Gentle Like a Mother

The first part: Gentle Like a Mother.

Paul and his team were "gentle… like a nursing mother", sharing "not only the gospel of God but also our own selves". Walking with a new believer begins with care and friendship, not a programme.

Listen to their story, their questions, their joys and their fears. Be patient. New believers grow at different speeds, and many face confusion, doubt or opposition from family and friends.

Like Barnabas, be someone who will stand alongside them when others hold back.

8 · Part 2: Encourage Like a Father

The second part: Encourage Like a Father.

"Like a father with his children, we exhorted each one of you and encouraged you and charged you to walk in a manner worthy of God." Encouragement includes gentle guidance and, at times, honest challenge, always for the other person's good.

But it never means control. Paul told another church, "Not that we lord it over your faith, but we work with you for your joy." Peter told leaders not to be "domineering over those in your charge, but being examples". A new believer's decisions belong to them and to God.

Paul uses both pictures, a mother's and a father's, for the same ministry team. They describe qualities of care, not fixed roles for women and men. And a mentor supports another adult. They do not treat them like a child, or take control of their decisions.

9 · Part 3: Teach the Basics Together

The third part: Teach the Basics Together.

Priscilla and Aquila explained the way of God "more accurately". Teaching a new believer should be respectful and humble, and shared rather than lectured.

The eight lessons of Begin offer a simple path: Jesus Christ and the good news; repentance, faith and grace; being born again; following Jesus as Lord; your new identity and assurance; beginning with the Bible; beginning with prayer; and belonging to Christ's church.

Read together, ask and answer questions, and practise the habits together. Let the new believer set the pace where possible. "Iron sharpens iron, and one man sharpens another." Even when one believer is helping another, both should remain humble and teachable before God.

Priscilla and Aquila helped Apollos discreetly, taking him aside. But discreet does not mean secret or unaccountable.

10 · Part 4: Point Them to Christ and His Church

The fourth part: Point Them to Christ and His Church.

The goal is not to make someone dependent on you, but to help them follow Christ and belong to his people. Paul wrote, "Him we proclaim… that we may present everyone mature in Christ." And remember who gives the growth: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth."

So help the new believer connect with a healthy church, find other Christian friends, and talk with church leaders about next steps such as baptism, which you met in Establish.

Christians differ over whether new believers are best helped one to one or in small groups, and over how soon new believers should be baptised. Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that new believers need personal care, that mentoring should be accountable to the church, and that baptism should be freely chosen, understood and safe.

Over time, encourage them to help others too: "what you have heard from me… entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also." That is how discipleship multiplies.

11 · Ana's story

Let me tell you about Ana. Her story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Ana is a schoolteacher in Lisbon, Portugal. Her neighbour Beatriz, a single mother of two, recently began following Jesus after attending Ana's church for a few months. Beatriz asked Ana to help her understand the Bible. Ana was excited but nervous. She had never done this before, and she worried about saying the wrong thing.

Ana spoke with her small group leader, Rui, who encouraged her and explained the church's simple guidelines: meet in a public place, avoid becoming Beatriz's only support, and refer serious needs. Ana and Beatriz agreed to meet every two weeks at a café for eight sessions, working through Begin together. They read a passage, talked about questions, and prayed briefly if Beatriz wished.

One day Beatriz shared that her former partner was sending threatening messages. Ana listened calmly and did not try to handle it alone. With Beatriz's agreement, she helped her contact the church's safeguarding lead and a specialist support service.

After the eight sessions, Beatriz joined a small group and made friends with other mothers in the church. Ana and Beatriz still meet occasionally as friends. Ana learned that her role was not to be everything for Beatriz, but to walk alongside her for a season, and help her connect with Christ and his people.

12 · What walking with someone is not

Here are six common mistakes about walking with a new believer.

"A mentor is the new believer's spiritual authority." No. Their decisions belong to them and to God.

"New believers should depend on me." No. The goal is dependence on Christ, and belonging to his church.

"New believers must change everything at once." No. People grow at different speeds.

"Mentoring should be private and secret." No. Discreet is not secret, and formal mentoring is accountable.

"A mentor should handle every problem." No. Some needs require qualified help.

And "Mentoring must last forever." No. A defined season, reviewed together, is healthy.

14 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We care gently, encourage without control, learn the basics together, and point new believers to Christ and his church.

Gentle like a mother: care, friendship, patience and listening come first. Encourage like a father: guidance and honest challenge, never control, because their decisions belong to them and to God. Teach the basics together: read Begin together, discreetly, but never in secret. And point them to Christ and his church: not dependence on you, but belonging to his people, and, over time, helping others too.

Pause and seek help

One-to-one relationships need boundaries. Meet in appropriate places, such as public settings or homes with others present, and follow your church's safeguarding procedures. Be careful with power: do not mentor someone you employ, teach, supervise or provide essential help to. Avoid secrecy, exclusivity, constant contact, late-night messaging, physical closeness that may be unwelcome, and money arrangements. Children and young people should be mentored only within the church's safeguarding procedures and with parents' or guardians' knowledge, unless that would put the child at risk; in that case, do not act alone or promise secrecy, and seek safeguarding advice at once. Offer a same-sex mentor where that is preferred and possible, and keep the same standards in every mentoring relationship. Formal mentoring should have trustworthy oversight; ordinary adult friendships do not need church permission. If church leadership is unsafe or involved in a concern, seek help outside that leadership. Never pressure a new believer to take risky public steps. Serious mental health struggles, addiction, abuse, legal problems or crisis are beyond a mentor's role: listen, pray, and help the person connect with qualified help (Lesson 9). If someone is in danger, follow the safeguarding and crisis procedure (Support Page: "I am in danger or being abused").

Optional

Go deeper

One-to-one mentoring or small groups. Some churches pair each new believer with one mature believer for regular meetings (they often point to Acts 18:26 and 2 Timothy 2:2). Others prefer small groups or pairs of mentors, to share wisdom and reduce risk (they often point to Acts 2:42–47 and Proverbs 11:14). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that new believers need personal care, that mentoring should be accountable to the church, and that no mentor should replace Christ or the wider church family.

How quickly new believers should be baptised. Some churches baptise new believers soon after they profess faith, following the pattern in Acts (they often point to Acts 2:41, 8:36–38 and 16:33). Others prefer a period of teaching and preparation first; this is a pastoral judgement about teaching and readiness rather than a waiting period commanded in Scripture (they often point to the call to teach in Matthew 28:19–20). For a child, follow safeguarding procedures and consider the role of parents or guardians before any baptism (Lesson 4). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that baptism should be freely chosen, understood and safe.

When family or community oppose a new believer's faith. Help them think wisely about safety and timing (Begin, Lesson 4, and the Life Guide "When Your Family Opposes Your Faith"), and never pressure them to take risky public steps.

Mother and father pictures. Paul uses both for the same ministry team (1 Thessalonians 2:7, 11). They describe qualities of care, not fixed roles for women and men, and a mentor supports another adult rather than treating them like a child.
