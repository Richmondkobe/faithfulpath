Listening, Safeguarding and Knowing Your Limits

People have started telling me personal things. What if someone tells me something serious? Should I keep it secret? How do I know when it's beyond me?

About 15 minutesExodus 18:13–26 and Ephesians 5:11–13Look back: Lessons 6, 7 and 8

In this lesson

You will learn

  • Why Jethro told Moses, "You are not able to do it alone" (Exodus 18)
  • Why Christians must never protect wrongdoing through concealment (Ephesians 5:11–13)
  • The four parts: Listen Well, Keep Confidence Wisely, Protect the Vulnerable, Know Your Limits and Refer
  • Why referral is love, not abandonment, and how to keep caring
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · When people trust you

Welcome to Lesson 9. In Establish, you saw how the church is built up together. In Grow, you learned that help is faithful, and that we protect with boundaries. This lesson gathers those safeguarding principles together, now that you are helping other people.

As you walk with others, read Scripture with them and pray together, people will begin to trust you. They may tell you about their worries, their families, their past, and sometimes things that are serious or dangerous. That trust is a privilege. It is also a responsibility.

So here is our question: People have started telling me personal things. What if someone tells me something serious? Should I keep it secret? And how do I know when it's beyond me?

2 · Not able to do it alone

Our first passage is Exodus chapter 18. Moses sat to judge the people, "and the people stood around Moses from morning till evening."

His father-in-law Jethro saw that this was exhausting Moses and failing the people. He said: "What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone."

Let us use the first question: what does it say? Jethro advised Moses to share the work with trustworthy people, and to bring the hard cases to him. Knowing your limits is wise, care is shared, and difficult matters go to those equipped to handle them.

3 · Exposed by the light

Our second passage is Ephesians chapter 5. Paul calls believers to "walk as children of light." Then he says: "Take no part in the unfruitful works of darkness, but instead expose them… But when anything is exposed by the light, it becomes visible."

Christians must not protect wrongdoing through concealment.

4 · In the wilderness

Now the background. After the exodus, Israel was camped in the wilderness: a whole nation, newly free, with countless disputes to settle. Jethro, the priest of Midian and Moses' father-in-law, came to visit.

Now our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? Jethro told Moses to teach the people God's ways, and to appoint "able men… who fear God, who are trustworthy and hate a bribe" over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens. They would handle the ordinary cases, and "every great matter they shall bring to you."

This passage is a picture that helps us understand sharing care and referring hard matters. It is not a counselling or safeguarding procedure.

5 · Children of light

In context, Paul is speaking about the moral life of believers, who once were darkness but now are light in the Lord. He is not giving a procedure for church discipline, or for reporting an allegation.

But the wider principle is consistent with safeguarding. Christians must not protect wrongdoing through concealment. When serious wrongdoing or abuse must be reported, that should happen through proper, careful routes, rather than gossip or public shaming.

6 · The short answer

So, what do we do when people tell us serious things? Here is the short answer.

Listen well. Keep confidences, but never promise secrecy when someone may be in danger. Protect the vulnerable, and never conceal abuse. And know your limits: referring someone to the right help is love, not abandonment.

That gives us four parts: Listen Well, Keep Confidence Wisely, Protect the Vulnerable, and Know Your Limits and Refer.

7 · Part 1: Listen Well

The first part: Listen Well.

James tells believers to be "quick to hear, slow to speak". "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame." And "the purpose in a man's heart is like deep water, but a man of understanding will draw it out."

Good listening is patient, attentive and humble. It means giving full attention, without checking your phone or planning your reply. It means not interrupting, and not rushing to fix, advise or quote a verse. And it means checking that you have understood: "So what I'm hearing is…"

Many people simply need to be heard. But listening is not the same as investigating. You do not need to ask for details of painful events. Let the person share what they choose.

8 · Part 2: Keep Confidence Wisely

The second part: Keep Confidence Wisely.

"Whoever goes about slandering reveals secrets, but he who is trustworthy in spirit keeps a thing covered." Trustworthy people do not gossip, including through "prayer requests".

But confidentiality has limits when someone is in danger. So never promise absolute confidentiality. Explain gently, ideally before personal matters are shared, what you will keep private, and when you may need to seek help. For example: "I'll keep what you tell me private, unless I'm worried that you or someone else might be hurt. Then I'd need to get help, and I'll tell you what I'm doing."

If a child may be experiencing abuse or neglect, do not keep the concern to yourself. Get appropriate safeguarding help promptly, following the proper procedure where you live and serve. Explain this to the child, but do not make them responsible for deciding what happens next. And when an adult tells you about abuse affecting them, seek their consent before sharing wherever possible, unless someone is in serious danger.

9 · Part 3: Protect the Vulnerable

The third part: Protect the Vulnerable.

"Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute." "Rescue the weak and the needy." Jesus welcomed children: "Let the children come to me; do not hinder them." And he warned severely against anyone who causes one of these little ones to stumble.

Children and vulnerable adults need particular protection. A vulnerable adult is someone who, because of disability, illness, age, dependence or present circumstances, cannot adequately protect themselves from abuse or exploitation. But do not assume that every disabled or older adult lacks independence.

Suspected abuse, or a possible crime, must never be concealed to protect a church, a leader or a family. A church's own process must never delay a necessary report. Report concerns and facts; it is not your job to investigate, or to decide whether a crime has been proved.

Christians believe in repentance and forgiveness. But forgiveness does not prove that someone is safe, or restore positions of trust.

10 · Part 4: Know Your Limits and Refer

The fourth part: Know Your Limits and Refer.

Jethro told Moses, "You are not able to do it alone." Paul tells believers to "bear one another's burdens", and also that "each will have to bear his own load." Christian care combines real help with appropriate personal responsibility. It does not require one believer to become responsible for everything in another person's life.

Someone who helps others grow is not automatically a counsellor, doctor, safeguarding specialist or pastor. Prayer is not a substitute for emergency, medical or professional help. "With many advisers they succeed," and Paul spoke warmly of "Luke the beloved physician." Churches organise pastoral care in different ways, but across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that serious needs require qualified help, and that safeguarding responsibilities apply to everyone.

Signs that a need is beyond your role include danger to anyone, abuse, serious mental health struggles, thoughts of suicide, addiction, legal problems, and anything you feel out of your depth with.

Referral is not abandonment. It is love that seeks the best help. Keep caring, praying and staying in touch where appropriate, while others provide specialised help.

11 · Mehmet's story

Let me tell you about Mehmet. His story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Mehmet is an engineer in Izmir, Turkey, who came to faith through a small church and now helps lead its youth activities. One evening after a youth gathering, a fifteen-year-old girl, Elif, stayed back and asked if she could tell him something, if he promised not to tell anyone.

Mehmet remembered his training. He said gently, "I want to help you. I'll keep what you tell me private unless I'm worried someone might be hurt; then I'd need to get help, and I'll tell you what I'm doing." Elif hesitated, then told him that an adult relative had been abusing her.

Mehmet listened calmly, did not ask for details, and told her she was right to tell him and that it was not her fault. Another trusted leader stayed nearby, within sight. He made sure Elif was not in immediate danger, and contacted the church's safeguarding lead before she left. He did not confront the relative, or discuss the matter with anyone else in the church.

Elif is now receiving specialist help and protection. Mehmet did not try to become her counsellor, and he prays for her. He felt shaken afterwards, and talked with the safeguarding lead and his pastor about how he was coping, without sharing Elif's details further. Faithful care sometimes means passing a concern on quickly to people trained to help. Referral is not abandonment.

12 · What this is not saying

Here are six common mistakes about listening and safeguarding.

"A good Christian keeps every secret." No. Confidentiality has limits when someone is in danger.

"Reporting abuse betrays the church or family." No. Concealing abuse betrays the vulnerable.

"I should find out what really happened." No. Do not investigate, question or confront. Report.

"Prayer is enough." No. Prayer goes alongside emergency, medical and professional help, never in place of it.

"Referring someone means I have failed them." No. Referral is love that seeks the best help.

And "Forgiveness means an abuser should return to leadership." No. Forgiveness does not prove safety, or restore positions of trust.

14 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We listen well, keep confidences wisely, protect the vulnerable, and know our limits and refer.

Listen well: patient, attentive and humble, without rushing to fix or investigate. Keep confidence wisely: no gossip, but never promise secrecy when someone may be in danger. Protect the vulnerable: children and vulnerable adults first; never conceal abuse; and pass on concerns and facts through proper routes. And know your limits and refer: you cannot carry everything, and referral is love, not abandonment.

Pause and seek help

If anyone is in immediate danger, including at risk of harming themselves or others, contact emergency services or a crisis service now, where it is safe to do so. Do not leave someone in immediate danger alone if you can stay safely, and do not put yourself in danger. Then follow your church's safeguarding procedure. If you learn of abuse or a possible crime, especially involving a child or vulnerable adult, do not investigate, question or confront anyone yourself. Listen calmly, reassure the person that they were right to tell you, and report promptly to your church's safeguarding person and, where required or appropriate, to the authorities or specialist services. If the concern involves a church leader, the safeguarding lead or someone close to them, report outside that leadership. Hearing difficult stories can affect you: seek support for yourself, without sharing identifying details, and rest when you need to (Support Pages: "I may harm myself or someone else, or I cannot stay safe"; "I am in danger or being abused"; "I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority").

Optional

Go deeper

Keeping a record. Write down the date, time, place, who was present, what you observed, the person's own words and what you did, keeping facts separate from your opinions. Sign and date the record, keep it secure, and send it only through the approved safeguarding route; do not keep copies on your phone or share it in messaging groups. Where going to the police or another authority might put the person in further danger, get urgent advice from a reputable local safeguarding, child-protection, domestic-abuse or legal service, but never use this as a reason to do nothing.

Sharing information about adults. When an adult tells you about abuse affecting them, seek their consent before sharing information wherever possible. Information may still need to be shared without consent when there is immediate or serious danger, when a child or another person is at risk, when the adult cannot protect themselves or make the decision, or when the law requires it. Share only what is necessary, with the right people, and tell the person what you are doing where it is safe.

Authorities and church processes. God has given governing authorities a role in restraining wrongdoing (Romans 13:1–4). That is a principle, not a reporting procedure, and some authorities are corrupt or unsafe; the practical guide is local law, specialist safeguarding advice and the person's safety. A church's internal process must never delay a necessary report, and Matthew 18:15–17 must never be used for abuse (Establish, Lesson 9).

How churches organise pastoral care. Some churches rely mainly on pastors and trained church members for pastoral care (they often point to 1 Peter 5:2 and Galatians 6:1–2). Others also refer regularly to professional counsellors, doctors and specialist services (they often point to Proverbs 15:22 and Colossians 4:14). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that people should receive care within the limits of each helper's training, that serious needs require qualified help, and that safeguarding responsibilities apply to everyone.

Restoration, participation and positions of trust. Christians believe in the possibility of repentance and forgiveness (Luke 15:11–24; 2 Corinthians 2:5–8). Churches may differ about how someone who has abused others can take part in church life, but safety and fitness for leadership are not simply matters of church preference. Forgiveness does not prove that someone is safe, erase legal consequences, or create a right to return to leadership (1 Timothy 3:2; Titus 1:7). Participation must be considered in the light of the nature of the harm, ongoing risk, local law, and the safety and wishes of those harmed; positions of leadership or trust must never be restored automatically, and churches should use independent safeguarding advice and a clear written safety plan. A survivor must never be required to meet, forgive publicly, worship beside or reconcile with the person who harmed them as a condition of belonging to the church.

If you are still exploring. You may wonder whether it is safe to share personal things with Christians. This lesson describes what you should expect: careful listening, no gossip, honesty about when help is needed, and protection for anyone at risk. No one will ask you to share personal matters or to say publicly whether you have become a Christian.
