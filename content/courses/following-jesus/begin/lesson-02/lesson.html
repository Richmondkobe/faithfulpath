Lesson 2 of 8

Repentance, Faith and Receiving Grace

Do I have to fix my life before God will accept me?

About 16 minutesLuke 15:11–24First Steps: My faith · My prayer

In this lesson

You will learn

  • Why Jesus told the story of the lost son, and what it shows about God
  • What "repent" (metanoeō) and "faith" (pistis) really mean
  • Grace, Turn, Trust, Receive: how God welcomes us and how we respond
  • Why good works are the fruit of grace, not its price
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · The question

Welcome to Lesson 2. In Lesson 1, we learned the gospel in four parts: who Jesus is, what Jesus announced, what Jesus did, and why it is good news and how we respond. And we heard Jesus' call in Mark 1:15: "repent and believe in the gospel." Today we look closely at those two words.

But first, a question many people quietly carry. Maybe you have heard someone say it, or said it yourself. "First I need to stop drinking." "First I need to sort out my marriage." "First I need to become a better person. Then I will come to God." It sounds humble. It sounds sensible.

So here is our question: do I have to fix my life before God will accept me? By the end of this lesson, you will be able to answer that question from the Bible, and explain what repentance and faith really mean.

2 · Why Jesus told the story

Our main passage is one of the best-known stories Jesus ever told: the story of the lost son in Luke chapter 15. But Luke first tells us why Jesus told it. Listen to Luke 15, verses 1 and 2:

"Now the tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear him. And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them.'"

In Jesus' world, eating with someone was a sign of welcome and acceptance. Tax collectors were seen as collaborators with the Roman system, and many were known for cheating. "Sinners" meant people whose lives were openly far from God's law. The religious leaders were shocked that Jesus welcomed such people to his table. So Jesus answered with three stories about something lost and found: a lost sheep, a lost coin and a lost son.

3 · A son far from home

The story begins with a younger son who asks his father for his share of the family property. In that culture, the request was deeply dishonouring. The son wanted the father's property while rejecting life with his father.

The father gives it to him. The son leaves for a distant country and wastes everything in reckless living. Then a famine comes. He ends up feeding pigs. For a Jewish listener, pigs were unclean animals, so this was about as low as anyone could fall. He is so hungry that he longs to eat the pigs' food, and no one gives him anything.

Jesus is painting a picture of someone completely lost: far from home, and far from his father.

4 · He came to himself

Then something changes. Luke 15, verses 17 to 19:

"But when he came to himself, he said, '… I will arise and go to my father, and I will say to him, "Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Treat me as one of your hired servants."'"

Notice what happens. He comes to himself: he sees his situation honestly. He makes a decision: "I will arise and go." And he plans to admit his sin. Then verse 20 says, "And he arose and came to his father." He actually turns around and walks home.

But notice also what he still believes. He thinks he must earn his way back as a servant.

5 · The father runs

Now comes the most surprising part of the story. Verse 20:

"But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him."

The picture is deliberately striking. The father does not stand at a distance and make his son work his way back. He sees him, feels compassion, runs to him, embraces him and kisses him. The son begins his speech, but he never finishes it.

The father calls for the best robe, a ring for his hand, shoes for his feet and a feast. Together they publicly show that the father is receiving him back into the household as his son. Verse 24: "For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found."

6 · Repent = turn around

Now let us look at two words that help us understand this story. The first is the word Jesus used in Mark 1:15: repent. The Greek verb translated "repent" is metanoeō. In the New Testament, repentance is not simply feeling sorry or changing an opinion. It is a real change of mind and direction: a turning from sin towards God.

This fits the great biblical call we hear in the prophets. Joel says, "Return to the LORD your God." Repentance is that kind of return.

Earlier in this same chapter, Jesus says there is joy in heaven over one sinner who repents. The son's return helps us see what that repentance looks like: he comes to himself, admits his sin and goes home.

7 · Faith = trust

The second word is faith. In English, "faith" and "believe" look like two different words. But in Greek they come from the same family: pistis is faith, and pisteuō is to believe.

Saving faith is not merely agreeing that certain facts are true. It means relying on Jesus: trusting who he is and what he has done. It is like putting your full weight on someone you trust.

So Jesus' call to "repent and believe" means turning to God and trusting the good news about Jesus. These are the two Greek words for today: metanoeō, repent, and pistis, faith.

8 · The short answer

So, do you have to fix your life before God will accept you? No. You could not do it even if you tried, and God does not ask you to fix yourself first.

Here is the answer in a few sentences. God comes to us in grace. We turn to him and trust Jesus, and we receive what God freely gives. Our changed lives are the fruit of his welcome, not the price of it.

The rest of this lesson unpacks that answer in four parts: grace, turn, trust and receive.

9 · Truth 1: Grace

The first part is grace. Grace means God's undeserved kindness: love that is given, not earned. Everything in our salvation begins here, with God moving towards us.

Through this father, Jesus gives us a powerful picture of God's welcoming grace towards the lost. Then Paul states it plainly in Ephesians 2: "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast."

Saved means rescued: brought back to God, forgiven and given new life. Paul says this rescue is a gift. We cannot buy it with good deeds, religious ceremonies or money. No one can boast that they deserved it. That is why grace is such good news for people who know they have failed.

10 · Truth 2: Turn

The second part is turning, which is repentance. To repent means to turn away from sin and from insisting on our own way, and to turn towards God.

Repentance is more than feeling bad. Paul describes two kinds of sorrow in 2 Corinthians 7, verse 10: "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death." Sorrow by itself is not repentance. Paul distinguishes godly grief, which produces repentance, from worldly grief, which leaves us trapped in sorrow without turning to God. In Acts 3, Peter's preaching makes the direction clear: "Repent therefore, and turn back, that your sins may be blotted out." The promise attached to turning is forgiveness.

And remember, repentance is a real turn, not instant perfection. The son came home with nothing fixed: no money, no clean clothes, no restored reputation. Christians still struggle with sin, and when we fall, we turn again. We also repent of our own sin, not of what others have done to us. If someone has wronged you, that was their sin, not yours, and God sees it.

11 · Truth 3: Trust

The third part is trust, which is faith. In the book of Acts, a prison guard in the city of Philippi, terrified after an earthquake, asked Paul and Silas, "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" Their answer was simple: "Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved."

Faith is not the same as being certain about everything, or having strong religious feelings. Think of crossing a river on a bridge. A nervous person on a strong bridge is safe. A confident person on a rotten bridge is not. Faith is like stepping onto a strong bridge. What matters most is not the strength of your feelings, but the strength of the one you are trusting. Our confidence rests on Jesus.

So faith means we stop relying on our own goodness, on religious activity, on our feelings, or even on how strongly we believe. Instead, we rely on Jesus.

12 · Truth 4: Receive

The fourth part is receiving. John writes about Jesus: "But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God." Notice that John puts receive and believe side by side. Receiving is not an extra task. It is what faith looks like: open hands.

In the story, the son had his speech ready: "Treat me as one of your hired servants." But the father would not let him finish. Instead of a servant's job, he received a robe, a ring and a party.

This does not mean that nothing changes afterwards. Straight after saying we are saved by grace, Paul adds in Ephesians 2, verse 10: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them." Good works matter very much. But they are the fruit of grace, not its price.

13 · Niran's story

Let me tell you about Niran. His story is drawn from the experiences of many people. Niran works in an office in Bangkok and grew up in a Buddhist family. When a colleague invited him to church, he answered politely, "Maybe later. First I need to sort out my life and do more good things. Then I will come."

His colleague did not argue. She asked if they could read one short story together during their lunch break. They read Luke 15. Niran stopped at verse 20 and read it again: "While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "The son did not repair himself first. He did not earn his father's welcome. He just turned and came home."

Niran did not make any decision that day. But he asked whether they could read Luke's Gospel together each week. He still has many questions, and that is where his story stops for now.

Niran's way of thinking is common in every culture, and even among people who have gone to church all their lives. Many of us believe God's acceptance must be earned. Jesus' story shows us the Father's generous welcome of the returning lost.

14 · What repentance and faith are not

Because these words are so important, they are often misunderstood. Here are four common mistakes.

"I have to become good enough first." You do not make yourself good enough before coming to God. You come as you are, turning from sin and trusting Christ. Then God begins his transforming work in you.

"Repenting means feeling very guilty." Sorrow may come, but repentance is turning back to God, not sinking into shame.

"Faith means never having doubts." Faith is trusting Jesus, not feeling certain about everything. Even a small, shaky faith rests on a strong Saviour.

And "Saying the right prayer is what saves me." Praying simply and honestly can be a good way to express repentance and faith. But the words are not magic. It is Jesus who saves, not the words.

15 · This week

Now, because this is true, here is what I would like you to do this week. Your assignment is to respond to God's grace in one real, concrete way.

First, read Luke 15:11 to 24 again, slowly. Second, in private, ask yourself two questions. Where do I need to turn? Is there something I know is wrong that I need to turn away from, or an area of my life where I have been insisting on my own way? And where do I need to trust? Is there something I have been relying on instead of Jesus: my own goodness, my feelings, or my religious activity?

Third, choose one small, concrete step of turning and trusting, and take it this week. It might be saying sorry to someone, stopping something you know is wrong, or praying honestly each morning, "Lord, I trust you with this." And finally, receive. Thank God that his welcome is a gift, not a reward.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. You do not have to fix your life before God will accept you. Grace: God moves towards us in undeserved kindness, as Jesus pictures in the father's welcome. Turn: repentance means turning from sin and self-rule towards God; it is a real turn, not instant perfection. Trust: faith means relying on Jesus, not on our goodness, our rituals, our feelings or the strength of our belief. Receive: we stop trying to earn, and receive what God gives. Good works are the fruit of grace, not its price.

17 · Prayer

Let us pray. Father, thank you for your grace. Thank you that I do not have to make myself good enough before coming to you. Thank you that through Jesus you welcome sinners who turn to you and trust him. I turn from my own way and from the things I know are wrong. I trust Jesus, not my goodness, my feelings or my efforts. I receive your grace with open hands. By your grace, change the way I live. In Jesus' name. Amen.
