Loving Others in Family, Friendship and Church

Loving God feels easier than loving the people around me: my family, my difficult church members, my neighbours. What does Christian love actually look like?

In this lesson

You will learn

  • What Jesus means by "love one another… as I have loved you", from John 13:34–35
  • What God requires in Micah 6:8, and the meaning of "kindness"
  • The four parts: Love as Jesus Loved, Honour Your Family, Be a True Friend, Do Justice and Love Mercy

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Loving the people around me

Welcome to Lesson 6 of Grow. In Begin, you learned to care for one another. In Establish, you saw that the church is built up together, and that every person is made in God's image. Now we are going one step further, and asking what Christ's love looks like in the relationships of everyday life.

It can be easier to sing about love on Sunday than to show it on Monday. Family members can be demanding. Church members can be difficult. Neighbours can be strangers. And some people around us are lonely, overlooked or in real need.

So here is our question: loving God feels easier than loving the people around me: my family, my difficult church members, my neighbours. What does Christian love actually look like?

2 · A new commandment

Our first passage is John chapter 13, verses 34 and 35: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another. By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another."

Let us use the first question from Establish: what does it say? Notice the pattern: "just as I have loved you". And notice the result: love among believers becomes a witness to the world.

3 · What does the LORD require?

Our second passage is Micah chapter 6, verse 8: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?"

Three things: do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with your God.

4 · Kindness = ḥeseḏ

Our Hebrew word today is the one translated "kindness" in Micah 6:8: ḥeseḏ. It can describe steadfast, loyal and merciful love: faithful love expressed in the way we treat others.

So God is not asking only for occasional acts of niceness. He calls his people to value faithful, merciful love.

Keep this word in mind. We will meet it again in Lesson 11.

5 · The upper room

Now the background. Jesus spoke these words at the Last Supper, just after washing his disciples' feet, including the feet of the one who would betray him, and just before going to the cross.

Now use our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? The commandment's newness is especially seen in its Christ-shaped pattern: "as I have loved you". His self-giving love becomes the pattern for his people, and his saving work makes this new community possible. We love as people who have first been loved by him.

6 · Worship without justice

Micah spoke to a people among whom worship and injustice had become separated. In verses 6 and 7, the worshipper asks what sacrifices could satisfy God. God's answer turns attention to the life he requires: do justice, love kindness and walk humbly with him. Worship and justice belong together.

To walk humbly with your God means living faithfully before God, in humble dependence on him. So justice and merciful love belong to a life centred on God. They are not self-righteousness, or a badge of belonging to a group.

7 · The short answer

So, what does Christian love actually look like? Here is the short answer.

Jesus calls us to love one another as he has loved us. That love honours family wisely, makes us true friends, and moves us to do justice and show mercy to our neighbours.

That gives us four parts: Love as Jesus Loved, Honour Your Family, Be a True Friend, and Do Justice and Love Mercy.

8 · Part 1: Love as Jesus Loved

The first part: Love as Jesus Loved.

Christian love is practical. "Let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth." And John explains where it comes from: "By this we know love, that he laid down his life for us."

In the church, love welcomes people across differences of class, culture, age and background. James warns believers to "show no partiality". The rich visitor and the poor visitor receive the same welcome. Love shows itself in ordinary ways: "Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep."

Love is patient and kind, but it is not the same as approving everything. Love "does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth."

9 · Part 2: Honour Your Family

The second part: Honour Your Family.

"Honor your father and your mother" is, as Paul notes, "the first commandment with a promise." Believers honour parents with respect, gratitude and, as parents grow older, practical care. Paul says believers should "make some return to their parents."

Parents have responsibilities too: "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord."

And Jesus also made his followers into a wider family: "whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother." Single, widowed, divorced and estranged believers belong fully in God's household.

10 · Honour with wisdom

But honour is not unlimited obedience. No human authority is absolute. When the apostles were commanded to stop preaching Jesus, they answered, "We must obey God rather than men." Jesus taught that loyalty to him comes before even our closest family ties. For an adult believer, honouring parents does not mean obeying every parental demand.

And honour never requires accepting abuse. Children are normally called to obey their parents, but no child is required to submit to abuse, take part in wrongdoing, keep harmful secrets, or stay silent when someone is unsafe. A child who is frightened or harmed should tell a safe adult.

For an adult, honouring a parent can sometimes coexist with limited or no contact for safety, while refusing contempt or revenge. Distance for safety is not dishonour.

Families also live with different cultural customs, and Christians differ on which ones believers can join. You will find that under Go deeper on the lesson page.

11 · Part 3: Be a True Friend

The third part: Be a True Friend.

"A friend loves at all times", and "there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother." Jesus called his disciples "friends", and he showed the greatest love of all: "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends."

True friends are loyal, honest and kind. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend." But honest friendship is not permission to criticise constantly, shame someone publicly or control their decisions. Faithful correction is truthful, proportionate, and offered for the other person's good, with humility and a willingness to listen.

Healthy friendship is freely chosen and mutual. A friend is not entitled to your passwords, constant availability, private information or exclusive attention. And many people today are lonely. Christian friendship is one of the clearest ways to show Christ's love.

12 · Part 4: Do Justice and Love Mercy

The fourth part: Do Justice and Love Mercy.

When a lawyer asked Jesus, "Who is my neighbor?", Jesus told the story of the Good Samaritan, a man from a despised group who stopped to help a wounded stranger. Then he asked which one "proved to be a neighbor", and said, "You go, and do likewise." Instead of asking which people count as our neighbours, his followers are called to become neighbours, by showing mercy to people in need.

"Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction." And Scripture calls God's people to "open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute." Believers care for people who face exclusion, poverty or exploitation, honouring the dignity of every person made in God's image.

Biblical justice is not owned by any political party, and Christians may disagree about the wisest solutions. Churches also differ in how they pursue justice and mercy, and you will find that under Go deeper. And helping must respect people. Ask what would help rather than assuming, respect their choices, and never photograph or publicise people in need without their consent.

13 · Camila's story

Let me tell you about Camila. Her story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Camila is an office administrator in Santiago, Chile. Her mother has dementia and now lives with her. Camila's brother lives nearby but rarely helps, and they often argue about it. After months of broken sleep and missed work, Camila felt angry, guilty and alone. She wondered how she could honour her mother when she sometimes felt only resentment.

Her small group read John 13:34 and 35, and the leader asked, "What would love look like for Camila right now?" The group organised a simple rota: two people sat with her mother on Saturday afternoons, so that Camila could rest, and another member helped her contact a local carers' support service. With Camila's and her mother's agreement, and following the church's safeguarding procedures, those helping were given basic information about her mother's routines and emergency contacts. They did not take on tasks they were not trained for, and they respected her mother's dignity.

Camila wrote to her brother, calmly asking whether he could cover two evenings a week. He agreed to one. The group leader reminded her, "Your wellbeing matters too. Receiving rest and help is part of caring well, not a failure to love your mother."

Caring for her mother is still hard, and Camila still struggles with resentment some days. But she sleeps better and feels less alone. Recently, noticing a Venezuelan family new to the church, she offered to help them with school enrolment, asking first what they needed. A few weeks later, the father, an electrician, offered to repair the lighting in the church hall. Love grows when believers share burdens, and those who receive care often become people who give it.

14 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are five common mistakes about love.

"Loving people means approving of everything they do." No. Love rejoices with the truth.

"Honouring parents means obeying them in everything." No. Adults follow Christ first, and honour never requires accepting abuse.

"Keeping distance from an unsafe relative is dishonouring them." No. Distance for safety is not dishonour.

"Justice belongs to one political side." No. Biblical justice is not owned by any party.

And "Helping means doing what I think is best for people." No. Ask what would help, and respect their dignity and choices.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We love as Jesus loved, honour our families wisely, become true friends, and do justice and love mercy.

Love as Jesus loved: practical, self-giving love that welcomes without favouritism and rejoices with the truth. Honour your family: respect, gratitude and care, not unlimited obedience, and distance for safety is not dishonour. Be a true friend: loyal, honest, kind and freely chosen, never controlling. And do justice and love mercy: be a neighbour to anyone in need, speak up for the vulnerable, and respect people's dignity.

Give me wisdom to honour my family, faithfulness as a friend, and a heart to do justice, to love kindness, and to walk humbly with you. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

Abuse, violence, threats, control of money or movement, pressure to marry someone, or harm to children or older family members are never acceptable, whatever a culture or family expects, and honouring your family does not require keeping such harm secret (Support Page: I am in danger or being abused). If someone fears forced marriage or family retaliation, do not contact the family, attempt mediation or reveal where the person is staying; seek specialist help and follow their safety plan. When helping vulnerable people, follow your church's safeguarding procedures, and never make food, money or church support depend on attending worship, sharing a testimony or being photographed. Caring for a sick, disabled or older family member can be exhausting: rest and help are not selfish, so talk with your church, a doctor or a carers' support service.

Optional

Go deeper

Which family and cultural customs believers can join. In many cultures, family life includes customs such as rites for ancestors, festival offerings, or ceremonies connected with other religions. Some Christians join family gatherings and meals while declining any act of worship directed to another god or spirit (they often point to 1 Corinthians 10:25–33). Others stay away from such events altogether to avoid confusion or compromise (they often point to 2 Corinthians 6:14–17 and 1 Corinthians 10:20–21). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that believers should honour their families with love and respect while worshipping God alone. See the Life Guide "Traditional Religious Practices and Ancestors".

How churches pursue justice and mercy. Some churches focus mainly on personal kindness, practical help and sharing the gospel, and are cautious about public campaigning (they often point to Galatians 6:10 and 1 Timothy 2:1–2). Others also speak up publicly for the vulnerable and work to change unjust practices (they often point to Isaiah 1:17, Jeremiah 29:7 and Proverbs 31:8–9). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that the gospel calls believers to love their neighbours in practical ways and to care especially for the vulnerable. Caution about politics must never become an excuse to ignore abuse, exploitation, corruption, favouritism or preventable harm.
