Emotions, Wounds and Knowing When to Seek Help

Christians are supposed to have joy and peace. So why do I feel so low, anxious or hurt? Is something wrong with my faith?

  • How God cared for Elijah when he was worn out, from 1 Kings 19:1–8
  • The four parts: Made to Feel, Pour Out Your Heart, Care for the Whole Person, Help Is Faithful
  • Why seeking help from a doctor or counsellor is a faithful step, not a failure of faith
Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Is something wrong with my faith?

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Grow. In Begin, you learned that prayer can be honest. In Lesson 2, you saw that feelings are brought to God, not labelled true or false. Now we are going one step further, and looking more closely at emotions and wounds.

Many believers carry a hidden burden. They feel sad, anxious, angry or numb, and they think a real Christian should not feel this way. Sometimes others make it worse: "Just pray more." "Where is your joy?" So they hide their feelings, even from God.

So here is our question: Christians are supposed to have joy and peace. So why do I feel so low, anxious or hurt? Is something wrong with my faith?

2 · As a deer pants

Our first passage is Psalm 42. It begins with longing: "As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God."

Then come tears: "My tears have been my food day and night." And then the writer speaks to his own soul: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation."

Let us use the first question from Establish: what does it say? Notice that the writer is honest about his tears, and he still speaks hope to himself. He does both at once.

3 · Arise and eat

Our second passage is 1 Kings chapter 19. The prophet Elijah ran for his life, collapsed under a tree in the wilderness, and said, "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life."

Notice what God did first. He did not rebuke him. Elijah slept, and an angel touched him and gave him food and water. Then the angel came a second time and said, "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you."

If you have ever felt as Elijah did, please do not carry it alone. The lesson page shows where to find help straight away.

4 · Soul = nepeš

Our Hebrew word today is the one translated "soul" in Psalm 42: nepeš. In the Old Testament it often means the living person, the self, with all its longing and desire.

So when the psalmist says, "so pants my soul for you, O God", he is describing his whole self thirsting for God. And when he says, "Why are you cast down, O my soul?", he is speaking to himself.

That is a gift for us. We can be honest about how low we feel, and in the same prayer we can say to ourselves, "Hope in God."

5 · Far from home

Now the background. The writer of Psalm 42 appears to be far from the place where he once worshipped with God's people, and he is surrounded by people who mock him: "Where is your God?" He remembers better days, when he went with the crowd to the house of God, "with glad shouts and songs of praise."

Now use our second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? Psalms 42 and 43 belong together, and the same words come three times: "Why are you cast down, O my soul? Hope in God." The writer's mood rises and falls, and the psalm does not end with everything resolved.

So its lesson is not "feel better quickly". It is "keep turning to God, even when feelings are heavy."

6 · After the mountain

Elijah had just seen God's great victory on Mount Carmel. Then Queen Jezebel threatened his life, and he ran.

Notice the order of God's care. First, rest and food. Later, a gentle conversation, in which God also corrected Elijah's view of his situation. Then a companion, Elisha, and a new task.

The passage does not tell us whether Elijah had a particular health condition, so we should not diagnose him from the text. Nor does it prove that every low mood is only tiredness. But it shows that God attends to the whole person, and does not shame those who are worn out.

7 · The short answer

So, is something wrong with your faith if you feel low, anxious or hurt? Here is the short answer.

God made us as feeling people. We can bring every emotion honestly to him. He cares for our bodies as well as our souls. And when wounds or struggles need more than prayer and friendship, seeking qualified help is a faithful step.

That gives us four parts: Made to Feel, Pour Out Your Heart, Care for the Whole Person, and Help Is Faithful.

8 · Part 1: Made to Feel

The first part: Made to Feel.

God made us as whole human beings who think, choose, relate and feel. Scripture speaks openly about human emotion, and it describes God's own joy, compassion, grief and anger.

Jesus, fully human, felt deeply. "Jesus wept" at his friend's tomb. He looked around at people "with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart." In Gethsemane he was "greatly distressed and troubled", and said, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death." So "we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses."

Emotions are not sinful in themselves. They can alert us that something matters. But they do not always tell us accurately what has happened, or what we should do. So they should be neither denied nor blindly obeyed. "Be angry and do not sin."

9 · Part 2: Pour Out Your Heart

The second part: Pour Out Your Heart.

The psalms show us how to pray when we feel low. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" They do not hide their feelings from God, and neither do we need to.

"Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us." "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit."

Pouring out our hearts is not the opposite of faith. It is an act of faith. Like the writer of Psalm 42, we can be honest about our tears and still speak hope to our own souls. Faith does not pretend. It brings everything to God.

10 · Part 3: Care for the Whole Person

The third part: Care for the Whole Person.

God's care for Elijah began with sleep, food and water. God cares for our bodies as well as our souls. Sleep, food, rest, movement, friendship and help with burdens are part of faithful living, not distractions from it.

Jesus invites us, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." And Paul says, "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ." God often comforts us through other people. He "comforts us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction."

11 · Wounds can take time

Wounds can take time to heal. Grief, betrayal and hurt are not usually healed instantly, and healing does not mean pretending we feel fine.

Psalm 147 celebrates God's restoration of his wounded people: "He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds." That same God meets his people in grief and hurt. Paul could describe himself as "afflicted in every way, but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair."

12 · Part 4: Help Is Faithful

The fourth part: Help Is Faithful.

Some wounds and struggles need more than personal prayer and encouragement from friends. Grief, trauma, the effects of abuse, depression, anxiety, addictions and other health concerns may need qualified help.

"Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed." Paul spoke warmly of "Luke the beloved physician." And Jesus once said, "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick." He was speaking about sinners needing him, so that verse is not a proof of our point. But it reminds us that needing a physician is nothing to be ashamed of.

Seeing a doctor or counsellor, or taking prescribed medication, is not a lack of faith. Prayer, Scripture and church care can go alongside professional help. Christians differ in their approach to counselling and to prayer for inner healing, and you will find both under Go deeper on the lesson page.

13 · Marcia's story

Let me tell you about Marcia. Her story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Marcia works at a hotel reception desk in Montego Bay, Jamaica. Last year her mother died, and a few months later her hours at work were cut. She began waking at three in the morning, crying without warning, and losing interest in the things she loved. At church, someone told her, "The joy of the Lord is your strength. Just praise your way through." Marcia tried, but she felt worse, and she began to wonder whether God had left her.

Her small group leader, Desmond, sat with her and read 1 Kings 19. "Look how God cared for Elijah," he said. "He let him sleep and gave him food before he said anything else. God is not disappointed with you." He also read Psalm 42 and said, "Your tears are not a lack of faith. But I think it would be wise to see a doctor about your sleep and mood."

With Marcia's agreement, two women from the church brought meals for a few weeks. Her doctor diagnosed depression and connected her with a counsellor. Desmond kept checking in, without trying to be her counsellor himself.

Change has been slow. Marcia still has heavy days, and she still misses her mother deeply. But she sleeps better, meets her counsellor every two weeks, and prays Psalm 42 honestly, including verse 5. She has returned to her small group. Many believers feel ashamed of sadness or anxiety, and need to hear that seeking help is a faithful step, not a failure of faith.

14 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are five common mistakes about emotions.

"Faithful Christians always feel happy." No. Jesus wept and was deeply troubled.

"Depression or anxiety shows weak faith or hidden sin." No. They do not by themselves show weak faith or hidden sin, and they may need appropriate health care.

"Emotions should always be obeyed." No. Emotions are brought to God, neither denied nor blindly followed.

"Prayer and medicine are opposites." No. Prayer, Scripture and church care can go alongside professional help.

And "The group can replace professional care." No. A group can support and pray, but it is not therapy.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. God made us to feel. We pour out our hearts to him. He cares for the whole person. And seeking help is a faithful step.

Made to feel: God made us whole people who think, choose, relate and feel, and Jesus felt deeply too. Pour out your heart: faith does not pretend; bring everything to God, and speak hope to your soul. Care for the whole person: rest, food, friendship and shared burdens are part of faithful living. And help is faithful: doctors, counsellors and medication can go alongside prayer and church care.

17 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord, you know my heart, and you are near to the brokenhearted. I bring you what I feel today, honestly. Thank you that Jesus wept, and that he understands our weakness. Care for me, body and soul, and give me the help I need, through your people and through those you have given skill to heal. My soul, hope in God; I shall again praise him. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

If you are thinking about ending your life, have made a plan or cannot keep yourself safe, seek urgent help now: contact local emergency or crisis services and, if possible, stay with a safe person (Support Page: I may harm myself or someone else, or I cannot stay safe). Seek prompt professional help if you begin seeing or hearing things others do not, have periods of needing very little sleep while feeling unusually energetic or agitated, or experience serious emotional changes during pregnancy or after giving birth. Consider seeing a doctor or qualified counsellor if low mood or anxiety continues most days for two weeks or more, or sooner if it is severe or affecting your daily life. Choose help wisely: a safe helper has recognised training and accountability, and never guarantees healing, pressures you to stop medication or blames continuing symptoms on lack of faith (Support Page: Safety and Support: Start Here).

Optional

Go deeper

Approaches to Christian counselling. Some Christians prefer counselling based mainly on Scripture, given by pastors or counsellors trained to counsel from the Bible, and are cautious about ideas that conflict with Christian belief (they often point to 2 Timothy 3:16–17 and Colossians 2:8). Others also welcome qualified counsellors and psychologists, Christian or not, who use well-tested methods, seeing helpful knowledge as a gift from God (they often point to Proverbs 15:22 and Colossians 4:14). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that Scripture is central to Christian life, that some conditions need medical treatment, and that any counsellor should be qualified, accountable and respectful of the person's faith.

Prayer for inner healing. Some churches pray for the healing of painful memories and emotional wounds, asking Christ to bring comfort and freedom (they often point to Psalm 147:3 and Luke 4:18). Others welcome prayer for comfort but are cautious about methods that try to uncover hidden or forgotten memories, because suggestion can distort memory (they often point to 1 Thessalonians 5:21). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that Christ heals and comforts. Prayer must never suggest what supposedly happened, use imagination exercises to create scenes, or treat pictures, sensations or impressions during prayer as proof of past abuse. If a memory arises naturally, do not question the person or try to verify it in the prayer time; respond calmly and help them find suitably qualified help.
