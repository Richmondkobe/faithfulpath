Suffering, Doubt, Lament and Perseverance

I have prayed and believed, but the suffering has not stopped. Some days I am not even sure God is listening. Is it wrong to feel this way? How do I keep going?

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · When suffering does not stop

Welcome to Lesson 11 of Grow. In Lesson 4, you learned to pour out your heart to God. In Establish, you saw the hope of Christ's return and the renewed creation. This lesson brings those together: how to suffer honestly with God, how to hold on through doubt, and how to keep going together.

Sooner or later, every believer meets suffering that does not go away quickly: illness, grief, disability, loss of work, broken relationships, persecution. Prayers seem unanswered. Questions grow. Some people are told that their suffering shows a lack of faith, and they begin to hide their pain and their doubts.

So here is our question: I have prayed and believed, but the suffering has not stopped. Some days I am not even sure God is listening. Is it wrong to feel this way? How do I keep going?

2 · How long, O LORD?

Our first passage is Psalm 13. It begins: "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? How long must I take counsel in my soul and have sorrow in my heart all the day?"

And near the end, the psalmist says: "But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation." Use the first question: what does it say? Notice that the trouble has not ended. The psalmist chooses to trust God while still waiting.

3 · Not crushed

Our second passage is 2 Corinthians 4. Paul writes: "We are afflicted in every way, but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; struck down, but not destroyed."

4 · Steadfast love: ḥeseḏ again

You have met today's word before. In Lesson 6, we saw ḥeseḏ, translated "kindness" in Micah 6:8: steadfast, loyal and merciful love.

In Psalm 13, it is translated "steadfast love". The whole lament turns on it: "But I have trusted in your steadfast love." When nothing else has changed, the psalmist holds on to God's loyal love.

5 · The shape of a lament

Now the background. Psalm 13 is a lament psalm. It has three movements: honest complaint in verses 1 and 2, urgent request in verses 3 and 4, and chosen trust in verses 5 and 6. In the first two verses alone, the psalmist asks "How long?" four times.

Now use the second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? God gave his people prayers like this to use. The psalm gives words for every stage. You do not need to reach verse 5 quickly.

6 · Jars of clay

Paul describes himself as a jar of clay: "But we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us." Clay jars were ordinary, inexpensive household vessels in Paul's world: common and fragile rather than impressive.

Paul had faced severe hardship. Earlier in the same letter he wrote, "we despaired of life itself." His hope is not that suffering is unreal, or that faith removes it. It is that God's power is shown in weakness, and that resurrection is coming.

7 · The short answer

So, is it wrong to feel this way? And how do I keep going? Here is the short answer.

We can bring our pain, protest and questions honestly to God. Doubt can sit alongside real faith. Our hope rests on God's presence now and on the resurrection to come. And we keep going together, held by God and by his people.

That gives us four parts: Bring Your Lament; Doubt Honestly; Hope Beyond What Is Seen; and Keep Going Together.

8 · Part 1: Bring Your Lament

The first part: Bring Your Lament.

"How long, O LORD?" Lament is honest prayer that brings pain, protest and confusion to God, rather than away from him. Many of the psalms are laments. Some, like Psalm 88, end without any resolution at all. Jesus himself prayed in anguish in Gethsemane, and he wept at his friend's grave. He told his disciples plainly, "In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world."

Lament is not a lack of faith. It is faith that keeps speaking to God when life hurts. And the words "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning" were written in the middle of Jerusalem's devastating grief. Biblical hope can speak from within suffering, not only after it has passed.

9 · Part 2: Doubt Honestly

The second part: Doubt Honestly.

A desperate father once brought his suffering son to Jesus and said, "I believe; help my unbelief!" Jesus did not reject him. He helped him.

Doubt is not necessarily the rejection of God. Questions and uncertainty can exist alongside genuine faith. Faithful believers can experience seasons of questions, darkness or numbness. Scripture tells the church, "have mercy on those who doubt."

Honest doubt is best brought to God, to Scripture and to trusted believers, rather than hidden in shame. You do not need to have every answer before you pray.

10 · Part 3: Hope Beyond What Is Seen

The third part: Hope Beyond What Is Seen.

"So we do not lose heart. Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day." Paul calls his suffering "light momentary affliction" only by comparing it with "an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison". He is not making light of pain.

God is present in suffering. "The Spirit helps us in our weakness." And nothing, "neither death nor life… nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Our final hope is the resurrection and the new creation, where God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more."

11 · Healing, and continuing illness

God does heal today, and believers are invited to pray for healing. "Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him."

But Scripture also shows faithful people living with continuing weakness or illness. Paul prayed three times for his "thorn in the flesh" to be removed, and the Lord answered, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." And Paul left his co-worker Trophimus ill at Miletus.

Continuing illness, disability or death does not prove weak faith. Nor should suffering automatically be blamed on a person's particular sin. When the disciples asked whether a man's blindness resulted from his own sin or his parents' sin, Jesus rejected both explanations: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents."

12 · Part 4: Keep Going Together

The fourth part: Keep Going Together.

"Let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith." Perseverance is not gritting our teeth alone. We keep trusting, praying and taking the next faithful step, because God holds us and gives us his people to help carry us.

When Job's friends first came, "they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great." That was their best moment. They did harm when they began to insist that his suffering must be his fault, and God later said they had not spoken of him what is right.

God's people carry one another through suffering with presence, practical help and prayer. Ordinary steps matter too: rest, medical care, a meal shared, a short prayer, showing up to church when you can. Keeping going may look small. It may mean praying only "How long?" today. That is still holding on.

13 · Christians differ

Christians are not all agreed on two questions here. First, healing and the cross. Some Christians teach that healing is provided through Christ's death, and that believers should pray in faith for healing today. Among them, some believe physical healing is promised to every believer now, while others do not. Other Christians agree that God heals today, but emphasise that full healing belongs to the resurrection, and that God does not always heal now.

Second, how God's rule relates to suffering. Some emphasise God's sovereign rule over all things, and his ability to bring good even through suffering, while insisting that God is not the author of evil. Romans 8:28 says God works in all things for good, not that all things are good. Others emphasise God's opposition to evil, and describe much suffering in terms of what God permits within a fallen world, rather than something he directly causes.

Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that God is able to heal and that Christians pray for the sick, that seeking appropriate medical care is consistent with faith, and that continuing illness does not prove weak faith. And they hold that God is good, that evil is real, and that God will one day end suffering.

14 · Nimali's story

Let me tell you about Nimali. Her story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Nimali is a university lecturer in Colombo, Sri Lanka. Two years ago she was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis. Her church prayed for her healing, and she believed God could heal her. When her symptoms worsened, a visiting preacher told her, "If you had enough faith, you would be healed." Nimali stopped going to the evening prayer meeting. Some nights she lay awake, wondering whether God was listening at all.

Her pastor's wife, Ruth, visited her and sat with her quietly before saying anything. Then she read Psalm 13 aloud. "You are allowed to pray like this," Ruth said. "Paul prayed three times for his thorn in the flesh to be removed, and God gave him sustaining grace instead. Your illness is not a sign of weak faith."

With Nimali's agreement, the elders prayed for her gently, and she continued her medical treatment. Her small group arranged lifts to hospital appointments, and asked Nimali what would help before changing the meeting room so she could attend more easily. Nimali began praying the psalms of lament, sometimes stopping at verse 2 when she could not yet say verse 5.

Nimali is still ill, and some days are hard. But she has returned to her small group, teaches part-time, and has started a quiet prayer group for others living with long-term illness. These were her own free choices, not proofs of her faith or worth. She says, "I still have questions, but I am not alone, and I have not let go of God."

15 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are six common mistakes.

"Everything happens for a reason, so don't be sad." No. We do not explain why God allowed a particular person's suffering; we lament and hope.

"Lament and doubt show weak faith." No. They can be faith that keeps speaking to God.

"If you had enough faith, you would be healed." No. Continuing illness, disability or death does not prove weak faith, hidden sin or a curse.

"God does not heal today." No. God does heal, and we pray for healing, without guarantees.

"Suffering is always a lesson, so accept it." No. Seek help, treatment and safety where they are available.

And "I must keep going alone." No. Perseverance is being held by God and his people.

16 · This week

This week, your assignment is to bring your lament to God, and take one small step each day.

First, read Psalm 13 and 2 Corinthians 4:7 to 18. Second, write one short line for each of the four parts, in your own words. You can use the book to help.

Third, if you wish, write a short, honest lament using Psalm 13 as a pattern: tell God what hurts, ask him for what you need, and, if you can, name one thing you will trust about him. You may stop at any point. Use a separate sheet that you can destroy. If this brings up overwhelming feelings or thoughts of harming yourself, stop and seek support.

Fourth, each day, take one small step that helps you keep going, such as resting, contacting a friend, or keeping a medical appointment.

And fifth, without looking, try to say the four parts of Lessons 4 and 10. You may take one lesson a day. And follow the seven-day reading plan below this lesson. If you miss a day, simply begin again the next day. This assignment belongs to the My Growth area called keep going through hardship.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We bring our lament to God, doubt honestly, hope in what is not yet seen, and keep going together.

Bring your lament: honest prayer brings pain to God; lament is faith that keeps speaking. Doubt honestly: "I believe; help my unbelief!" Bring your questions to God and to trusted believers. Hope beyond what is seen: God is present, and he heals today, but continuing suffering is not weak faith, and resurrection is coming. And keep going together: be held by God and his people, through presence, practical help and prayer.

18 · Prayer

Let us pray. How long, O Lord? You know what I carry, and you know what I cannot put into words. I believe; help my unbelief. Hold me in your steadfast love, even when I cannot feel it. Let me not lose heart. Give me people to walk with, and give me grace for the next small step. Keep my eyes on the hope to come, when you will wipe away every tear. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

If you are thinking about ending your life, have made a plan, feel unable to keep yourself safe, or may harm yourself or someone else, seek urgent help now: contact local emergency or crisis services, stay with a safe person if you can, and do not manage this alone; if you are helping someone in immediate danger, contact emergency or crisis help and, if it is safe for you to do so, stay with them or help them stay with another safe person (Support Page: I may harm myself or someone else, or I cannot stay safe). If anyone tells you that your illness, disability, grief or loss is caused by a lack of faith, hidden sin or a curse, or pressures you to stop medical treatment, this is not faithful to Scripture, and you may decline such prayer and seek support elsewhere (Support Page: I am being controlled through prophecy, spiritual language, money or church authority). If you are suffering because of your faith, through threats, violence, loss of work or family pressure, your safety matters to God; seek wise counsel and specialist help where available. If your doubts come after harm in a church or by a Christian leader, your questions are understandable: take time, seek safe people, and remember that God is not the same as those who misused his name.
