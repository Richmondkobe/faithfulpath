Words, Anger, Conflict and Peacemaking

I say things I regret, especially when I am angry or online. And there is a conflict in my life I keep avoiding. How do Christians handle disagreements well?

You will learn

  • What it means to be "quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger", from James 1:19–20
  • Why Paul calls for words that build up, from Ephesians 4:25–32

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Words that wound, conflicts we avoid

Welcome to Lesson 9 of Grow. In Lesson 5, you learned to confess honestly and to protect with boundaries. In Lesson 3, you saw that gentleness is strength governed by love, not silence about wrongdoing. Now we look at how Christ shapes the way we listen, speak, handle anger and make peace.

Words can build up or tear down in seconds. A sharp reply, a careless message, a sarcastic post: these can damage relationships for years. At the same time, many of us avoid conflict altogether, hoping problems will disappear if we stay silent.

So here is our question: I say things I regret, especially when I am angry or online. And there is a conflict in my life I keep avoiding. How do Christians handle disagreements well?

2 · Quick to hear

Our first passage is James chapter 1, verses 19 and 20: "Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God."

Use the first question: what does it say? Notice the order: quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger.

3 · Words that give grace

Our second passage is Ephesians 4. Paul writes, "Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear."

Notice the reasons Paul gives. We are "members one of another". And we have been forgiven: "forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you." Kind words and forgiveness flow from the gospel, not from mere politeness.

4 · James and a church in conflict

Now the background. James was writing to believers facing trials, and divisions among themselves. Later he asks, "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you?"

Now use the second question: what did it mean to the people who first heard it? In context, "quick to hear" leads into receiving God's word and doing what it says. That posture of humble listening should also shape the way believers listen to one another. James warns that sinful human anger does not produce the righteous life God desires. Even when our concern is right, our methods can still become sinful.

5 · Members one of another

In the earlier chapters of Ephesians, Paul explains that Christ has made Jewish and Gentile believers into one new people. "For he himself is our peace," Paul writes, breaking down the dividing wall of hostility.

So when Paul says, "speak the truth", he gives the reason: "we are members one of another." Harsh words in the church are like one part of a body wounding another. And when Paul says, "Be angry and do not sin", he is quoting Psalm 4, from the prayer book of God's people.

6 · The short answer

So, how do Christians handle disagreements well? Here is the short answer.

Believers listen before they speak. They speak the truth in love, with words that build up. They can be angry without sinning. And they take wise, safe steps to make peace.

That gives us four parts: Quick to Listen; Speak Truth in Love; Angry Without Sinning; and Make Peace.

7 · Part 1: Quick to Listen

The first part: Quick to Listen.

Many conflicts grow because no one feels heard. "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame."

Listening means trying to understand the other person's view and feelings before answering. It means asking honest questions, such as, "Can you help me understand what concerns you?" And checking that we have understood: "So you are saying…?"

Listening does not mean we must agree. But people are far more able to hear us once they know we have heard them. And listening is a form of humility: it admits that we may not yet see the whole picture.

8 · Part 2: Speak Truth in Love

The second part: Speak Truth in Love.

"Having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor." Words have great power. James compares the tongue to a small fire: "How great a forest is set ablaze by such a small fire!" And Proverbs says, "There is one whose rash words are like sword thrusts, but the tongue of the wise brings healing."

So Christians avoid lying, gossip, slander, mockery and harsh words, and this includes what we write online. "Where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases."

9 · Hard truths, spoken with grace

"A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger." A gentle answer may calm ordinary tension, but it cannot guarantee another person's response. No one is responsible for another person's threats, violence or abuse because they failed to speak calmly enough.

Speaking the truth in love does not mean avoiding hard truths. It means speaking honestly, proportionately and without cruelty, seeking the good of the person, the protection of others and the health of the community. "Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt."

Christians differ on when correction should be private and when a public response is needed. You will find that under Go deeper.

10 · Part 3: Angry Without Sinning

The third part: Angry Without Sinning.

"Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil." Anger is not always sinful. Jesus "looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart" when they resisted doing good. Anger can arise in response to real evil and injustice. But unlike Jesus, our motives and responses are never beyond examination. Anger becomes sinful when it leads us to attack, control, humiliate, seek revenge or otherwise act against God's ways.

"Do not let the sun go down on your anger" does not set a strict deadline for resolving every conflict. It warns against nursing anger and letting it grow.

Healthy steps include pausing before speaking or posting; praying honestly about what we feel; naming what we feel, rather than acting it out; and addressing the issue, rather than attacking the person.

11 · Part 4: Make Peace

The fourth part: Make Peace.

"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God." If you remember that someone has something against you, Jesus says, leave your gift, "first be reconciled", and then come back. Where we have wronged someone, we should take responsibility and, where appropriate and safe, seek to make things right. In situations involving abuse, serious harm or safeguarding concerns, taking responsibility may require respecting the other person's boundaries rather than contacting them. This does not require a harmed person to approach someone dangerous.

"If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all." Peacemaking means taking appropriate steps: owning our part, without excuses; apologising specifically; listening; looking for fair solutions; and, where needed, asking a wise third person to help.

12 · When believers still disagree

Paul urged two church workers who disagreed, Euodia and Syntyche, "to agree in the Lord", and he asked others to help them.

Sometimes believers still disagree. Paul and Barnabas had "a sharp disagreement" and went separate ways. Acts does not tell us that they resolved every part of it. But years later Paul wrote of Mark, the man they had argued about, "he is very useful to me for ministry." Christian peace does not always require everyone to reach the same judgement about every matter.

Peace is not pretending. And it is never the same as giving in to abuse. Christians also differ about nonviolence and the use of force, and you will find that under Go deeper.

13 · Sipho's story

Let me tell you about Sipho. His story is a composite, drawn from the experiences of many people.

Sipho is a secondary-school mathematics teacher in Durban, South Africa. He serves on his church's building committee. At a meeting about repairing the church roof, he and another member, Mr Naidoo, disagreed sharply about the budget. That night Sipho posted a sarcastic message about "people who cannot count" in the committee's group chat. Mr Naidoo did not reply, and the next meeting was tense and silent.

Sipho felt justified, but James 1:19 and 20 kept coming to mind. He talked with his pastor, who asked, "Is this an ordinary disagreement? Do you feel safe talking to him?" Sipho said yes. "Then start by owning your part, without excuses," the pastor said.

Sipho phoned Mr Naidoo and said, "I am sorry for the message I posted. It was mocking and unkind, and I was wrong." He deleted the message, and posted a clear correction in the same group: "What I wrote about Mr Naidoo was unfair and disrespectful. Our disagreement is about the budget, not his intelligence." He did not pressure Mr Naidoo to accept the apology at once. Then he asked, "Can you explain your concern about the budget? I want to understand it properly." They met with the pastor present and listened to each other. They agreed on most of the plan, and agreed to ask a builder for a second quote on the part they still disagreed about.

Sipho and Mr Naidoo still see some things differently, but they speak respectfully, and the committee is working together again. Sipho now writes messages, but does not send them while angry. Sipho's approach fitted an ordinary disagreement between equals. Where there is abuse, threats or a serious imbalance of power, peacemaking does not require contact, meetings or an apology from the person harmed.

14 · What this lesson is not saying

Here are six common mistakes.

"All anger is sinful." No. Jesus was angry at hardness of heart; the question is what anger does.

"Christians should avoid all conflict to keep the peace." No. Peace is not pretending, and hard truths can be spoken in love.

"Speaking the truth means saying whatever I think." No. Truth is spoken with love, proportion and grace.

"If I answer gently enough, nothing bad will happen." No. No one is responsible for another person's threats or abuse.

"Peacemaking means meeting someone who has harmed me." No. It never requires contact with someone dangerous.

And "peace means everyone must agree." No. Believers may still disagree and remain respectful.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. We listen first, speak truth in love, are angry without sinning, and make peace wisely and safely.

Quick to listen: seek to understand before answering, and check you have understood. Speak truth in love: honest, kind, proportionate words that build up, including online. Angry without sinning: anger is not always sin; pause, pray, name it, and address the issue, not the person. And make peace: own your part, apologise specifically, listen and seek fair solutions, and never give in to abuse.

17 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you are our peace. Make me quick to listen and slow to speak. Guard my words, including what I write and post, so that they build up and give grace. When I am angry, keep me from sin. Where I have wronged someone, give me courage to own my part. And make me a wise and safe peacemaker. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Pause and seek help

If someone regularly frightens, threatens, controls, humiliates or harms you, this is abuse, not ordinary conflict: peacemaking never means giving in, staying silent or meeting them alone, and joint counselling or mediation is not appropriate (Support Page: I am in danger or being abused). If your own anger has frightened, threatened or hurt others, take it seriously and seek specialist help alongside pastoral care; do not demand forgiveness or contact from those affected, and respect the boundaries they set. If you are bullied or harassed at work, school, church or online, keep a record and, where safe, save evidence (dates, usernames, links and screenshots) before blocking or reporting, and use reporting routes; do not respond with threats or revenge. If you think someone is monitoring your devices, seek specialist advice before changing settings in ways that might alert them.

Optional

Go deeper

Correcting others in public or in private. Some Christians believe that correction should almost always happen privately first, especially online, to protect reputations and allow repentance (they often point to Matthew 18:15 and Galatians 6:1). Others believe that public wrongs, especially false teaching or misconduct by public leaders, may require a public response (they often point to Galatians 2:11–14 and 1 Timothy 5:20). Public accountability is not the same as public shaming; 1 Timothy 5 places public rebuke alongside credible evidence and fair process (5:19–21). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that correction should be truthful, fair, proportionate and humble, and that gossip and mockery are wrong. Safeguarding concerns must go through proper reporting routes, not private conversation or online exposure.

Nonviolence and the use of force. Some Christians believe followers of Jesus should never use violence, even in self-defence or war (they often point to Matthew 5:38–44 and Romans 12:17–21). Others believe that lawful authorities may use force to restrain evil and protect the innocent, and that Christians may serve in such roles (they often point to Romans 13:1–4 and Luke 3:14). Across historic Christian traditions, Christians hold that personal revenge is forbidden, enemies are to be loved, and peace is God's goal. If it would help, ask a trustworthy church leader how your church approaches these matters, and compare what you hear with Scripture.
