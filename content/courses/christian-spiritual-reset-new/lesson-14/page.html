1 · What small pattern will carry me?

Welcome back. By now you may have kept a rhythm for a week or two, imperfectly, and done some of the cards. Or you may still be finding your feet. Either way, this lesson is about turning a rhythm into a small pattern that can carry you: what older Christian traditions call a rule of life.

You can begin shaping it over the next two or three weeks, then continue adjusting it as ordinary life unfolds. It should be built in ordinary life, not on retreat, because ordinary life is where it has to work. So this lesson asks: what small pattern will carry me?

2 · Made for man

Our first passage is from Mark’s Gospel. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. One Sabbath, Jesus’ disciples plucked heads of grain as they walked through a field, and the Pharisees objected. Jesus answered them with the story of David, and then, in Mark chapter 2, verses 27 and 28:

"And he said to them, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. So the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.”"

3 · Remember that you were a slave

Our second passage is the Sabbath command as Moses repeated it in Deuteronomy. It tells Israel to observe the Sabbath, to work six days, and to give the seventh to rest for everyone, children, servants, animals and strangers alike. Then it gives the reason. Deuteronomy chapter 5, verse 15:

"You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. Therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day."

4 · Devoted, and as he had done previously

And two short passages about patterns of life. The first describes the church in Jerusalem after Pentecost. Acts chapter 2, verse 42:

"And they devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers."

The second is about Daniel, an exile serving under a foreign ruler, after a law was signed forbidding prayer to anyone but the king. Daniel chapter 6, verse 10:

"When Daniel knew that the document had been signed, he went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously."

5 · Recall: šāḇaṯ

There is no new word in this lesson. Instead, recall the one from Lesson 2. In Genesis 2, God rested on the seventh day, and the word translated "rested" is šāḇaṯ. At its heart it means to cease, to stop working. It is the root of the word "Sabbath". Keep that meaning in mind. The Sabbath is first of all a stopping.

6 · What Jesus did with the Sabbath

Now the background. By Jesus’ time, some interpretations of the Sabbath had added burdens to it, and much of his conflict with the religious leaders was over those interpretations. He healed on the Sabbath repeatedly and defended it: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good or to do harm, to save life or to kill?" Jesus rejected interpretations of the Sabbath that undermined mercy and human flourishing.

And notice the two reasons Scripture gives for the Sabbath. In Exodus 20, which you met in Lesson 2, the reason is creation: rest because God rested. In Deuteronomy 5, the reason is liberation: rest because you were slaves, and are no longer. Both reasons still hold. Sabbath declares that the world was made and is held by God. And it declares that you are not a slave to your work, your phone, or anyone’s expectations.

7 · Daniel at his window

Daniel is our portrait for this lesson. He prayed three times a day at his window, "as he had done previously". His pattern was so reliable that his enemies used it against him. They knew exactly where he would be. That is what a rule of life looks like from the outside: not intensity, but faithfulness that others could set a clock by.

And Acts 2:42 is a rule of life in one sentence: teaching, fellowship, the table, and prayer. Christians have made such patterns ever since. Benedict’s sixth-century Rule sought an ordered community life of prayer, work, reading, and rest, while expressing the intention to establish nothing harsh or burdensome. But the idea is much simpler than a monastery.

8 · The short answer

So, what small pattern will carry me? Here is the short answer. A few chosen commitments that support your life rather than judge it, with a regular day of ceasing at the centre, written on one page, and revised as you go.

That gives us four points: a trellis, not a cage; Sabbath at the centre; three core commitments; and review and resume.

9 · Point 1: A Trellis, Not a Cage

The first point: a trellis, not a cage. The word "rule" puts some people off, and it should be explained. It comes from the Latin regula, a straightedge, a guide by which something is given shape. A helpful picture is a trellis: a structure along which a living thing can grow without being crushed. A rule of life, in that sense, is not a set of laws. It is a small, chosen pattern of prayer, rest, work, and relationship that gives your life a shape it will not find by drifting.

A rule is not legalism. Paul wrote:

"For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery."

Paul was writing about freedom in Christ, against returning to the law as a way of being made right with God, not about devotional timetables. But the principle applies.

A rule that becomes a way of earning God’s approval, or a measuring stick you beat yourself with, has stopped being a trellis and become a cage, and it should be torn up. Lesson 2 said that rest declares that God, not you, holds the world together. A rule of life is the same declaration, made every week.

10 · The danger of drift

For many burned-out people, though, the more immediate danger is not legalism but drift. Without a chosen pattern, the loudest demand wins every day, and prayer, rest, and the people you love are what get squeezed. A rule is how you decide in advance what will not be squeezed. It is not a burden added to a full life. It is a fence around the parts of life that were being trampled.

11 · Point 2: Sabbath at the Centre

The second point: Sabbath at the centre. Session 10 promised that this part of the course would give Sabbath the fuller treatment it deserves. This course strongly recommends a regular period of worship, ceasing, rest, and delight as the centre of any sustainable Christian rhythm.

Remember the two reasons. Creation: the world is made and held by God, and he built a rhythm of ceasing into it. And liberation: you are no longer a slave. And remember what Jesus said: the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. It is a gift for your good, not a test you must pass.

12 · Christians differ

Christians differ about the Sabbath, and we should acknowledge those differences respectfully. Some observe the seventh day, Saturday, as the Sabbath. Many worship on Sunday, the Lord’s Day, although they differ over whether Sunday should be observed as a Christian Sabbath. Others understand the Sabbath command as fulfilled in Christ, and do not believe Christians are required to observe a particular day. These differences involve how Christians understand the relationship between the Old Testament command and Christ’s fulfilment of the law.

This course does not require you to adopt one position. Follow Scripture conscientiously, consider your church’s teaching, and respect believers who differ. Whatever your conviction about a particular day, make room for worship, rest, mercy, and a sustainable rhythm of life.

13 · How to practise it

How do you practise it? Choose a period, ideally a full day, and if that is impossible, a half-day, or evening to evening, in which you stop your ordinary work. Not only paid work: the work of managing, fixing, improving, and producing. Then fill the time with what Sabbath is for. Worship, if the day allows it. Rest, including sleep. Delight: the things that restore you and that you never have time for. And people, unhurried. Keep the phone boundary from Lesson 9. Do not use the day to catch up.

And Sabbath is not neglect. Necessary care, acts of mercy, emergencies, and responsibilities that genuinely cannot be postponed may remain. Jesus’ Sabbath healings settle that. The aim is to cease avoidable production and management, not to become unavailable to human need.

14 · Three practical problems

Three practical problems. If Sunday is your work day, as it is for pastors, church staff, and many shift workers, protect another regular period for rest, just as deliberately as you protect your responsibilities to others. Christians differ about whether this should be called Sabbath observance, but the need for rest remains. If you are a caregiver and cannot stop caring for a day, then if safe respite is available, receiving it is not selfish. If it is not available, look for Sabbath fragments: a protected hour, help with one task, prayer while another person sits with the one you care for. Receive those limits without guilt. And if a full day is impossible now, start with a protected half-day, and let it grow. A half-day kept for a year is worth more than a full day intended and never taken.

Sabbath will cost you something at first: the things that do not get done, the sense of falling behind. That cost is the point. Each time you stop, you practise the truth that your responsibility has limits, and that God remains God while you rest. The manna in Exodus 16 did not fall on the seventh day, and the people who trusted that had enough.

15 · Point 3: Three Core Commitments

The third point: three core commitments. Around the Sabbath, a rule of life covers a few other areas. What follows is a suggested framework for this course, not a biblical formula. The rule should fit on one page, and three to five commitments in total is enough. Begin with the three core areas.

First, regular ceasing and rest: the Sabbath practice, and sleep. Protect a realistic sleep window where possible, follow any medical care you need, and do not sacrifice sleep merely to complete devotional practices. Second, prayer and Scripture: a fixed time, however short, most days. The Lesson 8 practice, a psalm and a few minutes of silence, is a complete daily pattern for a tired person. The fixed time matters more than the length. Daniel’s window was about the fixedness. Third, one limit that protects your health or calling, usually a work limit: a number of hours, an evening that is off. The Jethro conversation in Lesson 13 may have produced it. Write it into the rule, so that it is a decision made once rather than fifty times a week.

Then, only if they help, add at most two optional lines. A relationship you will tend: church first, a community you belong to and are known in, not only one you serve. If you are recovering from church harm, name one safe form of Christian connection you can sustain while you seek a healthy community. Or the body, from Lesson 10. Or screens, from Lesson 9, named exactly, so that it is a rule and not a wish. A screen boundary can also sit inside one of the core lines, as it does in the example that follows, rather than needing a line of its own.

16 · Writing it

Here is an example, deliberately unimpressive, because unimpressive rules are the ones that get kept. Regular ceasing and rest: Saturday noon to evening; no paid work or email; eat with family and walk. The phone stays outside the bedroom. Prayer and Scripture: a psalm and five minutes of prayer at seven in the morning, Monday to Friday. One limit: laptop closed by seven in the evening. And at the bottom, one line: "When I miss something, I will resume at the next appropriate opportunity without punishment, compensation, or guilt."

"Without compensation" matters. Some of you will try to repay a missed Sabbath with a double one, or a missed morning with an hour of penitential prayer. Do not. Resume.

17 · Two cautions

Two cautions. Do not write the rule you wish you had the capacity for. Write the one you can keep in a difficult ordinary week, and let it grow at the reviews. And do not write it alone if you share a life. A Sabbath that your spouse or household does not know about will collide with theirs.

Then read the page to God, and share it, or the commitments you want accountability for, with your Eli or support person. Ask them to ask you about it at the review dates. You decide what personal details they need to see. A rule that nobody else knows about is easy to abandon quietly.

18 · Point 4: Review and Resume

The fourth point: review and resume. At two weeks, a light check: is this livable? If the rule already feels like a cage, remove something immediately. You do not need to wait for the review date to subtract.

At three months, sit down with the page and your Eli and ask four questions. What have I kept? What have I dropped, and why? What has changed in my life that the rule needs to reflect? What one thing would I add or remove? Then rewrite the page with the new date. A rule of life is not written once. It is revised for the rest of your life, and every revision is a small act of the same trust that Sabbath is: that God holds the world, and that you can afford to stop and look at how you are living in it.

19 · Your step

Here is your step, which is also below this lesson and on the worksheet. Write your one-page rule of life this week. Your regular ceasing, and the first thing you will have to stop doing to protect it. Your prayer and Scripture, at a fixed time. One limit that protects your health or calling. At most two optional lines, or none. Then the resume line, a date for the two-week check, a date for the three-month review, and the person you will share it with.

Finally, read it through once more and ask: is there anything here that is already a cage rather than a trellis? Cross it out.

20 · Remember

Let us bring it together. A rule of life is a trellis, not a cage: a shape to grow along, not a way to earn approval. Sabbath is at the centre: a regular ceasing, made for you. Three core commitments on one page, small enough for a difficult ordinary week. And review and resume, without punishment, compensation, or guilt.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord of the Sabbath, you rested, and you set your people free. Give me a small pattern I can keep, and the humility to stop each week. When I miss it, let me resume without fear. Amen.

When you are ready, take one page and write the first line: your regular ceasing. The rest can follow.
