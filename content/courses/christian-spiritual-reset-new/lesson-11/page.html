1 · What if silence frightens me, or nothing happens?

Welcome back. This is the last lesson of Part 2. Everything so far has been about arrangements: a place, a length, an intention, a boundary, a bag, and a plan for your body. This lesson is about the thing those arrangements are for: silence.

Most of the sessions ahead contain periods of silence, some short, some longer. For some people, silence sounds like relief. For others, it brings unease, dread, or even a sense of threat, and they may hesitate to admit it, because fear of quiet can seem unspiritual. So let me say it plainly. The fear is common. It is not a sign of weak faith. And there are practical ways to work with it.

So this lesson asks: what if silence frightens me, or nothing happens?

2 · Always adjustable

Before anything else, hold on to this. The silence is always adjustable. You may shorten it, open your eyes, move, add neutral background sound, contact your support person, or stop entirely. Mild restlessness is not failure, and severe distress is not something you must endure to prove your faith.

And if you cannot engage safely in silence at all, spoken prayer, Scripture read aloud, gentle walking, or the company of a trusted person are all valid alternatives.

3 · For God alone

Our first passage is from Psalm 62. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Psalm 62, verses 1 and 2:

"For God alone my soul waits in silence; from him comes my salvation. He alone is my rock and my salvation, my fortress; I shall not be greatly shaken."

And verse 5:

"For God alone, O my soul, wait in silence, for my hope is from him."

4 · Seven days and seven nights

And our second passage comes from the book of Job, after Job has lost his children, his wealth and his health. His three friends came to comfort him. Job chapter 2, verse 13:

"And they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great."

5 · In silence = dûmiyyâ

Our Hebrew word in this lesson comes from verse 1: "For God alone my soul waits in silence." The word translated "in silence" is dûmiyyâ. It means silence, or quiet waiting.

It is related to dāmam, the Hebrew verb we met in Lesson 8. And in verse 5, the psalmist uses a command form of that verb, calling his own soul to be silent before God and wait for him. Once again, the quietness is chosen. He is not silent because he has nothing to say. He is silent because he has decided to wait.

6 · Silence and speech

Now the background. Psalm 62 begins and returns to silence, but it is not a silent psalm. In between it is full of words: about enemies, about trust, and an invitation to God’s people to pour out their hearts before him. Silence and speech alternate. Neither is the whole of prayer.

That is how Scripture treats silence before God: as a natural posture, not a strange one. The passages in this lesson come from different settings, and they do not make a technique. But together they show that silence can go with reverence, with waiting, with grief, and with faithful presence.

7 · Silent friends, and quiet waiting

The book of Job gives us silence as companionship. When the friends first saw Job’s suffering, they sat with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights, and said nothing. Those days of silent presence may have been the closest they came to giving Job what he needed. Their later speeches did far more harm. Sometimes silence is not about hearing anything. It is about being present.

And Lamentations, written after the fall of Jerusalem, offers silence as endurance:

"It is good that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the LORD. It is good for a man that he bear the yoke in his youth. Let him sit alone in silence when it is laid on him;"

This is not comfortable silence. It is the silence of someone who has been through devastation, and is learning to wait without demanding an explanation. But it is not an instruction to stay silent about abuse, injustice or danger. Biblical waiting never forbids seeking safety, speaking the truth, lamenting aloud, or asking others for help.

8 · The short answer

So, what if silence frightens me, or nothing happens? Here is the short answer. Name what you fear, without shame. Remember that silence is attention, not emptiness. Start small, and hold an anchor. And if you sense nothing, you have not failed.

That gives us four points: why silence unsettles us; silence is attention, not emptiness; start small, use an anchor; and if you sense nothing.

9 · Point 1: Why Silence Unsettles Us

The first point: why silence unsettles us. Silence is rarely experienced as empty. When the noise around us drops, we notice thoughts, sensations and feelings that were already there. Sometimes we use noise to avoid what we do not want to face. At other times, sound helps us concentrate, feel safe, or manage a health condition. The point is not to condemn every use of sound, but to understand what it does for you.

The fears vary, and it helps to name yours. Some people fear boredom. Some fear their own thoughts: the worries, the self-criticism, the memories that arrive when there is nothing to hold them back. Some fear what they will feel if they stop performing. Some fear that they will listen for God and sense nothing. And some fear the opposite: that something will surface that they would rather avoid.

And for some people, particularly those with a history of trauma, silence and stillness bring the body to a state of alarm that has nothing to do with faith, and everything to do with what happened to them. None of these fears is foolish. They deserve care, not shame. The question is not how to make the fear go away before you begin. It is whether silence is helpful and safe for you now. You may begin gently, use another form of prayer, or seek support before trying again.

10 · One clarification

One clarification belongs here, because it matters. If during the retreat you hear a voice that others cannot hear, particularly one giving commands, threatening you, or making it hard to tell what is inside you from what is outside, do not assume it is God. End the retreat, and seek prompt professional assessment.

11 · Point 2: Silence Is Attention, Not Emptiness

The second point: silence is attention, not emptiness. The silence this retreat asks for is not an attempt to empty your mind, or reach a state without thought. It is attention. You are not trying to become blank. You are trying to become present to God, who is already present to you. The sessions will nearly always give you something to hold: a line of Scripture, a phrase of prayer, a question, or the intention you wrote in Lesson 7.

And silence does not mean soundlessness. Birds, traffic, household noise, or a gentle background sound can remain. The boundary is about deliberate input and conversation, not every sound. For someone with tinnitus, total quiet can make the ringing louder, and a fan or low neutral sound may make prayer easier.

12 · When thoughts come

If thoughts come, and they will, you do not have to fight them. You notice them, and return to what you were holding. If a thought keeps returning, write it down if that helps. But do not treat its repetition as proof that God is emphasising it. You can decide later, perhaps with wise counsel, whether it deserves attention.

Unwanted or disturbing thoughts are not confessions, intentions, prophecies, or evidence of your character simply because they appeared in silence. Do not analyse every thought spiritually. And if religious fears or compulsive self-examination keep returning, seek help from a professional who understands scrupulosity. Silence is not a battle against your mind. It is a gentle, repeated returning.

13 · Three forms of silence

There are three acceptable forms of silence. Still silence: sitting with Scripture or a prayer phrase. Good for those comfortable with quiet. Moving silence: slow walking or gentle movement, without digital input. Good for restlessness, or differences in attention. And supported silence: eyes open, a neutral sound, a trusted person nearby. Good for anxiety, sensitivity after trauma, or accessibility needs.

None of these is more spiritual than the others. Silence does not require physical stillness. You may walk, rock gently, hold a quiet object, draw, or keep a low background sound, if that helps you stay present. Adaptation is not avoidance, when it makes attention possible.

14 · Point 3: Start Small, Use an Anchor

The third point: start small, and use an anchor. You do not have to begin with thirty minutes. If silence frightens you, you should not. In Lesson 8 you began with up to three minutes. Every suggested length in the sessions is adjustable. So begin with one to five minutes. If that feels manageable, add a few minutes in a later session. Stay at the same length for as long as you need.

There is no spiritual requirement to reach thirty minutes. Ten attentive minutes can be a complete practice. If a session suggests thirty and you can manage ten, do ten, and count it as the session. God is not measuring your devotion with a stopwatch. Use a timer, so that you are not watching the clock. Choose a length before you begin, but treat it as a plan, not a contract. You remain free to stop, shorten, move, or seek help at any time.

15 · Anchors

An anchor is something you return to when your attention drifts. It makes silence far less frightening, because you are never simply adrift. The best anchor is a short line of Scripture, ideally from the session’s passage. "For God alone my soul waits in silence." "Here I am." Or, "Be still, and know that I am God." That line from Psalm 46 first addressed nations in conflict, calling them to stop and recognise God’s rule. It can still remind you, on retreat, that you do not hold the world together. Say your line slowly and inwardly when you notice you have wandered, and then let it go again.

Some people anchor to their breathing, simply noticing it. If that helps you, use it. If focusing on your breath makes you more anxious, as it does for some, do not. Feel your feet on the floor. Notice the chair holding you. Hold a textured object. Look around and name a few neutral things you can see.

You do not have to close your eyes. For many people, silence with eyes open, resting on a window, a tree, a cross or a candle, is far easier than silence in the dark. And choose a posture that is stable and supported. There is no spiritually superior position. If you lie down and fall asleep, that may be the rest your body needs.

16 · Discomfort or distress?

Before you begin, know the difference between the ordinary discomfort of silence and something that means you should stop. Only you can judge it in the moment. Mild restlessness, boredom, wandering attention, an urge to check something, or a mild sadness as things surface, can usually be managed. Notice what is happening, and return gently to your anchor. But you are never required to stay with anything that feels unsafe, unfamiliar, or quickly getting stronger.

Distress feels different. Panic. A racing heart that does not settle. A sense of being back inside a memory, rather than remembering it. A feeling of unreality, or of watching yourself from outside. A fear out of all proportion to sitting in a quiet room. That is not something to push through. Stop the silence. Open your eyes, stand up, move. Use the exercise from Lesson 5: name where you are, the date, and several things you can see, hear and touch. Contact your support person if you need to.

If you are in immediate danger, or have chest pain, fainting or severe difficulty breathing, call emergency services for urgent medical help. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, or feel you cannot stay safe, contact emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Emotional distress without immediate danger may call for a qualified professional or a crisis support service. And if distress happens more than once, or severely, do not continue the silent parts of the retreat alone. A trauma-informed professional can help you find ways into quiet that your body will accept. Nothing in this retreat is worth reliving harm. Silence is offered, not assigned.

17 · Point 4: If You Sense Nothing

The fourth point: if you sense nothing. This is the fear many people hold most quietly: that they will be silent, and faithful, and attentive, and nothing will happen. It may not. Lesson 5 said so, and I will not soften it now.

Some retreats are marked by a strong sense of God’s presence. Others are marked by faithfulness without feeling. Both are real retreats, and Scripture makes room for both. If you sit in silence and sense nothing, you have not failed, and you have not been abandoned. Christian confidence rests in God’s character and promises, not in whether a retreat produces a response you can feel. You have waited, as the psalmist waited, and the waiting itself is prayer.

In my experience as a pastor, some people recognise the significance of a retreat only later. A decision becomes clearer, a pattern becomes visible, or a quieter change grows over the weeks that follow. I cannot promise that. But immediate emotion is not the only measure of value, and a retreat is not judged by what you felt on day two.

18 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 11 worksheet. Your main step is to choose your way into quiet. First, your starting length: what can you honestly manage right now? Start there. Second, your form of silence: still, moving or supported, with eyes open or closed. Decide before you begin, and give yourself permission to change. And third, your anchor phrase for the first session. Write it on a card, and keep it in your Bible.

As you prepare, one more question: which of the fears in this lesson is closest to yours? Write it in a sentence.

19 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Silence unsettles us for many reasons, and naming your fear is the beginning, not a failure. Silence is attention, not emptiness: something to hold, in the presence of God who is already there. Start small, use an anchor, and remember that every length is a plan, not a contract. And if you sense nothing, you have still waited, as the psalmist waited. The waiting itself is prayer.

20 · Prayer

After this prayer, there will be ten seconds of quiet. You may remain with it, keep your eyes open, or simply continue when you are ready.
