1 · How do I protect these days?

Welcome back. In Lesson 6 we asked four ordinary questions: when, where, for how long, and what will I do with my phone? This lesson answers the last one, and then helps you pack.

You can have three protected days and a good place, and still spend them checking. So this lesson is about drawing a boundary you can actually keep, one that removes the noise without removing your access to help. And it is not an anti-technology lesson. Your phone may carry your Bible, your map, and the number of your support person. The goal is not to reject the device. It is to stop it being the centre of your attention for a few days, so that something else can be.

So this lesson asks: how do I protect these days without cutting myself off from help?

2 · Begin where you are

You have a length and a place. In Lesson 8 you began the seven-psalm week and handed over your responsibilities. This lesson covers two short chapters of the book: the digital boundary, and what to bring. Both are practical, and both protect the same thing: your attention, for God.

3 · Martha and Mary

Our first passage is a short scene in Luke’s Gospel. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Luke chapter 10, verses 38 to 42:

"Now as they went on their way, Jesus entered a village. And a woman named Martha welcomed him into her house. And she had a sister called Mary, who sat at the Lord’s feet and listened to his teaching. But Martha was distracted with much serving. And she went up to him and said, “Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to serve alone? Tell her then to help me.” But the Lord answered her, “Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her.”"

4 · Not dominated

And two short verses from Paul. First Corinthians chapter 6, verse 12:

"“All things are lawful for me,” but not all things are helpful. “All things are lawful for me,” but I will not be dominated by anything."

And Second Timothy chapter 4, verse 13, written from prison:

"When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas, also the books, and above all the parchments."

5 · Distracted = perispaō

Our Greek word in this lesson comes from verse 40: "But Martha was distracted with much serving." The word translated "distracted" is perispaō. It means to be drawn away, or pulled around in several directions. Luke uses a form that describes something ongoing: Martha was being drawn away by much serving.

Two things are worth noticing. First, the word describes Martha’s attention in that moment. It is not a modern psychological label, and we should not turn it into one. Second, her service itself was good. Welcoming Jesus into her home was a good thing to do. The problem was not the serving. It was being pulled away from the one thing that mattered most at that moment.

6 · One thing is necessary

Now the background. In that world, welcoming a guest was an important duty, and Martha took it seriously. Mary, meanwhile, sat at Jesus’ feet, the place of someone learning from a teacher. When Martha complained, Jesus did not scold her for serving. He said her name twice, gently, and told her that she was anxious and troubled about many things, but that one thing was necessary.

Martha’s situation was not about technology. But her story gives us a helpful picture of good and legitimate activity crowding out attentive presence. A phone can do something very similar during a retreat. Nothing on it may be wrong. It can still pull you away, again and again, from the one thing you came for.

7 · A cloak and the books

Paul’s first verse comes from a letter to Corinth. Many scholars think "All things are lawful for me" was a saying some in the church were using to excuse their behaviour. The ESV presents those words in quotation marks, reflecting this interpretation. Paul was writing about sexual immorality, and he answers the saying: but not all things are helpful, and I will not be dominated by anything.

His words were not written about phones, and we use them here as an analogy, not as their direct meaning. But the principle invites a useful question: has something permissible gained more control over my attention than I intended? If the thought of turning your phone off produces something close to anxiety, that is worth noticing. Not as a reason for shame, but as information.

And the second verse is one of the most human in the New Testament. Near the end of his life, in prison, Paul asks Timothy to bring his cloak, and his books, and above all the parchments. Warmth for his body, and the written words that mattered to him. Physical care and attentive reading still belong together, and that will guide what you pack.

8 · The short answer

So, how do I protect these days without cutting myself off from help? Here is the short answer. Draw a boundary around your attention, not a test of your willpower. Choose one arrangement for your phone, and test it. Bring what you truly need. And leave behind what would pull you away.

That gives us four points: a boundary, not a test; two arrangements that work; what to bring; and what to leave behind.

9 · Point 1: A Boundary, Not a Test

The first point: a boundary, not a test. Most of us underestimate how much of our inner life is now shaped by a screen. The phone is often the first thing we touch in the morning and the last at night. It fills every gap: the queue, the lift, the two minutes before a meeting, the moment of boredom, the moment of discomfort. Those gaps used to be places where thoughts surfaced, where prayer began, where we noticed how we actually felt.

A retreat is, in large part, the deliberate reopening of those gaps. That is why silence is so central to the sessions ahead, and why the phone can be the most persistent threat to your attention. Every time you reach for it, you close the gap again.

So the boundary has one purpose: to protect your attention for God during the time you have set aside. It is not a test of willpower, or a spiritual fast to be proud of. If you slip and check something, you have not failed. You have noticed, and you can put it down again. And it is not a boundary against help. As Lesson 5 said, emergency calling should stay available.

10 · Point 2: Two Arrangements That Work

The second point: two arrangements that work. There are two fundamentally different ways to do this, and you need to choose one, because they do not combine. A phone in airplane mode cannot ordinarily receive a call, so airplane mode with an emergency channel on the same phone is not an arrangement at all.

Option A: remain reachable. Keep your mobile service on. Use your phone’s Focus or Do Not Disturb setting to silence all apps and people except one or two designated emergency contacts, who can call through. Turn off badges and previews, and keep the phone face down, or in another room. This is usually the right choice for parents, caregivers, and anyone with safety needs.

Option B: disconnect. The phone stays off, or in airplane mode with Wi-Fi off. Because you cannot receive calls, you need another emergency route: someone nearby, such as the host, the retreat centre, or a person on site, who can receive an emergency call and reach you in person. Option B works only if that person is genuinely available for the whole retreat, including overnight. A host who leaves at five in the evening is not a dependable route at night. You can add scheduled checks to Option B if you wish, but scheduled checks on their own do not make you reachable in an emergency.

11 · Test it, tell people, define emergency

Whichever you choose, test it before the retreat. For Option A, ask your emergency contact to make a real test call while the settings are on, and check that their call rings and everything else stays silent. Settings vary and change with updates, so if it fails, fix it before you go. For Option B, confirm that the person nearby can receive an emergency call, and that they can physically reach you.

Then tell people. Send a short message to those who normally reach you: "I am away from Thursday to Sunday and will not be checking messages. If something urgent comes up, contact" and then the name of your emergency contact, "who can reach me." That one message removes most of the anxiety, because the people who might need you know what to do.

Choose one emergency contact, and define emergency together, out loud. A serious health or safety problem, an urgent issue with a dependant, or something that truly cannot wait. Not ordinary questions, routine work decisions, or matters that someone else can resolve. Ask them to call rather than send a message, and agree what they should do if you do not answer.

12 · The rest of the arrangement

A few more parts of the arrangement. If you can, read Scripture from a printed Bible, because a Bible app sits beside the very apps that compete for your attention. If you use your phone, turn notifications off, and be honest with yourself about how it goes on day one. Print or download the session pages in advance too.

Leave the other screens off: the television, the laptop, the tablet with email, the smartwatch. It saves you making the same decision fifty times a day. And do not narrate the retreat publicly while you are living it. Posting turns attention towards an audience, and it can reveal your location. Before you leave, download what you need, turn off previews and badges, write essential numbers on paper, and pack the charger.

13 · Music, and what to expect

Is worship music allowed? Yes, in a limited way. If music helps you enter prayer, use it. But the default of the retreat is silence, so try not to fill every quiet moment. Set podcasts, sermons and audiobooks aside, unless a session directs you to one.

And here is what to expect when you stop. You may reach for the phone without deciding to. That is habit, not weakness. You may feel a low restlessness with no obvious cause. And your mind may be louder than you expected, as thoughts held down by the constant stream begin to surface. Write down what surfaces; the sessions will give it somewhere to go.

But if the experience becomes severe or destabilising, do not treat it as spiritual progress you must endure. Shorten the silence, keep your eyes open, move gently, contact your support person, or pause the retreat, as Lesson 5 described.

14 · Other formats, and a written agreement

If you are using another format, adjust. For a one-day retreat, keep the boundary for the whole day. For a three-hour reset, keep the phone out of sight, and use Option A if you must stay reachable. At home, the realistic version is a boundary around each session: the phone in another room, or Option A, for the session time.

Then write it down, because a boundary on paper is easier to keep than one held vaguely in mind. Below this lesson, and on the worksheet, there is a short written agreement: Option A or Option B, your emergency route, when you tested it, who you have told, and how you will read Scripture. It ends: if I slip, I will put the phone down and return to what I was doing, without guilt.

If you are retreating with a spouse or a group, talk about expectations in advance. Each person states their own boundary. Encourage one another, without monitoring, shaming, or confiscating devices.

15 · Point 3: What to Bring

The third point: what to bring. A retreat that needs a great deal of equipment has already become something else. But a few things matter, and forgetting them can cost you an afternoon, or more seriously, leave you without medication or a way to call for help. Remember Paul: his cloak, and his books.

For the sessions, bring a Bible, the session pages, and a journal with two pens. Handwriting often slows us down, but if it is difficult for you, use an offline document or a voice recorder that does not open work or messages. Bring the pages you have already written: your check-in from Lesson 4, your intention from Lesson 7, and your notes from Lesson 8. Session 1 will ask for them. Bring a simple timer or watch, so you do not have to pick up your phone to time the silence, and a few index cards.

If something helps you settle, a small cross, a blanket, a favourite mug, bring it. None of these is a spiritual necessity. If you use a candle, a battery one is the safest. And decide how you will keep your journal private, especially in a shared home or a group. Do not write identifying details if that could put you or someone else at risk.

16 · For your body, and for safety

Then, for your body. Bring enough prescribed medication for the retreat and any reasonable delay, and do not alter doses or stop medication for the retreat without medical advice. Keep essential medical information where emergency responders can find it. And bring the aids and equipment you normally rely on: glasses, hearing aids, mobility aids, a CPAP machine, insulin supplies. A retreat is not the time to prove that you can manage without them.

Bring comfortable clothes in layers, because sitting still makes people cold, and shoes you can walk in. Bring whatever helps you sleep well, and do not experiment with unfamiliar sleep aids. Lesson 10 will say more about food. For now, bring simple food that meets your needs, and enough water.

And for safety, carry a paper card with the address where you are staying, your emergency contact, the host’s number, your doctor’s number, and the emergency number for the country you are in. If your phone fails, this card is your backup. Bring identification, your booking details, some cash or a card, and confirm your transport in both directions.

17 · Point 4: What to Leave Behind

The fourth point: what to leave behind. This list is as important as the other one. Leave your work and work devices behind, unless a device is needed for accessibility, Scripture, the session pages, or emergency contact. If you must bring one, sign out of work accounts and keep it offline. The retreat has room for you to think about your work. It does not have room for you to do it.

Leave unfinished projects behind: finish, delegate or reschedule what is genuinely urgent before you go, including next Sunday’s sermon if that is yours to give. Keep other books to a minimum; do not pack a stack of books to protect yourself from silence. And leave behind objects that predictably bring guilt, compulsive work, or a sense that you must perform for someone.

Finally, if guilt about leaving returns while you pack, remember Lesson 2, and write down what the guilt is saying. You do not need to eliminate the feeling before you go. You only need to keep it from making the decision for you.

18 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 9 worksheet. Your main step is to fill in the written digital agreement, and test your emergency arrangement before you go. Write down which arrangement you are choosing, who your emergency contact is, and when you will test it.

As you prepare, four more questions. First, what is your honest reaction to the thought of several days without your phone? Write it down without judging it. Second, how will you read Scripture and the session pages during the retreat? If the answer is on my phone, what will you do to make that workable?

Third, go through the packing checklist and mark anything you do not yet have. Which missing items are essential? And fourth, is there anything on the leave-behind list you are reluctant to leave? Write down what it is, and what you are afraid will happen if you do not bring it.

19 · Remember

Let us bring it together. The boundary protects your attention; it is not a test of your willpower. Choose one of the two arrangements, and test it before you go. Bring what you truly need, for body and soul, like Paul’s cloak and books. And leave behind your work, your projects, and the guilt that would like to make the decision for you.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you told Martha, "One thing is necessary." I am anxious and troubled about many things. Help me to set down what pulls me away, to keep what keeps me safe, and to give these days my attention, for you. Amen.

A safety note

Never a boundary against help

Keep emergency calling available in every arrangement. If silence or the absence of your usual input brings panic, flashbacks, dissociation or severe distress, do not treat it as progress you must endure: shorten the silence, keep your eyes open, move gently, contact your support person, or pause the retreat.

Do not change medication doses or routines for the retreat without medical advice.
