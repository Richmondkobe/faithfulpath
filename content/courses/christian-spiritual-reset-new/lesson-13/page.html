1 · How do I make the retreat real in ordinary life?

Welcome back. This lesson is for your first week home. Lesson 12 dealt with impressions and major directions that need careful testing. This lesson is about the steps you have already identified as low-risk, responsible, and reversible: the Act, Share, and Seek-counsel cards from Session 3, the action and the person from Session 10, and, if you did Session 9, your one faithful thing.

If any card turns out to be more consequential than it first appeared, affecting relationships, work, safety, or money more than you realised, take it back to Lesson 12 before acting.

A retreat can begin a change, but insight takes root through what you practise afterwards. The work of these two weeks does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be actual. So this lesson asks: how do I make the retreat real in ordinary life?

2 · Doers of the word

Our first passage is from the letter of James. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. James chapter 1, verses 22 to 25:

"But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves. For if anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer, he is like a man who looks intently at his natural face in a mirror. For he looks at himself and goes away and at once forgets what he was like. But the one who looks into the perfect law, the law of liberty, and perseveres, being no hearer who forgets but a doer who acts, he will be blessed in his doing."

3 · Nehemiah prays, then asks

Our second passage is from Nehemiah, a Jewish official who served as cupbearer to the Persian king. News came that Jerusalem’s walls were broken down. Nehemiah chapter 1, verse 4:

"As soon as I heard these words I sat down and wept and mourned for days, and I continued fasting and praying before the God of heaven."

Months later, the king noticed his sadness. Nehemiah chapter 2, verses 4 and 5:

"Then the king said to me, “What are you requesting?” So I prayed to the God of heaven. And I said to the king, “If it pleases the king, and if your servant has found favor in your sight, that you send me to Judah, to the city of my fathers’ graves, that I may rebuild it.”"

4 · Let us rise up and build

And when Nehemiah reached Jerusalem, he spoke to its leaders. Nehemiah chapter 2, verses 17 and 18:

"Then I said to them, “You see the trouble we are in, how Jerusalem lies in ruins with its gates burned. Come, let us build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision.” And I told them of the hand of my God that had been upon me for good, and also of the words that the king had spoken to me. And they said, “Let us rise up and build.” So they strengthened their hands for the good work."

5 · The mirror and the two houses

Now the background. James compares someone who hears the word and does nothing to a person who looks in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what they saw. It is a precise description of the dip after a retreat: three days of seeing yourself clearly, followed by a week in which the image fades, because nothing was done with it.

Jesus said the same thing more starkly at the end of his teaching in Luke 6. The difference between the house that stands and the house that falls is not what the two builders heard. They heard the same words. One heard and did them; the other heard and did not. And the flood comes to both houses. During the retreat, you listened, reflected, and began responding to God’s Word. Now comes the challenge of continuing that obedience in ordinary life.

6 · Nehemiah: insight, then a plan

Nehemiah is our portrait for this lesson, because his story shows how a burden received in prayer became a project without becoming a stunt. When he heard about the walls, he wept, fasted, and prayed for days. He did not resign his post. Chapter 2 opens months later. He has been carrying the burden and waiting for the right moment. When the king asks, he prays briefly before answering, and then asks for exactly what he needs: leave, letters, and timber. He has clearly thought it through.

When he reaches Jerusalem, he inspects the walls quietly at night, before telling anyone his plan. And when he does tell them, the work is organised into manageable sections, with families and other groups taking responsibility for different parts of the wall. Nehemiah’s situation was not yours, and his strategy is an example, not a command. But the shape is transferable.

7 · The short answer

So, how do I make the retreat real in ordinary life? Here is the short answer. Do something with what you heard. Make a small plan before you announce anything. Take the cards one at a time. And change one thing about the structure that emptied you.

That gives us four points: hearers and doers; insight, then a plan; the cards, one at a time; and the Jethro conversation.

8 · Point 1: Hearers and Doers

The first point: hearers and doers. James’s alternative to the forgetful hearer is the person who looks into the perfect law, the law of liberty, and perseveres, being no hearer who forgets but a doer who acts. Notice the word "perseveres". It is not one heroic act. It is continuing.

And this is not about earning anything. We do not obey God’s Word to earn his favour. We obey because his grace has already begun its work in us. Grace does not leave us unchanged; it teaches us to live differently. Session 10 said it this way: service grows from grace; it does not purchase it. And the doing does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be actual.

9 · Point 2: Insight, Then a Plan

The second point: insight, then a plan. Notice Nehemiah’s sequence. Insight, then time. A plan with specifics. A quiet look at the facts before the announcement. And work broken into pieces people could actually do.

That shape is the opposite of coming home from a retreat and announcing everything on the first evening. It is also the opposite of doing nothing. Nehemiah waited months, but he was not idle. He was carrying the burden in prayer and thinking it through, so that when the moment came, he knew what to ask for. James gives us the command to do the word; Nehemiah gives us an example of doing it prayerfully and wisely.

10 · Point 3: The Cards, One at a Time

The third point: the cards, one at a time. Start with the Act cards. Each names a next faithful step within your responsibility. This week, choose the one you wrote on Session 10’s action card, and do it. Not all of them; one. If it is a conversation, have it. If it is an appointment, book it. If it is a task you have been avoiding, do the first hour of it.

When it is done, take the next Act card, at a pace of roughly one a week. The point is not speed. It is that the cards leave the pile and become things that happened.

11 · The Share cards

The Share cards are the burdens that should not be carried alone, and they will not share themselves. First, decide what kind of sharing the card needs: being heard, receiving support, dividing a task, or asking the appropriate leader to redistribute responsibility. Not every shared burden is a task someone else should take over, and you may not have the authority to reassign it yourself.

Then ask. Choose the person the card names, request a short conversation, and say three things. This is the burden. This is how I have been carrying it alone. This is the specific help I am asking for. For example: "I have been managing all the finances on my own, and it is wearing me down. Would you take over the monthly bills?" Specific requests are easier to answer than general appeals. A request lets the other person answer honestly; it is not a demand that they rescue you. Expect that some people will say no, and that some will be glad to have been asked.

12 · The Seek-counsel cards

The Seek-counsel cards named the kind of guidance you need: a doctor, a counsellor, a lawyer, a financial adviser, a safeguarding lead, a domestic-abuse specialist, a pastor. This week, make the appointment. Not the decision; the appointment. Your first step is complete when the appointment is booked. Keep the card until you have received the guidance you need and considered your next step.

If you cannot book straight away, take the next available step. Identify two suitable providers, join the waiting list, ask your doctor for a referral, contact a helpline, or find out what it will cost. Do not let an unavailable appointment become no action at all. For urgent safety or medical concerns, do not wait for an ordinary appointment; Lesson 5 applies. And if you have been putting off a medical or mental-health appointment, this is the card that matters most. Lesson 15 will say more.

13 · Entrust cards, and repairs

The Entrust cards have no action. That is the point. If you find yourself trying to act on one, notice it; you may be trying to control the thing again. Read the card, pray the words on it, and put it back. But entrusting does not mean neglecting a responsibility, tolerating harm, or refusing necessary help. It means releasing the outcome you cannot control after taking whatever responsible action is yours. If new facts show that action is needed, reclassify the card rather than using "entrust" to avoid it.

And the repairs from Session 5. If your confession pointed to an apology or a repair, it is an Act card like any other, with care. Centre the harm done to the other person, not your own relief, and do not pressure them to respond on your timetable. An apology does not oblige anyone to forgive, reply, resume contact, or restore trust. Do not contact someone merely to relieve your conscience if contact would be unsafe, unwanted, legally restricted, or likely to burden them further. In those cases, seek qualified guidance first. And if the matter is serious in the way Session 5 described, it is a Seek-counsel card first.

14 · Point 4: The Jethro Conversation

The fourth point: the Jethro conversation. Lesson 4 promised that this part of the course would help with the structural causes of burnout, and this is where it does. Rest, as Lesson 4 also said, does not fix a structure. A person can take three days away and walk straight back into the pattern that emptied them, and many do, unless something about the pattern changes.

Go back to Exodus 18. Jethro’s advice to Moses had three parts: you are doing too much; some of this can be done by others; and here is how to divide it. For many exhausted people, a Jethro conversation is an important part of recovery. Sometimes rest is not enough; the responsibilities and arrangements that contributed to exhaustion must also be examined. Some exhaustion comes from illness, bereavement, or circumstances that cannot be reorganised, and that is not a failure to delegate. But where the workload can change, have the conversation: with yourself, and with whoever has authority to change the workload or shares its consequences. That might be a manager, an elder board, a spouse, a family member, a ministry team, or a care coordinator. For caregivers and people with no organisation above them, it may mean extended family, community services, respite care, or professional support.

15 · Five questions

Ask yourself these five questions, and write the answers down. One: what am I doing that only I can do? Be strict. The list is usually shorter than it feels. Two: what am I doing that someone else could do, if they were asked and trusted? Three: what am I doing that does not need to be done at all, by anyone? Every role gathers these. Four: what am I doing because I am afraid of what will happen if I stop? Session 8 may have surfaced this. And five: what would this role look like if it were designed for a human being with legitimate limits, rather than depending on one person continually exceeding them?

16 · One structural change

Then take the answers to the person who can change something, and ask for one structural change. One. A protected day off. A task removed. A responsibility shared. A meeting that stops. If the answer is no, ask why, and whether another form of relief is possible. A clear refusal is important information about whether the present arrangement can last, and it may belong on a "To test" card for Lesson 12.

Expect some resistance, not necessarily from ill will. A system that has grown used to your overfunctioning will have to adjust when you stop. Some resistance is practical and temporary. Some may show that the system depends unfairly on your exhaustion. Pay attention to the difference. Neither makes you wrong to have asked.

17 · When it does not work

Some of this will fail. The person you asked will decline. The structural change will be refused. The appointment will be weeks away. The rhythm will be missed three days running. None of that undoes the retreat.

Session 10’s rhythm card had a line for exactly this: "When I miss it, I will start again the next day without guilt." Apply the same sentence to everything in this lesson. A refused request is information. A missed rhythm is restarted, not abandoned. An appointment weeks away is still booked, which is more than it was. Jesus described a house that stood because its foundation was secure. Building a faithful life also requires perseverance. When you miss a step, return to what is right, and continue.

18 · Your step

Here is your step, which is also below this lesson and on the worksheet. It is the minimum two-week plan. One Act step, with the day you will do it. One Share conversation: with whom, and your three sentences. One Seek-counsel appointment or access step, and when you will make the call. And the five Jethro questions, answered in your journal. Alongside these, keep practising the rhythm from Session 10, imperfectly. It is not another task on the list; it is the ongoing practice the list sits inside. You can note the days you kept it.

Begin with the minimum. Add the rest only if your present capacity allows: a second Act step, a second Share conversation, and one structural change requested. The structural request may itself be one of your Share conversations; one action may do both. If your capacity allows less than the minimum, choose the actions that most protect your health, safety, and faithfulness.

19 · Remember

Let us bring it together. What began on retreat now continues in ordinary life: be a doer of the word, not a hearer who forgets. Insight, then a plan, with a quiet look at the facts before any announcement. The cards, one at a time, until they become things that happened. And, where change is possible, a Jethro conversation that asks for one structural change. When some of it fails, resume.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord, I heard you in the quiet. Help me not to walk away and forget. Give me one faithful step this week, and the courage to ask for help. Show me what is mine to carry, and what is not. When I miss it, help me begin again. Amen.

When you are ready, choose one Act card and write the day you will do it. That is enough to begin.
