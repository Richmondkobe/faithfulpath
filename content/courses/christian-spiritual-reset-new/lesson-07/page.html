1 · What do I actually want to bring to God?

Welcome back. In Lesson 6 you chose a length and a place, or at least a format and a target month. Before we prepare your heart in Lesson 8, and before we deal with your phone and your packing, there is one short exercise that will shape everything else: deciding, honestly, why you are going.

That sounds obvious. It is not. People often arrive at a retreat with no clear intention, or with an exhausting list of things to fix, or with a spiritual-sounding purpose that hides what they truly want. None of these will serve you well.

So this lesson asks: what do I actually want to bring to God?

2 · Begin where you are

Two earlier lessons prepare the way. In Lesson 4 you made a private check-in: what you have been carrying, and how heavy it has become. In Lesson 5 you learned to separate what you are asking of God from what you are expecting your retreat to produce.

This lesson brings those together into one page, written to God, which you will carry into the first session of your retreat. A retreat intention is a brief, honest description of what you are bringing before God. It is not a goal list, and it is not a performance.

3 · What do you want me to do for you?

Our first passage is from Mark’s Gospel. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Mark chapter 10, verses 46 to 52:

"And they came to Jericho. And as he was leaving Jericho with his disciples and a great crowd, Bartimaeus, a blind beggar, the son of Timaeus, was sitting by the roadside. And when he heard that it was Jesus of Nazareth, he began to cry out and say, “Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!” And many rebuked him, telling him to be silent. But he cried out all the more, “Son of David, have mercy on me!” And Jesus stopped and said, “Call him.” And they called the blind man, saying to him, “Take heart. Get up; he is calling you.” And throwing off his cloak, he sprang up and came to Jesus. And Jesus said to him, “What do you want me to do for you?” And the blind man said to him, “Rabbi, let me recover my sight.” And Jesus said to him, “Go your way; your faith has made you well.” And immediately he recovered his sight and followed him on the way."

4 · One thing

And our second passage is a single verse from the Psalms. Psalm 27, verse 4:

"One thing have I asked of the LORD, that will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD and to inquire in his temple."

5 · A man by the road

Now the background. Jesus was on his way to Jerusalem, and he had just told his disciples, for the third time, what would happen to him there. Jericho was the last town before the long climb up to the city. Crowds were travelling with him.

By the road sat a blind man, Bartimaeus, begging. When he heard who was passing, he called Jesus "Son of David", a title of hope for the promised King, and he asked for mercy. The crowd told him to be quiet. He shouted louder.

Then Jesus stopped, called him, and asked: "What do you want me to do for you?" Bartimaeus’ blindness was visible, yet Jesus still invited him to express his request. Mark does not tell us everything behind Jesus’ question, but he does show Bartimaeus answering with a clear and personal request.

6 · The same question, twice

Jesus had asked the same question a few verses earlier. James and John came to him with a request, and he said to them, "What do you want me to do for you?" They asked for the seats of honour, one at his right hand and one at his left, in his glory. Their answer revealed something about them that they might not have admitted otherwise.

Bartimaeus’ need could be seen by everyone. But the question invited him to name his desire for himself. The same question, asked of James and John, exposed a very different desire. In both cases, the answer revealed the heart of the person asking.

Saying what we want, plainly, in his hearing, tells us the truth about ourselves. It moves us from a vague hope that something will happen to an honest request whose outcome we entrust to God.

7 · Hannah at Shiloh

Our second portrait is Hannah. She had no children, and year after year she was provoked about it. At the house of the Lord at Shiloh she prayed in such distress that only her lips moved; no sound came out. The priest, Eli, watched her and assumed she was drunk.

Hannah answered him: "I have been pouring out my soul before the Lord." She did not tidy up her anguish for God, or for the priest. She brought her longing as it was, and she asked plainly for a son.

And notice one more detail. When she left, the account says that her face was no longer sad. She did not yet know how God would answer. But she had said it, and she had left it with him.

Hannah’s experience does not mean that everyone will feel lighter immediately after praying. Her story shows us that we can bring our deepest longing to God before we know how he will answer.

8 · The short answer

So, what do I actually want to bring to God? Here is the short answer. Answer the question Jesus asked. Gather your desire into one thing. Say it honestly, not beautifully. And hold it with open hands.

That gives us four points: "What do you want me to do for you?"; one thing, not a list; honest, not polished; and open hands.

9 · Point 1: "What Do You Want Me to Do for You?"

The first point: "What do you want me to do for you?" Your intention for this retreat is your answer to that question. Not the answer you think a mature Christian should give. The real one.

An intention is provisional, not controlling. It gives you a place to begin, while leaving God free to redirect your attention. And when you bring it to him, his response may affirm what you asked, redirect it, delay it, or reshape it. You are not writing instructions for God. You are telling him the truth about what you are bringing.

10 · Point 2: One Thing, Not a List

The second point: one thing, not a list. The first mistake is to arrive with too much. Some retreat plans read like a project plan: resolve my calling, forgive my father, decide about the job, restart my prayer life, plan the next year, and lose the anxiety. Each of those may be real. But together they turn the retreat into work, and you already have enough of that.

Psalm 27 offers a different posture. David knew danger, opposition and many competing needs. Yet he writes: "One thing have I asked of the Lord." Not because he had only one need, but because he gathered his desire into the one that lay underneath the others.

And notice what that one thing was. It was not simply his most urgent personal concern. It was to dwell in the presence of the Lord, and to seek him. Our intentions may begin with our immediate burdens, but the deeper invitation is to bring those burdens into fellowship with God.

You are not looking for one perfect sentence that explains your whole life. You are looking for the concern or desire that seems most important to bring before God now. Other concerns may surface during the retreat, and your intention is allowed to change.

11 · Expectation or intention?

Here is what that sounds like, building on what you learned in Lesson 5. On one side, an expectation. On the other, an intention.

"God must tell me whether to leave my job" becomes, "I want to bring my fear and uncertainty about work before God, without forcing an immediate answer." "I must forgive everything by Sunday" becomes, "I want to become honest about the wound, and open to the next safe step towards freedom."

"I need to feel God again" becomes, "I want to remain present with God, and truthful about the distance I feel." And "This retreat must cure my burnout" becomes, "I want to recognise what has emptied me, and become willing to change what I can."

12 · Point 3: Honest, Not Polished

The third point: honest, not polished. The second mistake is the opposite of the first. Some of us will write an intention that is entirely correct and entirely hollow: "I want to draw closer to God and be renewed in my faith." There is nothing wrong with that sentence. But if the truer sentence is, "I am so angry about what happened that I cannot pray, and I have not told anyone," then the polished version is a way of avoiding the retreat before it starts.

Scripture does not reward polished prayers. Hannah poured out her soul. God already knows the unpolished version. Writing it down is for you, so that you enter the retreat as the person you actually are.

So read your sentence slowly, and ask: Is this what I truly want to bring to God? Am I using general spiritual language to avoid something more specific? Does this sound like me? And can I say it without pretending to feel something I do not feel? An honest intention may be deeply private. You do not need to force yourself to say more than you can safely hold.

13 · What else am I hoping for?

Underneath the stated intention, there is sometimes a hidden one. Some people go on retreat hoping God will confirm a decision they have already made. Some hope he will change a person who is not there. Some go to prove that they are still spiritual. Some go to escape a conversation they are supposed to be having. And some are hoping for a dramatic experience that will make everything feel real again.

None of these disqualifies you. But left unspoken, each of them can distort what you hear. If you are seeking only confirmation, you may hear every impression as confirmation. If you are seeking escape, you may mistake relief for direction. So ask yourself, gently, and write it down: is there anything else I am quietly hoping for, or something I have found difficult to admit? Then bring that to God as well.

Your motives do not have to be perfectly pure before you begin. Few human motives are. The aim is to bring as much of yourself into the light as you can at present. Psalm 139 is the prayer for this:

"Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!"

14 · Point 4: Open Hands

The fourth point: open hands. Lesson 5 introduced this, and here it matters most. An intention is a request, not a demand. You bring it to God, and you leave the outcome with him.

You may ask for clarity about a decision and receive, instead, peace about not knowing yet. You may ask for God’s felt presence and receive three days of quiet faithfulness without it. You may ask to forgive someone and discover that what you needed first was to grieve.

God spoke to his people in exile, whose displacement would not be the end of their story: "You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart." That is not a promise of a dramatic retreat. It shows God’s desire for people who turn to him with their whole heart.

And we do not compare our decisions with what Jesus faced at the cross. Yet his prayer in Gethsemane gives us a pattern: "Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will." Honest desire, held together with surrender to the Father. Your intention can do both. State it plainly, and then add, in your own words: but I am open to what you have for me instead.

15 · What open hands do not mean

One important clarification. Open hands do not mean passivity towards abuse, injustice, danger, or responsibilities that require action. Surrender means refusing to manipulate the retreat into producing the answer you want. It does not mean surrendering wisdom, boundaries, or safety.

16 · If you cannot find an intention

Some of you will sit with this exercise and find nothing. Not because you are hiding something, but because you are too tired or too numb to know what you want. If that is you, your intention can simply be: "I do not know what I need. I only know I need to stop and be with you." That is a complete and honest intention, and God has met people with far less.

Elijah, in Lesson 1, could name little more than his exhaustion: "It is enough." God responded first with care for his body, and with his presence in Elijah’s despair. If your words mean only, "I have reached my limit and need to stop," write that honestly, and go.

But if "It is enough" means that you do not want to remain alive, please do not begin the retreat alone. Follow the guidance from Lesson 5, which you will find again below this lesson, and seek support straight away.

17 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 7 worksheet. Take a page in your journal, or a single sheet you will bring with you, and write four things.

Your main intention may be one sentence. These four short prompts simply help you discover and express that sentence. You do not need to write a long journal entry.

First, what I am carrying: a few sentences about the situation you are coming from. Use what you wrote in your Lesson 4 check-in. Second, what I am asking: one sentence, in the first person, addressed to God. The concern that matters most right now, in plain words. Hannah’s honesty, not polished religious language. Third, what I have not said: anything else you are quietly hoping for, or have found difficult to admit, if there is anything.

And fourth, what I am releasing: one sentence letting go of your demand for a particular outcome. Name what you will not force, and say that you are willing to keep seeking wise help if clarity or relief does not come. For example: "God, I will not force an answer during these days. Help me receive what becomes clear, and continue patiently with what remains unresolved."

If you have enough privacy, read it aloud once as a prayer. If not, read it slowly and silently before God. You will carry this page into Session 1, where it becomes the first thing you say to God on your retreat, and you will read it again in the final session, to see what has shifted. If you have a trustworthy support person, you may choose to share it with them. You are never required to share what is private.

18 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Jesus asks, "What do you want me to do for you?" and your intention is your honest answer. Gather it into one thing, not a list; the desire underneath the others. Say it honestly, not beautifully, as Hannah did. And hold it with open hands: a request, not a demand.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you stopped on the road and asked, "What do you want me to do for you?" Search me, and show me what I truly want. Give me the courage to say it plainly, and the trust to leave the answer with you. Not what I will, but what you will. Amen.

If “It is enough” means that you do not want to remain alive, do not begin the retreat alone. Tell a trusted person and contact a qualified mental-health professional or crisis service promptly.
