Jesus and the Practice of Withdrawing

How did Jesus rest, and what does that teach me?

About 18 minutesMark 1:21–39 and Luke 6:12–13Part 1 · Understand

In this lesson

You will learn

  • Five moments when Jesus was away from the crowds, which were ordinary withdrawals and which were unique, and why there is no single mood that qualifies us for solitude (Mark 1:35; Luke 6:12)
  • Why prayer was central to Jesus' life, and how honestly he prayed (Mark 14:36)
  • What to do when your quiet is interrupted (Mark 1:36–37; Matthew 14:13–23)
  • Why solitude with God does not mean isolation from people

1 · How did Jesus rest?

Welcome back. In Lesson 2 we asked whether it is selfish to stop, and we heard Scripture's answer: God commanded rest, and Jesus withdrew in order to return.

Now we turn from permission to pattern. If Jesus withdrew to pray, how did he do it? When did he go, what did he do there, and what happened when people came looking for him? The Gospels tell us more than many Christians realise, and what they show is a pattern, not a formula.

So this lesson asks: how did Jesus rest, and what does that teach me?

2 · The day in Capernaum

Our first passage is the end of one very long day. It is a Sabbath in the town of Capernaum, by the Sea of Galilee. Jesus has taught in the synagogue, set free a man with an unclean spirit, and healed Simon Peter's mother-in-law. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Mark chapter 1, verses 32 to 35:

"That evening at sundown they brought to him all who were sick or oppressed by demons. And the whole city was gathered together at the door. And he healed many who were sick with various diseases, and cast out many demons. And he would not permit the demons to speak, because they knew him. And rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed."

3 · Everyone is looking for you

But the quiet did not last long. The next verses:

"And Simon and those who were with him searched for him, and they found him and said to him, “Everyone is looking for you.” And he said to them, “Let us go on to the next towns, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out.”"

He had his time with the Father, and it was interrupted. And when it was interrupted, he did not simply go back to meet the crowd's expectations. He went on to the next towns, where his purpose was leading.

4 · All night in prayer

Our next passage comes from a different moment: the night before an important decision. Luke chapter 6, verses 12 and 13:

"In these days he went out to the mountain to pray, and all night he continued in prayer to God. And when day came, he called his disciples and chose from them twelve, whom he named apostles."

5 · In the garden

And our last reading comes from the night before Jesus died. Mark chapter 14, verses 32 to 36:

"And they went to a place called Gethsemane. And he said to his disciples, “Sit here while I pray.” And he took with him Peter and James and John, and began to be greatly distressed and troubled. And he said to them, “My soul is very sorrowful, even to death. Remain here and watch.” And going a little farther, he fell on the ground and prayed that, if it were possible, the hour might pass from him. And he said, “Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.”"

Gethsemane belongs to Christ's unique path to the cross, and no retreat of ours compares to it. But we read it here because it shows us, more clearly than anywhere else, how honestly Jesus prayed.

6 · Erēmos again

We have no new word in this lesson. Instead, an old one comes back. In Lesson 1 we met the Greek word erēmos: a desolate, deserted or solitary place, away from the crowds.

It is the word Mark uses in verse 35. Jesus "went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed." And Matthew uses the same word when Jesus heard that John the Baptist had been killed:

"Now when Jesus heard this, he withdrew from there in a boat to a desolate place by himself."

As we said in Lesson 1, the word itself does not mean spiritual renewal. It means a place where there are fewer people and fewer demands. The significance of that place was not in the location itself, but in what Jesus did there: he prayed to his Father.

7 · At sundown, the whole city

Now the background. Mark tells this day in some detail. It began in the synagogue, where Jesus taught with an authority that amazed the people, and where a man with an unclean spirit cried out and was set free. Then Jesus went to the home of Simon and Andrew and healed Simon's mother-in-law.

Then Mark says the people came "at sundown". The Sabbath ended at sunset, and it seems that many waited until then before carrying their sick through the streets. Once the sun went down, "the whole city was gathered together at the door."

It had been an extraordinarily demanding day. And the very next morning, while it was still dark, Jesus got up and went out to pray. He went before anyone else could reach him.

8 · Loss, and a long night

Two more pieces of background. In Matthew chapter 14, Jesus hears that John the Baptist has been killed by Herod. John had prepared the way for Jesus and had baptised him, and Luke's Gospel also tells us that their mothers were relatives. When Jesus heard of John's death, Matthew records that he withdrew in a boat to a desolate place by himself. The text does not describe everything Jesus felt. But the timing invites us to see that withdrawing can be a fitting response in a season of loss. If you are carrying grief, seeking solitude after loss is not weakness.

And about the night before Jesus chose the Twelve: Matthew and Mark both record the choosing, but only Luke tells us that Jesus spent the whole night before it in prayer. Luke places it there on purpose, right before the decision that would shape the rest of the movement. The direction came after the withdrawal, not instead of it.

9 · The short answer

So, how did Jesus rest? Here is the short answer. Jesus withdrew at many different moments, and prayer to his Father was central to his life. His times alone were often interrupted. And he did not always go entirely alone.

That gives us four points: a pattern, not a program; time with the Father; when the quiet is interrupted; and not always alone.

10 · Point 1: A Pattern, Not a Program

The first point: a pattern, not a program. The Gospels show Jesus in several settings away from the crowds. Some were deliberate withdrawals for prayer. Others, particularly the wilderness testing and Gethsemane, belonged to unique moments in his mission. We must not treat them all as ordinary retreats, but together they teach us about prayer, dependence on the Father, and faithfulness.

So read them together. Jesus withdrew after a day of overwhelming demand, in Capernaum. He withdrew in a season of loss, when John was killed. He withdrew before a major decision, the night before choosing the Twelve. He was led by the Spirit into the wilderness in a time of testing. And he went to Gethsemane before his suffering.

The lesson of that night before the Twelve is not that every big decision needs a whole night without sleep. The point is that Jesus gave serious, undistracted attention to prayer before making a significant choice. During this retreat, protecting your sleep may itself be part of receiving God's care.

Lay these side by side, and you see there was no single mood that qualified Jesus for solitude. He went when the work was going well and when it was about to cost him everything. If you are waiting to feel a particular way before you take this retreat, the Gospels suggest you can stop waiting.

11 · When the quiet is not peaceful

One of those five moments needs a closer look. Luke chapter 4, verses 1 and 2:

"And Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit, returned from the Jordan and was led by the Spirit in the wilderness for forty days, being tempted by the devil."

Those forty days were not a restful retreat in the usual sense. They were a unique period of fasting, testing and preparation at the beginning of Jesus' ministry, and you are not being asked to reproduce them.

But they remind us of something important. Solitude does not always feel peaceful. When the distractions disappear, temptation, fear and unresolved pain may become more noticeable. If that happens during your retreat, it does not necessarily mean you have made a mistake. Sometimes the quiet shows us struggles that the noise had helped us avoid.

12 · Point 2: Time With the Father

The second point: time with the Father. The Gospels repeatedly show Jesus withdrawing to pray and spend time with his Father. Prayer was central to his life and ministry. And where Scripture records his prayers, they are remarkably honest. We hear thanksgiving. We hear him name what lies before him and pray for his followers. We hear honest requests, and submission to the Father's will. In Gethsemane he said plainly, "Remove this cup from me," and then, "Yet not what I will, but what you will."

This is worth saying clearly, because much retreat language today, including some Christian retreat language, has drifted towards wellness. Silence, reflection and physical rest all belong in a Christian retreat, and this course includes all three. But they are not its centre. We do not withdraw merely to become calmer. We withdraw to become present to God.

Notice, too, how ordinary the places were: a hillside, a garden, a boat, the far side of a lake. You do not need a retreat centre. You need a place where interruptions can be reduced, and where you can give sustained attention to God. Lesson 6 will help you choose yours.

13 · Point 3: When the Quiet Is Interrupted

The third point will encourage anyone whose life does not co-operate with quiet. Jesus' times of solitude were interrupted again and again. In Capernaum, Simon and the others came looking for him in the dark: "Everyone is looking for you."

And after John's death, when Jesus withdrew by boat, the crowds heard where he was going and walked round the lake on foot. Matthew says:

"When he went ashore he saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them and healed their sick."

That evening, after feeding the five thousand, Matthew tells us:

"And after he had dismissed the crowds, he went up on the mountain by himself to pray."

He did get his time alone. It simply came later than the story first suggested.

14 · A genuine need, or a distraction?

So, two things to take from this. First, do everything you can to protect your retreat. Lesson 9 will show you how. Second, an interruption does not automatically ruin your retreat. Respond to genuine needs, return to the quiet when you can, and reschedule what you cannot complete. Even if circumstances prevent you from finishing, the time you gave to God was not wasted.

At the same time, compassion does not require you to answer every notification, request or preference straight away. Jesus responded to people in need, but he did not surrender his life to every demand made of him. Learning the difference between a genuine need and an avoidable distraction is part of what this retreat will teach you.

15 · Point 4: Not Always Alone

The fourth point: not always alone. Jesus often prayed alone. But on at least two especially significant occasions, the Transfiguration and Gethsemane, he brought Peter, James and John with him. They were imperfect companions. On the mountain they were heavy with sleep, and in the garden they could not stay awake. Yet Jesus still let them come, and they became witnesses to moments they would never forget.

Jesus sometimes withdrew completely alone, and sometimes brought trusted disciples nearby. His example leaves room for both private solitude and a supported retreat. Solitude with God is essential. Unhealthy isolation from people is not the goal.

That is why, even in the personal version of this retreat, we encourage you, where possible, to let at least one trustworthy person know about your retreat. If you do not yet have someone you trust, consider whom you could begin approaching. And afterwards, it will help to speak with someone about what became clear, and test any significant impression against Scripture, the character of Christ, wise counsel, and the responsibilities God has already given you. We will return to that testing in Lesson 12.

16 · More than an example

One last thing. Jesus is not merely the example behind this retreat. He is the one who invites you into it. You are not trying to reproduce his life through discipline alone. You are responding to the living Christ, who says, "Come to me," and who meets tired people with grace. The pattern tells you what the days may look like. The person tells you who is waiting in them.

And the shape of your retreat follows the pattern we have seen. Following the example of Jesus withdrawing after the demanding day in Capernaum, you will begin by stepping away from everyday noise and giving your attention to God. You will be given room, as he took after John's death, to grieve and to release what you are carrying. You will spend time listening, as he did on the mountain, before you make any decisions about direction. And when your retreat ends, you will return to everyday life, seeking to follow Christ faithfully in the responsibilities and relationships before you. Some things may continue as before; others may need to change. The aim is not simply to return to busyness, but to live with greater attention to God.

17 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too.

First: of the five moments we saw, which one is closest to where you are right now? After demand, in loss, before a decision, in a season of testing, or facing something hard? Write a sentence about why.

Second: where possible, who is one trustworthy person you could tell about your retreat? If you do not yet have someone you trust, think of a pastor, a counsellor, a mature believer or a supportive community you could begin approaching.

18 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Jesus withdrew at many different moments, so there is no single mood that qualifies you for solitude. Prayer was central to his life, and so we withdraw, not merely to become calmer, but to become present to God. His quiet was often interrupted, so respond to genuine needs, and return to the quiet when you can. And he did not always go entirely alone. Solitude with God is essential; isolation from people is not the goal.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you rose while it was still dark and went out to pray. You withdrew in grief, you prayed through the night, and in the garden you told your Father the truth. Teach me to come to the Father as you did, honestly and without pretending. When I am interrupted, give me compassion and wisdom, and bring me back to the quiet. Thank you that you are not only my example; you are the one who invites me. Amen.
