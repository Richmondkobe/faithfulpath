1 · When is it more than tiredness?

Welcome back. This lesson is for any week, whenever it applies. Lesson 5 said, before the retreat, that a retreat cannot replace medical or psychological care. Then the question was whether you were safe to go. Now the question is what the retreat showed you.

Three days of quiet can make visible what months of noise had covered. If you came home knowing that what you carry needs more than rest, this lesson is not a verdict. It is a map. So it asks: when is it more than tiredness, and who can help?

2 · If you are in danger now

Before anything else. Everything in this lesson assumes you have time to choose. If you do not, the map comes down to one instruction: get help immediately.

If you may act on thoughts of suicide, have a plan or access to the means, cannot keep yourself safe, or are in immediate danger from another person, contact emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department now. Use a crisis or abuse service from a safe device. Move to a safer or more public place if you can, tell a trusted person, and do not remain alone. If it is safe, put distance between yourself and anything you could use to harm yourself. If you do not know the number, ask a hospital, a doctor, or a trusted person to help you make contact.

And if you have thoughts of suicide without a plan or intention, they still need prompt professional help: contact a doctor, a crisis line, or an urgent mental-health service today. The rest of this lesson can wait.

3 · With many advisers

Our passages are short. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Proverbs chapter 15, verse 22:

"Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed."

And Galatians chapter 6, verse 2, which you met in Lesson 5:

"Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ."

4 · Luke, the beloved physician

Now the background. Our portrait for this lesson is Luke, whom you met in Lesson 5. Paul calls him "the beloved physician". He travelled with Paul, and is traditionally understood to have written a Gospel and Acts. And Paul, writing to Timothy, gave practical advice about his stomach and his frequent illnesses.

Neither of these is a theory of mental health. But together they show that early Christians did not set faith against ordinary means of care. And Proverbs teaches the wisdom of counsel in making decisions. It is not a map of modern health services, but its principle holds: we are not meant to carry hard things on our own judgement alone.

5 · The short answer

So, when is it more than tiredness, and who can help? Here is the short answer. Notice the signs. Go to the right first person. Let faith lead you towards help, not away from it. And if you lead others, or are unsure what you believe, you are not alone.

That gives us four points: signs the retreat surfaced more; who does what; faith and help together; and for leaders, and for those in doubt.

6 · Point 1: Signs the Retreat Surfaced More

The first point: signs the retreat surfaced more. Lesson 4 described where pastoral description stops being enough. Read it again in the light of what happened, and add these signs.

The dip has not lifted. Session 10 said to expect a flat week. If the flatness has lasted most days for two weeks or more, or is deepening rather than easing, it may be more than readjustment. If it is severe, rapidly worsening, preventing ordinary life, or includes thoughts of suicide or self-harm, seek help now. And you do not have to wait two weeks, or reach a particular threshold, before asking for professional help.

The grief remains overwhelming. Session 4 may have brought grief or anger closer to the surface, and grief does not close neatly. But if it keeps overwhelming you, or significantly disrupts sleep, eating, or your ability to cope, more support may help. A counsellor can help well before grief reaches any clinical threshold. You do not need to prove it is serious enough.

7 · More signs

Something surfaced that frightens you: a memory, a pattern of thought, a fear. If it affects your safety or daily life, or keeps intruding, take it to a qualified person. You do not have to tell everything at once. You may begin by saying that something difficult surfaced, and asking how confidentiality works.

Or you recognised yourself in Lesson 5: the sections on suicidal thoughts, abuse, dependence, addiction, or trauma. If you read them before the retreat and thought "not me", and now think "perhaps me", that shift is the point of this lesson. Or the rule of life keeps being knocked over by something underneath it: anxiety, low mood, compulsions, panic. Or simply this: you are not getting better, and you have been faithful. Faithfulness without improvement, over weeks, is a reason to seek help, not a reason to try harder.

8 · Point 2: Who Does What

The second point: who does what. Christians often go first to a pastor, because it is the person they know. Pastors are essential, but not the right first call for everything. Here is a rough guide; how care is accessed varies by country.

For spiritual dryness, questions of faith, guidance, or church-related hurt: a pastor or spiritual director, for prayer, Scripture, spiritual counsel and discernment, not diagnosis or treatment. For grief, relationship difficulty, life transitions, or moderate anxiety or low mood: a qualified counsellor or therapist. Many are Christian or respectful of faith; ask. For persistent low mood, hopelessness, changes in sleep and appetite, panic, intrusive thoughts, trauma symptoms, or anything affecting daily life: a doctor or qualified mental-health professional, for assessment and treatment.

9 · When it is urgent or specialised

Some needs are urgent or specialised. For thoughts of death, suicide, or not wanting to be alive: prompt professional help, from a crisis line or urgent mental-health service, even without a plan. And emergency services if you may act, have a plan or the means, or cannot stay safe. For abuse or coercive control, past or present: a specialist domestic-abuse or sexual-violence service, contacted from a safe device. Joint counselling may be unsafe where abuse exists. For substance dependence: a doctor and an addiction service. If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, sedatives, or another substance, do not stop abruptly on your own; get medical advice about safe withdrawal.

For scrupulosity, compulsive confession or checking: a clinician experienced in OCD. Repeated reassurance and confession can become part of the compulsion, so a pastor helping alongside should avoid feeding that cycle. And for serious misconduct or safeguarding concerns: the appropriate safeguarding, legal, or professional authority.

10 · Two things the table implies

Two things the table implies. First, most serious difficulties need more than one kind of help, and a pastor is often one part of it. Second, the pastor’s role is real. Someone receiving treatment for depression can also benefit from prayer, Scripture, and a caring Christian community, according to what they are able to receive. The mistake is not going to a pastor. It is going only to a pastor, for something a pastor is not trained to treat.

11 · Point 3: Faith and Help Together

The third point: faith and help together. Lesson 5 answered the common objections; here they are again, shorter. "Seeking therapy means my faith is not enough." Faith does not require you to refuse appropriate care. Prayer, pastoral support, medicine, and therapy can work together.

"Medication would be a spiritual defeat." Medication is a medical treatment, not a verdict on your faith. Discuss benefits, risks, and alternatives with a qualified prescriber, and do not start, stop, or change prescribed medication solely because of something you experienced on retreat. "A Christian should be able to pray this away." Keep praying, and let prayer lead you towards truthful help rather than away from it. Receiving appropriate help may be one of the ways God provides care.

12 · Two more objections, and choosing well

"My pastor should be enough." Responsible pastors recognise when other expertise is needed. "People will find out." Professional care is generally confidential, within the law of your country. Ask about confidentiality and its limits before you begin. You may start by asking questions without telling your whole story.

When you choose a counsellor, check their registration and training, and ask how they relate to faith. You do not need a Christian counsellor for good therapy, but you need one who respects that your faith is central to you. After two or three sessions, ask: do I feel respected, listened to, and able to raise concerns? If not, it is acceptable to change. If cost is a barrier, the page lists options. Do not let an expensive appointment become no action.

13 · Point 4: For Leaders

The fourth point: for leaders, and for those in doubt. If you lead a church or a ministry, find help outside your own church. A minister receiving counselling from a member of their own congregation, or pastoral care from someone they lead, is rarely free to be honest, and the dual relationship is unfair to both. Use your denomination’s provision, a supervisor, a counsellor experienced with clergy, or a pastor in another town.

Consider who, if anyone, in your leadership needs to know you are receiving support. Follow the requirements that apply, but keep clinical details private, and seek confidential advice first if disclosure could put you at risk. And if your tradition does not allow ministers to be unwell, you may be the person who begins to change that, but you do not have to do it by exposing yourself to harm.

14 · For those in a crisis of faith

And some of you came home with something you did not expect: the discovery that you are not sure what you believe, or whether you believe. This is not a mental-health condition, and it is not necessarily a sign that anything is wrong with you. It often happens when a faith carried by activity stops moving and has to stand on its own.

Do not carry it alone, and do not carry it to someone who will panic. A pastor outside your own church, a spiritual director, a mature believer who has walked through doubt themselves: these are the people to talk to. Keep a gentle connection with God if you can, but do not turn prayer or Scripture into a test of whether you still believe. And be patient. Faith that is allowed to ask honest questions can become more mature, though the process may be slow, and its outcome should not be forced.

If your need is urgent, take that step today. Do not wait until later in the week.

If none of the signs applies to you, note who you would call if one did, and keep it somewhere you can find it.

16 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Faithful but not getting better is a reason to seek help, not to try harder. Go to the right first person. Let prayer lead you towards truthful help, not away from it. And if you lead others, find help outside your own church; if you are in doubt, talk to someone who will not panic.

17 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord, you gave us one another, and you gave us those who heal. Give me the courage to ask for the help I need, and the humility to receive it. Carry me, and carry those who help me. Amen.

If one of the signs described you, write your one step now, and take it this week. If you need help today, do not wait.

Who does what

The right first call

A rough guide. Open any need to see who can help. Titles, qualifications and how care is accessed vary by country. Most serious difficulties need more than one kind of help.

Thoughts of death, suicide, or not wanting to be alive

Who can help

Prompt professional help from a doctor, crisis line or urgent mental-health service, even without a plan or intention. Emergency services or the nearest emergency department if you may act, have a plan, have access to the means, or cannot stay safe.

What they do

Assess immediate risk, help you remain safe, and connect you with continuing care.

Spiritual dryness, questions of faith, guidance about a decision, church-related hurt, ongoing pastoral support

Who can help

A pastor or spiritual director

What they do

Prayer, Scripture, spiritual counsel, discernment, community. Not diagnosis or treatment.

Grief, relationship difficulty, life transitions, moderate anxiety or low mood, patterns you want to understand

Who can help

A qualified counsellor or therapist

What they do

Talking therapy. Many are Christian or respectful of faith; ask.

Persistent low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness, sleep and appetite changes, panic, intrusive thoughts, trauma symptoms, anything affecting daily functioning

Who can help

A doctor or qualified mental-health professional, according to how care is accessed in your country; a doctor especially where physical illness, medication, withdrawal or major sleep changes may be involved

What they do

Assessment, diagnosis where appropriate, and treatment, which may include therapy, medication, or both.

Abuse or coercive control, past or present

Who can help

A specialist domestic-abuse or sexual-violence service, contacted from a safe device; emergency services if there is immediate danger and it is safe to call

What they do

Help you assess danger, make a safety plan, and consider housing, legal, medical or police options without pressuring you. Joint counselling or a couples’ retreat may be unsafe where abuse exists.

Substance dependence

Who can help

A doctor and an addiction service; a recovery community

What they do

Safe withdrawal, treatment, ongoing recovery support. If you may be physically dependent, do not stop abruptly on your own; seek medical advice.

Behavioural addiction

Who can help

A counsellor with relevant experience; an accountable community

What they do

Therapy and accountability.

Scrupulosity, compulsive confession or checking, religious anxiety

Who can help

A clinician experienced in OCD and scrupulosity, particularly cognitive behavioural therapy including exposure and response prevention where appropriate

What they do

Evidence-based treatment. Repeated reassurance, confession and requests for certainty can become part of the compulsion; a pastor working alongside should avoid reinforcing it.

Serious misconduct, safeguarding concerns, legal exposure

Who can help

The appropriate safeguarding, legal or professional authority

What they do

Accountability, reporting, protection of others.

Choosing a counsellor

What to ask

  • Registration or recognised credentials
  • Training relevant to what you are bringing
  • Their approach, and why
  • Experience with grief, trauma, burnout, anxiety, OCD, abuse, addiction or clergy care, as relevant
  • How they relate to faith
  • Confidentiality and its limits
  • Costs and cancellation
  • Review points, and how concerns are handled

You do not need a Christian counsellor for good therapy, but you need one who respects that your faith is central to you. You may start by asking questions without telling your whole story.

After two or three sessions, ask: do I feel respected, listened to, and able to raise concerns? If not, it is acceptable to change.

If cost is a barrier: sliding scales, charitable services, church schemes, employee assistance programmes, university training clinics, and public provision. Do not let an expensive appointment become no action.
