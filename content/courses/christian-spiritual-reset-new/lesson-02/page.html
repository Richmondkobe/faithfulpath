1 · Isn't it selfish to stop?

Welcome back. In Lesson 1 we met Elijah under the broom tree, and we saw that God cared for him with food, water and sleep before the journey went on. Perhaps you recognised yourself in that tired prophet.

But somewhere between that lesson and this one, a voice may have started arguing with you. It sounds reasonable. And until it is answered, you will either not take this retreat at all, or you will take it while feeling guilty the whole time.

So this lesson asks a direct question: isn't it selfish to stop?

2 · The voice that argues

The voice says things like this. People are counting on you. You can rest when things settle down. Taking time for yourself is selfish. Real faith pushes through.

If you are a parent, a caregiver, a leader, or simply the reliable one in your family, that voice may be very loud. Many sincere Christians have absorbed a picture of faith in which the most spiritual person is the one who never stops, never says no, and never admits to needing anything.

The Bible does not support that picture. It gives us something more honest. Let us hear it.

3 · The commandment to stop

Our first passage is the fourth commandment, given at Sinai. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Exodus chapter 20, verses 8 to 11:

"Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days you shall labor, and do all your work, but the seventh day is a Sabbath to the LORD your God. On it you shall not do any work, you, or your son, or your daughter, your male servant, or your female servant, or your livestock, or the sojourner who is within your gates. For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth, the sea, and all that is in them, and rested on the seventh day. Therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and made it holy."

4 · Remember that you were a slave

When Moses repeats the commandments forty years later, in Deuteronomy chapter 5, he gives the Sabbath a second reason. Verse 15:

"You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. Therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day."

5 · Jesus withdrew to pray

And our third passage is one we met briefly in Lesson 1. Luke chapter 5, verses 15 and 16:

"But now even more the report about him went abroad, and great crowds gathered to hear him and to be healed of their infirmities. But he would withdraw to desolate places and pray."

A commandment to stop, a people who had once been slaves, and a Saviour who withdrew to pray. Hold all three together as we go on.

6 · Šāḇaṯ: to cease

Our original word for this lesson is Hebrew. In Genesis chapter 2, verse 2, we read:

"And on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work that he had done."

The word translated "rested" there is šāḇaṯ. Other passages use different Hebrew words for rest, so we will stay with this one. At its heart it means to cease, to stop working. It is the root of the word "Sabbath".

Notice what this tells us. God did not rest because he was tired. He ceased from his finished work. Human rest certainly includes recovery, because we are not God and we do get tired. But the word itself is about stopping. In Genesis 2, God's rest is described as ceasing from his completed work. For us, making room for rest often begins with choosing to stop.

7 · Bread for two days

Now the background. Even before Sinai, God was teaching his people to stop. In Exodus chapter 16, he fed Israel in the wilderness with manna. They gathered it each morning. But on the sixth day there was enough for two days, so that the seventh could be kept for rest.

Moses told them, "See! The LORD has given you the Sabbath; therefore on the sixth day he gives you bread for two days." So before the commandments were given at Sinai, God was already teaching Israel to observe the Sabbath. He provided a double portion of manna on the sixth day so that they would not need to gather it on the seventh. God's provision made obedience possible.

8 · A people God had set free

Israel knew what it meant to live without freedom. In Egypt they had been slaves, and their work was never their own to lay down. So when the Sabbath command is repeated in Deuteronomy, God reminds them: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt." They were no longer slaves, and he commanded them to keep the Sabbath. Rest was part of the life of a people whom God had delivered.

That gives us something to reflect on. When we feel we can never stop, it may be a sign that something of that freedom is missing in us. This is not a judgement on anyone. Some people cannot stop because of poverty, unjust work, caring for others, or circumstances outside their control. If that is you, you may need practical help, not only permission, to make rest possible, and it is right to ask for it.

Rest, in Scripture, is not only a reward for finishing your work. It is also a reminder that God, not you, holds the world together.

9 · The short answer

So, isn't it selfish to stop? Here is the short answer. No, not when you stop in the way Scripture describes.

God himself commanded rest. Jesus withdrew in order to return. The real question is whether, when we stop, we turn towards God honestly. And the voices that keep you from resting are not telling you the truth.

That gives us four points: God commanded rest; withdraw in order to return; Jonah or Elijah; and three lies, and a permission.

10 · Point 1: God Commanded Rest

The first point: God commanded rest. The Sabbath was not placed in the margins of Israel's life. It was written into the Ten Commandments, alongside the commands against murder, theft and adultery. In Scripture, rest is not a luxury for people whose lives are easier than yours. It is something God commanded for his people, including their children, their servants, even their animals.

And notice that rest was commanded for everyone in the household. The command did not belong only to the master who could afford a day off.

Now, a Sabbath and a retreat are not the same thing. The Sabbath command was given to Israel within God's covenant at Sinai, as a regular rhythm of rest. A retreat is one practical way of stepping more deliberately into that rhythm. No commandment tells you to take this retreat, and this course is not adding another command to your life. It is helping you respond to a biblical invitation to stop, to listen, and to remember that God remains God when you are not working. We will think about building a weekly rhythm of rest in Lesson 14.

11 · Point 2: Withdraw in Order to Return

The second point: withdraw in order to return. Luke tells us that great crowds gathered to hear Jesus and to be healed. And Jesus "would withdraw to desolate places and pray". As we saw in Lesson 1, this was his habit, not a single emergency.

His withdrawals were not a sign that sin, unbelief or burnout had overtaken him. They show that time with the Father was central to the rhythm of his ministry. People were waiting to be healed, and he withdrew to pray. Then he returned to teach, heal and serve, and his service was sustained by a rhythm that included withdrawing to pray.

That is the difference between running away and retreating. Running away often means avoiding something we need to face. A spiritual retreat, by contrast, is an intentional withdrawal to spend time with God and prepare to return to our responsibilities. You may return with greater clarity, with renewed strength, or simply with a more honest understanding of where you are. The value of the retreat does not depend on experiencing a dramatic change.

Be honest about the alternative. If Lesson 1 described you, the people who depend on you may already be receiving a distracted, depleted and slightly irritable version of you, held together by willpower. Caring for your soul can help you return to them with greater patience, attention and honesty.

12 · Point 3: Jonah or Elijah?

The third point: Jonah or Elijah? The Bible shows us two men who fled, for very different reasons.

In Jonah chapter 1, God told Jonah to go to Nineveh. Instead, Jonah went down to the port of Joppa and boarded a ship for Tarshish, in the opposite direction:

"But Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD. He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish. So he paid the fare and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish, away from the presence of the LORD."

Jonah was moving away from what God had said, and away from the people God had sent him to.

Elijah also fled. First Kings 19 says plainly that "he was afraid, and he arose and ran for his life." Jonah fled in resistance to God's command. Elijah fled because he was afraid for his life. Their circumstances and motives were different. And God dealt with both men in their journeys: he pursued Jonah, and Jonah eventually went to Nineveh, though his heart remained troubled; he cared for Elijah in the wilderness, and Elijah went on to Horeb, where God asked him, "What are you doing here, Elijah?"

So the question for us is not simply whether we have stepped away. It is whether we are willing to turn towards God honestly and respond to what he shows us. Some of you will begin this retreat afraid and exhausted, and some may admit that you are partly running from something. Every session in this course will give you a passage, a prayer and a question that help you turn towards him.

13 · Retreat or avoidance?

So before you call your time away a retreat, ask yourself an honest question: what do I intend to do with the silence?

Am I creating space to face God honestly, or am I using spiritual language to avoid a person, a responsibility, a decision or a conflict? A genuine retreat creates space to face God honestly and consider what faithfulness requires next. Sometimes that means returning to a responsibility with renewed courage. Sometimes it means making a necessary change.

14 · Point 4: Three Lies and a Permission

The fourth point: three lies, and a permission. Over many years of pastoral ministry, I have heard three sentences again and again from people who needed a retreat and would not take one. You may recognise your own voice in them.

The first lie: "If I stop, everything will fall apart." For some people, stopping does need careful preparation. Children still need care. Medication must still be given. A vulnerable relative may need supervision. Essential responsibilities should not be abandoned in the name of a retreat. But preparation is different from postponement. Ask what can be delegated, simplified, rescheduled, or released for a time.

Underneath that first sentence, for most of us, is a belief that we are the ones holding everything up, and that belief is both exhausting and untrue. Psalm 127 says it directly:

"Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain. Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain. It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep."

The psalm's title links it with Solomon, a king with great responsibility. Psalm 127 does not condemn diligent work. It reminds us that our work cannot replace dependence on God. We are called to work faithfully, but we are not responsible for sustaining everything through our own strength. God gives his beloved sleep.

15 · Two more lies

The second lie: "Other people have it harder, so I have no right to be tired." Comparison does not restore anyone. There will always be someone carrying more, and their burden does not make yours lighter. Remember Lesson 1: Jesus did not say, "Come to me, all who labor more than average." He said, "all who labor and are heavy laden." The invitation is for anyone who is tired.

The third lie: "I will rest once this season is over." This is the most dangerous of the three, because the season rarely ends on its own. One deadline replaces another. The children grow, and the parents age. Ministry never runs out of need. If you keep waiting for rest to become convenient, exhaustion may begin to make decisions for you, through your health, your relationships, your emotional reactions or your spiritual life.

Elijah reached a place of deep distress and exhaustion. Under the broom tree, he asked God to take his life, and then he slept. We do not have to wait until we reach such a point before making room for rest.

16 · Permission

Some of you do not need convincing. You need permission. So let me give it as plainly as a pastor can.

You are allowed to stop. Scripture shows us that rest matters to God, and Jesus himself made room to withdraw and pray. You do not have to earn permission to recognise your limits and make responsible arrangements for rest.

Taking these days does not mean abandoning your responsibilities. Preparing for a retreat can itself be an act of responsibility. If you are married, explain to your spouse what you need, and listen to any practical concerns. If others depend on you, arrange appropriate care before you go. If a longer retreat is not possible for you, a shorter one is still a real retreat, and Lesson 6 will help you choose.

Not everyone will understand your decision straight away. You do not need to defend your exhaustion. But you should communicate clearly, make responsible arrangements, and protect the time you have set aside.

17 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too.

First: which of the three lies sounds most like you? Write it out in your own words.

Second: what must be arranged, delegated, postponed or explained so that you can step away responsibly and without unnecessary distraction? Make a short, practical list.

Whatever you write, bring it with you. The retreat has room for it.

18 · Remember

Let us bring it together. God commanded rest, and rest declares that God, not you, holds the world together. Jesus withdrew in order to return, and a retreat is time with God with the intention of returning. Jonah fled in resistance and Elijah in fear, and God dealt with both; the question is whether we will turn towards him honestly. And the lies that keep you going are not the truth: you are allowed to stop.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord God, you rested from your work, and you commanded your people to stop. Thank you that you, not I, hold the world together. Lord Jesus, you withdrew to pray, and returned to serve. Help me to step away in order to come back, facing towards you and not away. Quiet the voices that tell me I cannot stop. In Jesus' name. Amen.
