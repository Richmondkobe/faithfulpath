1 · How does caring for my body belong to meeting God?

Welcome back. In Lesson 9 you packed your bag, including food and anything you need for your health. This lesson is about the body itself: whether to fast, what to eat, how to rest, and how to look after yourself during the retreat.

Christians sometimes treat the body as an obstacle to spiritual life. Scripture does not. Psalm 139 receives embodied life with wonder: "I am fearfully and wonderfully made." Caring for your body is not separate from faithfulness, because we meet God as whole persons. The body you bring to the retreat is the only one you have.

So this lesson asks: how does caring for my body belong to meeting God?

2 · A word first

A necessary word first. I am a pastor, not a physician. This lesson offers general pastoral guidance, not an assessment of whether any practice is safe for you. Health risks are not always obvious, and no general list can account for every condition, medication, age or circumstance.

Ordinary eating is the default for this retreat. If you wish to change your food or fluid intake, get advice from a qualified healthcare professional who knows your health history, before the retreat, not during it.

3 · Arise and eat

Our first passage is one we met in Lesson 1. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. First Kings chapter 19, verses 5 to 8:

"And he lay down and slept under a broom tree. And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, “Arise and eat.” And he looked, and behold, there was at his head a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water. And he ate and drank and lay down again. And the angel of the LORD came again a second time and touched him and said, “Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.” And he arose and ate and drank, and went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God."

4 · He gives to his beloved sleep

And our second passage is a single verse from Psalm 127:

"It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep."

5 · Elijah fed twice

Now the background. Elijah’s story has followed us through this course. In Lesson 1 we saw that God’s first recorded response to his exhaustion was not correction or instruction. It was food, water and sleep. He was given food and water twice, with time to rest. The second time came with a reason: "for the journey is too great for you."

Only afterwards came the journey, the mountain, and God’s word to him at Horeb. Elijah did not fast. He was fed. In this story, God’s care for Elijah’s body came first. That is not a rule for every situation, but it is the thread of this lesson.

6 · Fasting in Scripture

Scripture does treat fasting as a recognised practice. In Matthew 6, Jesus speaks of fasting as something his followers do, and teaches them to do it without display. The passage does not set a length, or make fasting right for every person and circumstance. The early church fasted before major decisions, in Acts 13.

But Scripture is equally clear that fasting is not a technique for getting things from God. In Isaiah 58, God rebukes people who go without food while continuing oppression and conflict. Religious self-denial is empty when it is separated from justice, generosity and care for the vulnerable.

And Jesus’ forty days in the wilderness are sometimes held up as a model. As Lesson 3 said, they are not. That was a unique time of preparation, and you are not being asked to imitate it.

7 · The short answer

So, how does caring for my body belong to meeting God? Here is the short answer. The default is to eat normally, drink according to your usual health needs, and rest. Food fasting is optional, and only if it is already familiar to you and confirmed as appropriate by a healthcare professional who knows your current health. A non-food form of restraint is often the wiser choice. And any practice that causes concerning symptoms stops, and you seek help.

That gives us four points: eat normally, fasting is optional; simple, nourishing food; sleep is a gift; and walk, do not train.

8 · Point 1: Eat Normally; Fasting Is Optional

The first point: eat normally; fasting is optional. Let me be clear. This retreat does not require fasting. You can complete every session, with full benefit, while eating normally. For a retreat focused on burnout and restoration, my general recommendation is to eat normally and simply.

You do not need physical hunger to make the retreat spiritually serious. That is why fasting is placed in this lesson, rather than in the sessions: so that no one feels it is expected.

9 · When not to fast

Some people should not fast without individual medical guidance. These are examples, not a complete list, and not being on the list does not prove fasting is safe for you. Do not fast without medical guidance if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive; if you are under eighteen; if you are an older adult who has been advised to eat regularly; or if you have diabetes or any condition affecting blood sugar.

The same applies if you have a heart, kidney, liver or digestive condition, or any chronic illness; if you take regular medication; if you are recovering from illness, surgery or significant exhaustion; or if you have a history of fainting, migraines, anaemia or low blood pressure.

And separately, without exception: this course does not recommend food fasting for anyone with a current or past eating disorder or disordered eating. Fasting can reawaken patterns that took a long time to escape, under a spiritual disguise that makes them harder to recognise. Follow your established meal plan and any treatment guidance. If the retreat’s simplicity around food begins to feel like an invitation to restrict, pause the retreat exercises and contact your treatment provider or support person. Nothing in this retreat is worth that risk.

If any of this applies to you, there is no spiritual loss at all. Remember: Elijah did not fast. He was fed.

10 · If you do fast

Only consider food fasting if it is already a healthy, established practice for you, and a qualified healthcare professional who knows your current health and medications has confirmed that it is appropriate. Then keep the retreat fast modest, familiar, and less intensive than usual. Do not try a new or extended fast while you are alone. And whatever you do, do not restrict water. Follow your usual fluid intake, including any medical instructions.

Stop at once if you feel dizzy, faint, confused or shaky, if your heart races, if you develop a severe headache, or if you feel unwell in a way you cannot explain. Sit or lie down somewhere safe, and do not drive. If symptoms are severe or getting worse, seek urgent medical help, and do not remain alone. There is no spiritual merit in pushing through a body that is telling you to stop.

And remember that food is not the only way to practise restraint. The digital boundary from Lesson 9, or a pause from entertainment, news or unnecessary speech, can create space without changing your nutrition. For many people taking this course, that is the wiser choice.

11 · Point 2: Simple, Nourishing Food

The second point: simple, nourishing food. For everyone, fasting or not, the goal is simple: eat well, eat simply, and do not make food a project. Plan meals that need little preparation. Choose familiar food that meets your dietary, allergy, medical and cultural needs. Do not use the retreat to start a restrictive diet or try unfamiliar foods. If meals are provided, receive them gratefully, and mention any real needs without embarrassment.

And eat enough. Some people, in a spirit of simplicity, under-eat on retreat without meaning to, and then wonder why they cannot concentrate by the second afternoon. Simplicity is about not fussing, not about going without.

If you can, eat slowly, and without reading or listening to anything. A meal eaten with attention and thanks is itself a small act of retreat. After his resurrection, Jesus cooked breakfast on a beach for tired fishermen. Meals are part of how God cares for people. Let them be part of how he cares for you.

12 · Caffeine and alcohol

Two practical cautions. First, avoid a sudden change to your usual caffeine routine during the retreat. A sudden change can cause headaches and irritability that are easy to mistake for spiritual distress. If you want to cut down, do it gradually beforehand.

Second, alcohol is not part of this retreat, because it affects judgement, attention, sleep and mood. But if you drink heavily or regularly and may be physically dependent, do not use the retreat to stop suddenly on your own. Alcohol withdrawal can be medically dangerous. Seek medical advice and support before you change, as Lesson 5 said.

13 · Point 3: Sleep Is a Gift

The third point: sleep is a gift. Psalm 127 ends with a line that exhausted Christians need to hear: "he gives to his beloved sleep." Sleep is described as a gift, not an interruption. Elijah was allowed to rest, and was fed twice, before continuing his journey. Jesus slept through a storm. And the wider psalm is about depending on God, rather than anxious striving.

In Gethsemane, the disciples’ sleep was a failure to keep watch at a unique and solemn moment. Yet Jesus’ words also recognised their bodily weakness: "the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." We should not use that scene to excuse inattention, but neither should we pretend that bodily limits are unreal.

So sleep. If you arrive exhausted, you may spend a good part of day one asleep, and that is fine. Do not set an alarm for five in the morning to prove your seriousness. Keep a sleep pattern that suits your health and the retreat schedule. If you need an afternoon nap, take one. Rest is not time stolen from the retreat. It is part of the retreat. And keep your normal routines: take any prescribed sleep medication as usual, and do not try unfamiliar sleep aids.

14 · Point 4: Walk, Do Not Train

The fourth point: walk, do not train. Several sessions suggest walking. On the road to Emmaus, two disciples walked and talked through their grief, and Jesus joined them on the way. Some people find that gentle walking helps thoughts and feelings become easier to notice.

Movement is optional. This is not the time for a long run or a hard workout, especially if you are tired. Gentle movement, within your ability and near help, is what the retreat needs. Follow any medical advice, use mobility aids as you need them, and do not walk alone in unsafe conditions. If your mobility is limited, adapt the walking sessions to whatever movement is possible for you, or simply to sitting somewhere different, outdoors if you can. The point is a change of position and place, not distance.

15 · When you become unwell

And if you become unwell during the retreat, treat it as you would at home. Rest, follow your usual care, and seek professional advice if symptoms are unfamiliar, severe or getting worse. Do not spiritualise a fever. If you are ill enough that you would normally stay in bed, stay in bed, and let the sessions wait. The retreat is not undone by a day of illness. It is simply paused, and you can resume or reschedule.

If you have a contagious illness at a shared place, tell the host or retreat leader, and protect others. And if an injury or a chronic condition flares, follow your usual care, and do not push through pain to complete a session. The sessions are offered, not assigned, and your body’s limits are legitimate limits.

16 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 10 worksheet. Your main step is a simple plan for your body, with three parts.

First, food. The default is ordinary, nourishing food. Write a simple meal plan for each day: what you will eat, and how you will get it. Second, sleep. Write down the time you will allow yourself to sleep, and to wake, so that you do not renegotiate it with yourself at five in the morning. And third, movement. What gentle movement, if any, will you include, and where is it safe? These are gentle plans, not targets. Adjust them if your health or your need for rest requires it.

If you are considering a food fast, ask yourself two questions. Is it already a healthy, established practice for you? And has a healthcare professional who knows your current health and medications confirmed that it is appropriate? If either answer is no, choose a non-food form of restraint instead.

17 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Eat normally; fasting is optional, and you lose nothing by eating. Choose simple, nourishing food, and eat enough. Sleep is a gift, not an interruption: he gives to his beloved sleep. And walk, do not train, gently and within your ability. God cared for Elijah’s body before he gave him the journey. He cares for yours too.

18 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord, you made me, body and soul, fearfully and wonderfully. You fed Elijah when the journey was too great for him. Teach me to receive food, sleep and rest as your gifts, and to listen to my body with wisdom, not with shame. Care for me, as you have always cared for your people. Amen.

When you are ready, write your simple plan for food, sleep and movement, and then continue with Lesson 11.

Health and safety

Please read before you plan

Without exception: this course does not recommend food fasting for anyone with a current or past eating disorder or disordered eating. Follow your established meal plan and any treatment guidance. If the retreat’s simplicity around food begins to feel like an invitation to restrict, or food-related thoughts intensify, pause the retreat exercises and contact your treatment provider or support person.

Do not fast without individual medical guidance if, for example, you are pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive; are under eighteen; are an older adult advised to eat regularly; have diabetes or any condition affecting blood sugar; have a heart, kidney, liver or digestive condition, or any chronic illness; take regular medication; are recovering from illness, surgery or significant exhaustion; or have a history of fainting, migraines, anaemia or low blood pressure. This list is not complete.

Do not restrict water. If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, do not stop suddenly on your own: withdrawal can be dangerous, so seek medical advice first.

If you feel dizzy, faint, confused or shaky, your heart races, or you feel unwell in a way you cannot explain, stop any fast, sit or lie down somewhere safe, and do not drive. If symptoms are severe or getting worse (fainting, chest pain, confusion, difficulty breathing, seizure), call your local emergency number or seek urgent medical help, and do not remain alone.
