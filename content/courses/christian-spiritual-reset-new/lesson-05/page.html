1 · What can I honestly hope for?

Welcome back. This is the last lesson of Part 1. In Lesson 4 we named three experiences: burnout, dryness and overload. If something in that lesson described you more seriously than you expected, thank you for coming back. Nothing in this lesson is meant to disqualify you from the retreat. It is meant to help you enter it with the right expectations and the right support, so that the time helps you rather than harms you.

And if Lesson 4 did not describe you, this lesson is still for you. A retreat is a good thing, and good things get misused when we ask them to do more than they can.

So this lesson asks: what can I honestly hope for?

2 · Hope and expectation

One of the most helpful things you can do before a retreat is to separate two things that often get mixed together. The first is what you are asking of God. The second is what you are expecting your retreat to produce.

You may bring every desire to God, and you should. But a retreat is a limited thing, held in the hands of a God who is not limited. This lesson will help you see the difference, so that you go in with honest hope, and without demands that the retreat was never meant to carry.

3 · Bear one another’s burdens

Our first passage is from Paul’s letter to the Galatians. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Galatians chapter 6, verses 1 and 2:

"Brothers, if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness. Keep watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted. Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ."

4 · An abundance of counselors

Our second passage is a single proverb. Proverbs chapter 11, verse 14:

"Where there is no guidance, a people falls, but in an abundance of counselors there is safety."

5 · The beloved physician

And two short verses to go with them. In Luke chapter 5, verse 31, Jesus says:

"Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick."

And at the end of Colossians, in chapter 4, verse 14, Paul sends greetings from one of his companions:

"Luke the beloved physician greets you, as does Demas."

Shared burdens, wise counsel, and a physician among the apostles’ closest companions. Hold these together as we go on.

6 · A doctor in the mission team

Now the background. When Jesus said that the sick need a physician, he was speaking about sinners who needed him, the true physician. It is a spiritual picture. But the picture only works because everyone knew the ordinary truth behind it: when you are ill, you seek a doctor.

And Scripture shows that ordinary truth in practice. Paul calls Luke "the beloved physician". Luke is traditionally understood to have travelled with Paul and to have written the Gospel of Luke and Acts. And when Timothy, Paul’s young colleague, suffered from his stomach and frequent illnesses, Paul gave him practical advice for his health.

Scripture does not force us to choose between dependence on God and receiving care through ordinary means. God can work through both.

7 · Some burdens are too heavy alone

Look again at Galatians 6. Paul is writing to churches, and he tells them to restore one another gently, and to bear one another’s burdens. That command assumes something important: that some burdens are too heavy to carry alone.

This matters for a retreat. Solitude is one of the means God uses to help us. The body of Christ is another, and it is not optional. A retreat taken as a permanent escape from other people is not what this course is recommending.

8 · Elijah ate and slept twice

And one more piece of background, from the man we met in Lesson 1. When Elijah lay down under the broom tree, God did not begin with a lesson. An angel woke him with food and water, and he lay down again. Then the angel came again:

"And the angel of the LORD came again a second time and touched him and said, “Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.”"

Before Elijah’s encounter with God at Horeb, the account tells us that he received food, water and rest. Scripture does not separate bodily needs from spiritual life. Sometimes the most spiritual thing we can do is to rest, to eat, and to receive the care God provides.

9 · The short answer

So, what can I honestly hope for? Here is the short answer. A retreat can be a real beginning. It cannot do everything, and it was never meant to. Seeking help is not a lack of faith. And you can choose the path that fits where you are now.

That gives us four points: what a retreat can do; what it cannot do; help is not a lack of faith; and choose your path.

10 · Point 1: What a Retreat Can Do

The first point: what a retreat can do. Let me start with what you can reasonably hope for, because it is real.

A retreat can interrupt a pattern. Most of us live inside rhythms we did not choose and no longer notice. Time outside them lets you see them. It can give you unhurried honesty before God, time to say what you have not said. It can help you name what you are carrying: a grief, a resentment, a fear, a loss of direction. Naming does not resolve it, but it lets you respond with honesty and care. It can restore perspective, so that you see what matters, what you have neglected, and what you have overvalued.

It can become a place where you meet God, or become more aware of his presence. I say "can" deliberately. God is not absent until a retreat begins, but we cannot manufacture an experience of him. God is not summoned by a schedule, and a retreat is not a transaction. You make yourself available, without demanding a particular feeling, answer or outcome.

And it can begin things: a decision to seek help, a conversation you have avoided, a rhythm of rest, a step towards forgiveness. Beginnings are not completions, but they are how completions start.

11 · Point 2: What It Cannot Do

The second point: what it cannot do. A retreat cannot replace medical or psychological care. If you are living with depression, an anxiety disorder, an eating disorder, an addiction or the effects of trauma, a retreat may be part of your care, but it is not the care itself.

It cannot fix in three days what took years to form. Burnout with structural causes will not be cured by rest alone. Long-buried grief will not be fully processed in one weekend. The retreat is a beginning, and the lessons after it exist because a beginning needs a next step.

It cannot guarantee that you will feel God’s presence. Some people will finish with a strong sense of having met God. Others will finish having been faithful and honest, and still feeling dry. Both have done the retreat. Psalm 88 ends in darkness, and God kept it in his book.

It cannot substitute for community, as we saw in Galatians. And it cannot make a difficult decision for you.

12 · Hold impressions lightly

Some people come to a retreat with a major choice in front of them, hoping for clarity. The listening sessions may help. But any of us can mistake fear, desire, memory or strong emotion for God’s direction, especially when we are tired or distressed.

So hold everything lightly. Write down what you sense. No impression that contradicts Scripture, promotes harm, inflates your importance, removes accountability or demands reckless urgency should be accepted as God’s direction.

Unless you need to act to protect yourself or someone else from harm, avoid making major irreversible decisions during the retreat. Record your impressions, seek wise counsel, and test them carefully after the retreat. Lesson 12 will help you do that. Until then, treat impressions as impressions.

13 · Point 3: Help Is Not a Lack of Faith

The third point: help is not a lack of faith. Christians sometimes worry that seeking counselling or medication means their faith has failed. It does not. Proverbs teaches the wisdom of seeking sound counsel: "in an abundance of counselors there is safety." Modern professional counselling is not exactly what the proverb describes, but receiving qualified help fits its rejection of isolated decision-making.

Receiving help from a doctor, a counsellor and a pastor does not divide your trust in God. Each can contribute different knowledge, care and support, and wise care often involves them working together.

So, if you are already receiving care from a doctor, counsellor or therapist, tell them you are planning this retreat, and ask whether anything should be adjusted for you. Their advice is worth taking. And if Lesson 4 suggested that you might need care, please take one step towards it before you begin, even if it is only a phone call to book an appointment. Do not let the retreat become the reason you postpone help you already know you need.

14 · When support comes first

Some situations need support before or during a retreat. They include thoughts of not wanting to be alive, abuse in the past or present, physical dependence on alcohol or sedatives, strong reactions to trauma, and medical advice not to spend long periods alone.

If any of these applies to you, please read the note below this lesson before you go on. It says, in the book’s own words, what to do first. A retreat will still be here when you are safe and supported. Postponing is not failing. It may be the wisest way to prepare.

15 · When to pause

Whatever your situation, here is a simple rule for the days ahead. If a session brings up more than you can hold, stop the session. Not the retreat, necessarily, but the session.

Stand up. Go outside if you can. Walk, drink some water, eat something. Pray simply, even if the only prayer you have is, "Lord, help." Contact your support person. And if the reaction is severe, or involves danger to you or anyone else, contact a professional, a crisis service or emergency services, rather than relying only on a friend.

You do not have to finish the exercise that brought it up. You may return later, change to a supported format, or stop the retreat altogether. You do not need to continue simply because you started. The sessions are offered, not assigned. A retreat that is paused is not a retreat that failed. Elijah slept and ate twice before God spoke to him. Pausing is often exactly how God cares for us.

16 · Point 4: Choose Your Path

The fourth point: choose your path. Before you begin, choose one of four paths.

First, proceed independently: you feel stable, safe alone, and able to pause when you need to. Second, proceed with support: arrange regular contact with a trusted person or a professional during the retreat. Third, use an adapted format: a shorter, guided or group version, which Lesson 6 will help you choose. And fourth, postpone for now: seek professional, medical, crisis or safety support first.

All four paths are faithful choices. Postponing is not failing. It may be the wisest way to prepare.

17 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 5 worksheet.

First: is there any support you need to arrange before you begin? Write it down, and write down when you will arrange it. Second: who is the person you will call if a session becomes too much? Have you told them? And do you know how to reach professional or crisis help if you need it?

Third: what are you hoping the retreat will do? Mark anything that belongs in the "cannot" list. You may still bring every desire to God, but separate what you are asking of God from what you are expecting your retreat to produce. And fourth: which of the four paths are you choosing?

18 · Remember

Let us bring it together. A retreat can be a real beginning, but it is not a transaction, and God is not summoned by a schedule. It cannot replace care, community or wise counsel, and it cannot decide for you. Seeking help is not a lack of faith; God works through ordinary means, as he did through Luke the physician. And you may choose the path that fits where you are now. Postponing is not failing.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord God, I bring you everything I hope for. Help me to ask you freely, and to expect wisely. Thank you for the people and the care you provide through ordinary means. Give me humility to ask for help, and courage to pause when I need to. Lead me on the path that is right for me now. In Jesus’ name. Amen.

When you are ready, answer your four questions and choose your path. Part 2 begins with Lesson 6.

Before you begin

When support comes first

Some situations need support before or during a retreat. These are important examples, not a complete list.

  • Thoughts of not wanting to be alive. Do not rely on this retreat as your response. Tell a trusted person and contact a qualified mental-health professional or crisis service promptly. If you believe you may act on those thoughts, have made a plan, or cannot keep yourself safe, contact your local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department now, and ask someone trustworthy to stay with you.
  • Abuse, past or present. If you are being harmed now, your immediate priority is safety. A private retreat is not a substitute for specialist support or a safety plan. Contact a domestic-abuse service using a safe device if possible.
  • Dependence on alcohol, sedatives or another substance. Do not use a retreat to stop suddenly without medical advice; withdrawal from some substances can be dangerous. Speak with a doctor or addiction service first.
  • Trauma reactions. If silence, prayer or recalling the past tends to bring flashbacks, panic or dissociation, plan the retreat with a qualified trauma-informed professional, and consider a shorter or supported format.
  • Medical advice not to be alone, or if you are unsure whether solitude is safe for you now: seek professional guidance before you begin.

The book’s full list, including behavioural addiction and other conditions that can make extended solitude unwise, is in Go deeper. Postponing is not failing.
