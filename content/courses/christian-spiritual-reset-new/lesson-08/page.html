1 · How do I get ready inside, not only outside?

Welcome back. In Lesson 6 you chose a length and a place. In Lesson 7 you wrote your intention. Now we turn to something less visible: your heart.

A retreat does not begin when you arrive. It begins in the days before, when you decide whether to come towards it slowly, or to crash into it at full speed. A little preparation may reduce the restlessness of the first day, and help you arrive with greater attention.

So this lesson asks: how do I get ready inside, not only outside?

2 · Begin where you are

This lesson begins the week before your retreat. Lessons 9, 10 and 11 are practical, and you can work through them during that same week.

Before we start, one reassurance. If you cannot complete this preparation, you can still take the retreat honestly and benefit from it. And a restless retreat is not necessarily a failed retreat. Sometimes restlessness is itself part of what needs to be noticed, with compassion.

3 · Like a weaned child

Our first passage is a short psalm. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Psalm 131, a Song of Ascents, of David:

"O LORD, my heart is not lifted up; my eyes are not raised too high; I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me. But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me. O Israel, hope in the LORD from this time forth and forevermore."

4 · Leave your gift there

And our second passage comes from the Sermon on the Mount. Matthew chapter 5, verses 23 and 24:

"So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift."

5 · Quieted = dāmam

Our Hebrew word in this lesson comes from verse 2: "But I have calmed and quieted my soul." The word translated "quieted" is dāmam. It means to become still, or silent.

Notice how the psalmist uses it. He does not say, "My soul became quiet." He says, "I have quieted my soul." The verb is active. He did not arrive at that stillness by accident. He calmed and quieted his own soul, before God.

That does not mean stillness is something we achieve by effort alone. But it does mean that we can take part. This lesson is about beginning to do the same.

6 · A weaned child

Now the background. Psalm 131 is one of the Songs of Ascents, Psalms 120 to 134. They are usually understood as songs for the journey up to Jerusalem, as God’s people went up to worship. It is a fitting psalm for someone on the way to meet with God.

And the picture at its centre is a weaned child. A nursing infant comes to its mother urgently, seeking milk. Unlike that infant, the weaned child pictures a more settled kind of nearness. The psalmist uses this image to describe a soul that has learned to rest quietly before God.

He is not demanding an immediate answer or outcome. That is where this week is leading: not to emptiness, but to settled nearness.

7 · The gift left at the altar

Our second passage comes in the middle of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has just said that anger and contempt matter to God, not only murder. Then he gives this picture: a worshipper at the altar suddenly remembers that someone has something against him. Jesus tells him to leave the gift where it is, and go.

Jesus describes a situation where someone has something against us. When we have genuinely wronged another person, we must not use worship as an excuse to avoid taking responsibility. Where it is safe and appropriate, we acknowledge the wrong, apologise without excuses, make it right where we can, and leave the other person free to respond.

Notice also what Jesus is not saying. He is not telling you to chase reconciliation with everyone who has wronged you, or to contact someone who is unsafe. We will come back to that carefully.

8 · The short answer

So, how do I get ready inside, not only outside? Here is the short answer. Begin slowing down before you arrive. Notice your resistance, and respond to it wisely. Deal with what you can: a wrong you have done, or a sin you already know. And pray, ask others to pray, and hand over your responsibilities well.

That gives us four points: slow down before you arrive; notice your resistance; unfinished relationships and known sin; and pray, ask others to pray, and hand over well.

9 · Point 1: Slow Down Before You Arrive

The first point: slow down before you arrive. Think about what happens when a person who has been running for months finally sits down. The body sits; the mind keeps running. Lists appear. Anxieties that were held off by activity move into the empty space.

Many people who go on retreat with no preparation spend the first day, and sometimes the second, simply coming down from the speed they arrived at. That is not wasted, as Lesson 6 said. But if you can begin the slowing before you arrive, the retreat itself has more room.

10 · A seven-psalm week

Here is a simple practice for that week. For the five to seven days before your retreat, set aside ten minutes a day. Not more, unless you want to. Each day has a psalm.

Day one, Psalm 23: God’s care. Day two, Psalm 62: rest and waiting. Day three, Psalm 27: one central desire, which you met in Lesson 7. Day four, Psalm 42: spiritual thirst. Day five, Psalm 63: longing for God. Day six, Psalm 139: honest self-examination. And day seven, Psalm 131: quiet trust.

If you have fewer than seven days, choose the psalms that will help you prepare. You do not need to catch up, or complete every reading. The full plan is below this lesson, where you can tick off each day.

11 · Each day

Each day, do three things. First, read the psalm twice, the second time more slowly. Do not analyse every verse. Notice which words comfort you, challenge you, or describe something in you.

Second, sit quietly for up to three minutes. Set a timer, so that you do not have to watch the clock. Notice your breathing, your surroundings, and one phrase from the psalm. If thoughts arise, acknowledge them without judgement, and gently return to the phrase. If three minutes is too much, begin with one. If closing your eyes feels uncomfortable, leave them open. And if silence feels unsettling rather than calming, shorten it, or take a gentle walk while repeating the line. The purpose is attention, not endurance.

Third, pray one sentence about the retreat. Something like: "Lord, I am coming to be with you. Prepare me, and prepare the days." That is enough.

By the end of the week, you may or may not feel calmer. Either way, you will have practised returning to quiet, and learned something about how you respond when you slow down.

12 · Point 2: Notice Your Resistance

The second point: notice your resistance. Somewhere in that week, resistance may appear. It often does. It may arrive as a sudden urgency about tasks that could easily wait. It may arrive as a feeling that the retreat is self-indulgent, that this is not the right time, that someone else needs you more. Or it may arrive as a vague dread you cannot explain.

Resistance is information, not a command. It does not automatically mean you should cancel, and it does not automatically mean you should push through. So ask: what might this resistance be telling me? Is it ordinary reluctance to slow down? Fear of what may surface? A practical problem? Or a genuine concern about safety and readiness?

13 · Responding wisely

Sometimes the answer is a responsibility that needs attention before you go. If so, handle it. Sometimes the answer is a real concern about safety, health or readiness, and then the four paths from Lesson 5 apply.

And sometimes the resistance is reluctance to face something the quiet may surface: a grief, a truth about your work, a relationship you have been avoiding, a question about God you have not let yourself ask. If that is what you suspect, write it down, and bring the page with you. Do not try to resolve it beforehand. Simply name it, so that when it appears on the retreat, you will recognise it as something you already expected.

Not every distraction, scheduling problem or anxious feeling is spiritual opposition. Some are ordinary features of being human, and some need practical or professional attention. You do not need to dramatise the week before the retreat. Notice what is happening, pray honestly, and respond wisely. Elijah arrived in the wilderness afraid and exhausted, and God met him with care, in the condition in which he came. Whatever condition you arrive in, that is the God you are coming to.

14 · Point 3: Unfinished Relationships and Known Sin

The third point: unfinished relationships and known sin. Applied to the week before a retreat, Jesus’ words lead to a simple question: is there anyone I have hurt, or am in open conflict with, whom I could reasonably approach before I go?

Not every strained relationship. Not old wounds that need the retreat itself to be looked at. But a sharp word you have not apologised for, a debt you have not acknowledged, a misunderstanding you could begin to address through one honest conversation. If something like that exists, and it is safe and wise to address it, do so before you leave.

Make the apology without demanding forgiveness or immediate reconciliation. Do not approach someone merely to make yourself feel lighter before the retreat. Centre the harm done to them, accept that they may not forgive you straight away, and do not pressure them to resolve things on your timetable.

15 · Two cautions

Two cautions, both of which Lesson 5 anticipated. First, this is about responsibility for a wrong you have genuinely done. You are not required to contact someone who has harmed you, or who is unsafe. Forgiveness and reconciliation are not the same thing, and Session 6 will hold that distinction carefully.

Second, do not use this principle to talk yourself out of going. If a relationship is too complicated to address in a week, it is exactly the sort of thing to bring to the retreat, not a reason to postpone it.

16 · Confession before you go

The same logic applies to sin. Sometimes an unconfessed wrong weighs on our conscience, and makes it difficult to rest honestly before God. In Psalm 32, the psalmist describes the weight of keeping silent, and the relief when, as he says, "I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity." And First John gives the promise plainly:

"If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness."

If there is something like that in your life, name it to God in the week before. Confess it, and receive the forgiveness promised in Christ. Session 5 is about a deeper, gentler kind of confession, the kind that surfaces slowly in quiet. This is the other kind: the thing you already know. Forgiveness removes condemnation, but it does not make every consequence disappear. If your actions harmed another person, or involve abuse, crime, misconduct or someone’s present safety, seek qualified guidance about accountability and reporting. A private retreat must never become a way around those responsibilities.

But do not turn this into a search. Lesson 4 said that dryness is not always caused by sin. Confess what you know, and leave the rest to God. If you find yourself confessing the same thing again and again because you cannot feel forgiven, or searching anxiously for hidden sin, do not intensify the exercise. Speak with a pastor, or a mental-health professional who understands religious anxiety.

17 · Point 4: Pray, Ask Others to Pray, and Hand Over Well

The fourth point: pray, ask others to pray, and hand over well. King Jehoshaphat faced a national crisis, very different from yours. But Second Chronicles describes his response in a phrase worth keeping: he "was afraid and set his face to seek the Lord." It is not an instruction to take a retreat, but it is an example of deliberately seeking God. Setting your face is a chosen act, and it is a good description of what this week is for. You are turning, in advance, towards the days you have set aside.

So pray for them. Pray for the practical things: that the arrangements hold, that the interruptions are few, that your body rests. And pray for the deeper things: that you will be honest, that you will not run, and that God will meet you in whatever way he chooses.

And ask others to pray too. Ask your support person, or one or two trustworthy people, to pray for you on each of the retreat days. Choose someone steady, who respects confidentiality, and who will not demand details or interpret the retreat for you. You do not need to explain why you are going. "I am taking some days to be with God, and I would value your prayers" is enough. James tells us that the prayer of a righteous person has great power. Being prayed for connects your solitude to the community you will return to.

18 · Handing over well

One practical note, because it affects the heart more than people expect. If you are leaving work, ministry or family responsibilities in someone else’s hands, hand them over properly. Write down what needs doing and who is doing it. Tell people when you will return, and how genuine emergencies should be handled. Where possible, route urgent contact through one designated person, and give that person clear authority to handle agreed matters without consulting you.

You met Jethro in Lesson 4. He told Moses that carrying everything alone was not good, and that the remedy was to share the load. A poorly handed-over responsibility will follow you into the retreat as a low hum of worry. A well handed-over one lets you be absent in fact, not only in location.

19 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 8 worksheet. Your main step is to begin the seven-psalm week. Write down which day you will start, the time, and the place.

As you prepare, use four more questions to help you notice resistance, address what needs attention, arrange your handover, and ask for prayer. First, what resistance have you already noticed? Without analysing it, write what you think it may be telling you: ordinary reluctance, a practical problem, a safety concern, or something the quiet may surface.

Second, is there a relationship, or a known sin, that you can and should address before you go? If yes, write what you will do, and when. If no, or if it is not safe, write that down too, so you are not carrying it as an unanswered question.

Third, hand over your responsibilities in writing, and name one person for emergencies. And fourth, ask one person to pray for you on each retreat day. Write down who, and exactly what you will say.

20 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Slow down before you arrive: ten minutes a day, a psalm, and one sentence of prayer. Notice your resistance; it is information, not a command. Address the unfinished relationships and known sin that you safely can, and bring the rest to the retreat. And pray, ask others to pray, and hand over well, so that you can be absent in fact, not only in location.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord, I am coming to be with you. Quiet my heart, as a child rests with its mother. Show me what I need to put right, and give me courage to do it, and grace to leave the rest with you. Prepare me, and prepare the days. Amen.

Your step

Begin the seven-psalm week

Your main step: which day this week will you begin the seven-psalm week? Write the time and the place.

As you prepare, four questions to help you notice resistance, address what needs attention, arrange your handover and ask for prayer:

  1. What resistance have you already noticed? Write down, without analysing it, what you think it may be telling you: ordinary reluctance, a practical problem, a safety concern, or something the quiet may surface.
  2. Is there a relationship or a known sin that Matthew 5 or 1 John 1 applies to, one you can and should address before you go? If yes, what will you do and when? If no, or if it is not safe, write that down so you are not carrying it as an unanswered question.

A note about confession

When confession needs more than prayer

If your actions harmed another person, or involve abuse, exploitation, criminal conduct, professional misconduct, or someone’s present safety, seek qualified guidance about accountability, reporting, restitution and appropriate disclosure. A private retreat or a confidential conversation must never become a way around safeguarding or legal responsibility.

If you find yourself confessing the same matter again and again because you cannot feel forgiven, or searching anxiously for hidden sin, do not intensify the exercise. Speak with a pastor, or a mental-health professional who understands religious anxiety.

You are never required to contact someone who has harmed you, or who is unsafe.

Your intention

For groups, or with a friend

Talk about it

  1. What does the picture of a weaned child, content to stay near, say to you about being with God?
