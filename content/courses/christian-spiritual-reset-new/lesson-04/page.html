1 · What is actually happening to me?

Welcome back. In the first three lessons we used words like tired, dry and overloaded almost as if they meant the same thing. In Lesson 3 we saw how Jesus withdrew to be with his Father. Now, before you enter the retreat, it will help to look more carefully at what you are carrying.

Because tired, dry and overloaded are not the same, and they do not need the same care. Many of us will recognise ourselves in more than one of them. That is common. But naming what is actually happening is the first honest act of the retreat.

So this lesson asks: what is actually happening to me?

2 · A pastor, not a doctor

We will look at three experiences: burnout, spiritual dryness and emotional overload.

A word of care before we begin. What follows is a pastoral description, not a medical or psychological assessment. I am a pastor experienced in counselling, not a doctor. Some of what we describe overlaps with depression, anxiety, chronic stress and physical illness, and this lesson cannot tell you which is which. These three experiences often overlap, and none of them is a diagnosis.

If, as you listen, you think, "this has gone further than tiredness," please take that seriously. There is a short note below this lesson about when to ask for more help, and Lesson 5 will say more.

3 · From morning till evening

Our first passage comes from the wilderness, soon after God brought Israel out of Egypt. Moses’ father-in-law, Jethro, comes to visit, and watches him at work. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Exodus chapter 18, verses 13 and 14:

"The next day Moses sat to judge the people, and the people stood around Moses from morning till evening. When Moses’ father-in-law saw all that he was doing for the people, he said, “What is this that you are doing for the people? Why do you sit alone, and all the people stand around you from morning till evening?”"

And in verses 17 and 18, Jethro says to him:

"What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone."

4 · My soul thirsts for God

Our second passage is a prayer from someone who felt far from God. Psalm 42, verses 1 to 3:

"As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God? My tears have been my food day and night, while they say to me all the day long, “Where is your God?”"

5 · Burdened beyond our strength

And our third passage comes from the apostle Paul. Second Corinthians chapter 1, verses 8 and 9:

"For we do not want you to be unaware, brothers, of the affliction we experienced in Asia. For we were so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself. Indeed, we felt that we had received the sentence of death. But that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead."

A leader who could not carry it alone, a soul thirsting for God, and an apostle burdened beyond his strength. Each of them is in Scripture so that we would know we are not the first.

6 · Jethro’s answer

Now the background. Israel was a nation of many thousands, and Moses was trying to hear their disputes himself. Every case came to him, from morning till evening. Jethro saw it at once.

His advice was practical. Moses would continue to represent the people before God and teach them God’s ways. Capable, trustworthy people, who feared God, would be appointed to judge ordinary cases, while the difficult cases would come to Moses. Jethro said:

"So it will be easier for you, and they will bear the burden with you."

Notice what the remedy was. It was not a call to try harder, or to pray more so that he could keep going. It was a structural answer: shared responsibility. And Jethro added that Moses should do this if God commanded him.

7 · Too heavy to carry alone

Later, in Numbers chapter 11, the people complain bitterly about their food, and Moses comes to the end of himself. He says to God:

"I am not able to carry all this people alone; the burden is too heavy for me."

Moses was in such distress that he asked God to take his life. We should not put a modern diagnosis on Moses. But his words show a man who had become overwhelmed, and who could no longer carry his responsibility alone. The burden had become unbearable, not because Moses lacked faith, but because one person was trying to carry what needed to be shared.

These are two separate events. In Exodus 18, Jethro advised Moses to share responsibility with capable leaders. Later, in Numbers 11, God himself instructed Moses to gather seventy elders, who would help carry the burden of the people. God said:

"and they shall bear the burden of the people with you, so that you may not bear it yourself alone."

8 · Turning towards God in distress

The Psalms give us many prayers like Psalm 42. Psalm 13 begins:

"How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?"

These are not the prayers of people who have necessarily abandoned faith. They are the prayers of people who continue turning towards God even when they feel abandoned, distressed or unable to sense his nearness. Scripture kept them so that we would know the experience is not unusual.

Many of these laments move towards renewed trust before anything outside them has changed. But not all of them. Psalm 88 ends in darkness, without visible relief. And it is still in the Bible. Scripture makes room for both experiences, and so will this retreat.

9 · The apostle who was crushed

And Paul. We do not know exactly what happened in Asia. Whatever it was, Paul did not hide it from the church. He wrote that he and his companions were so utterly burdened beyond their strength that they despaired of life itself.

The apostle who wrote about rejoicing in the Lord also knew what it was to be crushed. And he tells us what God did with it: it was to make them rely not on themselves, but on God who raises the dead.

10 · The short answer

So, what is actually happening to me? Here is the short answer. Burnout is what can happen when demands outrun recovery. Spiritual dryness is when God seems absent. Emotional overload is when there is too much to carry. And because you are a whole person, these can affect one another.

That gives us four points: burnout, when demands outrun recovery; dryness, when God seems absent; overload, too much to carry; and the whole person.

11 · Point 1: Burnout: Demands Outrun Recovery

The first point: burnout, when demands outrun recovery. Burnout is a state of deep exhaustion that can develop when demands continue for a long time and keep exceeding your resources, your support and your opportunity to recover. In occupational health, burnout refers to a pattern associated with chronic workplace stress. In this course, we are using the word more broadly, in a pastoral sense, to describe similar exhaustion experienced in ministry, caregiving, parenting and other demanding responsibilities. This is not a clinical diagnosis. It can feel as though you have been pouring out for much longer than you have been receiving rest, help, encouragement or care.

Some common signs: dreading things you used to find meaningful, including ministry and service. Feeling that you have nothing left to give, even to people you love. Growing cynicism or numbness towards the people you serve. Resentment that comes out as irritability, sarcasm or withdrawal. And going through the motions of prayer and church without any sense of connection. Physical signs, like poor sleep or frequent illness, can also appear, but new or lasting symptoms deserve a doctor’s attention, not a guess.

If this describes you, the retreat will matter, but so will what comes after it. Like Moses, you may need a structural answer, not only a rest. A person can rest for three days and walk straight back into the pattern that emptied them. Lessons 13 and 14 will help you look at that.

12 · Point 2: Dryness: When God Seems Absent

The second point: dryness, when God seems absent. Spiritual dryness is different. You can be well rested, not overworked, and still find that God feels distant. Prayer feels like effort with no response. Scripture that once spoke now reads like a document. Worship leaves you unmoved. You may still believe everything you believed before, but belief has become a fact rather than a relationship.

It is important to say what dryness is not. It is not always the result of sin. Christians sometimes assume that if God feels far away, they must have done something to cause it, and they search their lives for the offence. Sometimes there is one. Psalm 32 describes how unconfessed sin drained the psalmist’s strength, and confession restored him. But often there is nothing to find. Job’s suffering was not caused by the hidden wrongdoing his friends accused him of, yet he still cried, "Oh, that I knew where I might find him."

If this is you, the retreat cannot force God to feel close again. No one can promise that, and you should be wary of anyone who does. What it can give you is unhurried space to be honest about the distance, to find yourself in good company with the psalmists, and to keep turning towards God whether or not the feeling returns.

13 · Point 3: Overload: Too Much to Carry

The third point: overload, when there is too much to carry. This is another pastoral description, not a clinical one. It is for the experience of feeling that everything you have stored up has gone beyond your present ability to deal with it. Grief that was postponed because there was no time. Disappointment that was never spoken. Anger that was buried because it seemed unspiritual. Fear that has been running in the background for months.

Overloaded people often look fine. They are often the strong ones. But the weight shows up in unexpected tears, in reactions out of proportion to small things, in difficulty concentrating, or in a flatness that has replaced both joy and sorrow. When Jesus saw the crowds, Matthew says:

"When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd."

And Scripture invites us to put down what we carry. Psalm 55 says:

"Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you; he will never permit the righteous to be moved."

And Peter repeats it:

"casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you."

This does not always mean that the responsibility or the feeling disappears at once. It means we no longer have to carry it as though we were alone. The middle sessions of the retreat will give you gentle room for lament, confession, release and forgiveness, and you will not be pressured to resolve everything in three days.

14 · Point 4: The Whole Person

The fourth point: the whole person. You are a whole person, and the spiritual, emotional, relational and physical parts of your life affect one another. A medical condition can make prayer difficult. Grief can disturb sleep. Overwork can make God feel distant. Unconfessed sin can trouble the conscience, but not every troubling symptom is evidence of sin. Wise care asks about the whole person, rather than forcing every experience into one explanation, whether that explanation is purely medical or purely spiritual.

These three experiences can also feed one another. Burnout may leave little energy for prayer. Dryness may make grief feel lonelier. Pain that has not been dealt with may deepen exhaustion. If you recognise yourself in all three, that does not mean you are beyond help. It means your retreat needs to be gentle and unhurried. That is one reason a longer retreat is recommended where it is possible, and Lesson 6 will help you choose.

15 · When to ask for more help

There is a point at which these pastoral descriptions are no longer enough. You do not need to wait until things become severe before you ask for help. What you are experiencing may not be burnout or dryness, or not only that, and a retreat is not a substitute for that care.

Below this lesson there is a short note. It says when to speak with a doctor or a qualified counsellor, and what to do straight away if you have thoughts of not wanting to be alive. Please read it. Asking for help is not a lack of faith, and Lesson 5 will say more about that.

16 · Your step: a personal check-in

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too.

Below the lesson, and on the Lesson 4 worksheet, are the three lists of signs. Go through them, and mark every line that has described you in the past month. Do it honestly. Nobody else will see it. This is not a scored test, and it is not a diagnosis. It simply helps you notice what deserves attention.

Then answer four questions. First: looking at the signs you marked, which experiences seem most relevant to you right now? You may recognise more than one. Second: how long has this been going on? Third: can you connect it to a specific event, such as a loss, a move, a conflict or a change of role, or did it build gradually? And fourth: did the note about asking for more help describe you? If it did, write the name of the person or service you will contact, and contact them.

Keep your answers. Session 1 of the retreat will ask you to bring them.

17 · Naming it before God

Naming what is happening is not the same as solving it. But it is the beginning of honesty. And when you come to the retreat, you will not have to explain to God what you have not yet admitted to yourself. He already knows. The psalmists, Moses and Paul all found that he could be spoken to, honestly, from exactly where they were.

18 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Burnout is when demands outrun recovery, and like Moses, you may need a shared load, not only rest. Dryness is when God seems absent; it is not always caused by sin, and the psalmists kept turning towards God. Overload is when there is too much to carry, and you do not have to carry it as though you were alone. And you are a whole person, who needs gentle, unhurried care, and help when you need it.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord God, you know what is happening in me better than I do. Where I am worn out, show me what needs to be shared. Where you seem far away, keep me turning towards you. Where I am carrying too much, teach me to cast it on you, because you care for me. Give me courage to ask for help when I need it. In Jesus’ name. Amen.

When you are ready, do your personal check-in, keep your answers, and then continue with Lesson 5.

When to ask for more help

If low mood, emptiness or loss of interest has been present most days for two weeks or more, or is affecting your work, sleep, eating or relationships, please speak with a doctor or a qualified mental-health professional. You do not need to wait until it becomes severe.

If you have thoughts of not wanting to be alive, tell a trusted person and contact a qualified mental-health professional or crisis service promptly.

If you believe you may act on those thoughts, have formed a plan, or cannot keep yourself safe, do not continue the retreat alone. Contact emergency services or a crisis line where you are now, and ask a trusted person to stay with you. A retreat can wait. Your immediate safety cannot.
