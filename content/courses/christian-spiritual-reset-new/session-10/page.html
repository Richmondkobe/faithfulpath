2 · The cloak

Our passages are 1 Kings 19, verses 19 to 21, followed by Luke 10, verses 17 to 20. In the first, notice what Elijah’s return from isolation looks like. In the second, notice what Jesus tells the returning disciples to rejoice in.

"So he departed from there and found Elisha the son of Shaphat, who was plowing with twelve yoke of oxen in front of him, and he was with the twelfth. 4 · Return = šûḇ

Our Hebrew word is the one translated "return": šûḇ. It means to turn back, to return. At Horeb, God told Elijah, "Go, return on your way." Elsewhere in the Old Testament the same verb can carry the full meaning of repentance, of turning back to God. Here it means something simpler: to go back the way he came. It is the anchor phrase for this session.

5 · Go, Return on Your Way

Lesson 2 said that the difference between running away and retreating is that a retreat intends to return. This is the returning. It matters as much as the leaving. Re-entry is where the insights of a retreat meet the pressures of ordinary life, and without support or structural change, old patterns can quickly reassert themselves.

So this session is not about resolutions. It is about rhythm. Across the Gospels, Jesus’ ministry includes both engagement and withdrawal. When the disciples returned from their mission, he said:

"Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while."

Then the crowds found them, the work resumed, and later he withdrew again. This is a pattern of life, not a timetable everyone must reproduce. What you take home is not a peak experience to remember. It is a small, repeatable pattern that lets some of what happened here happen again, in ordinary weeks.

6 · Not Alone

God’s response to Elijah’s isolation had included food, rest, honest encounter, and the knowledge that seven thousand remained. Now it includes Elisha, called to follow him: a successor who first assisted Elijah in the work. Not every difficulty disappeared. But Elijah returns to active life knowing he is not the only faithful person left, and isolation begins to give way to shared vocation.

Your return may likewise require appropriate companionship: a friend, a spouse, a pastor, a counsellor, a colleague, a recovery community, or professional support. The retreat’s insistence on a support person was never only about the three days.

7 · Expect a Dip

Two things about re-entry. First, expect a dip. Some people notice a dip after returning home. They may feel flat, irritable, or discouraged. This does not necessarily mean the retreat was unhelpful. Ordinary demands have returned, and it may take time to adjust. Do not judge the whole value of the retreat by your mood in the first week. But take severe distress, worsening depression, inability to function, or safety concerns seriously, and seek appropriate help.

8 · Share selectively and safely

Second, share selectively and safely. There is a temptation, on the way home, to tell everyone what changed. Some of it will not survive contact with ordinary life, and some of it, as Session 7 said, still needs testing. Tell your support person what would help them support you. If you have a safe spouse or partner, explain any practical rhythm that may affect shared life, and listen to their concerns. Keep private material private, but bring significant impressions or decisions to appropriately qualified counsel, rather than treating secrecy as spirituality. Elijah’s next step was not public announcement, but embodied obedience and shared work.

9 · Rejoice That Your Names Are Written

Then there is Luke 10. The disciples return rejoicing: even the demons submit to them. Jesus does not dismiss it, but he redirects it: rejoice that your names are written in heaven. For those who belong to Christ, the deepest reason for rejoicing is not the quality of a retreat experience, but God’s saving grace and the belonging Christ gives. That belonging is not earned by a successful retreat or a productive ministry. Service grows from grace; it does not purchase it.

If the three days were dry, that belonging is not diminished. If they were rich, that is not what to build on.

So here is one question to carry into the practice. What are you afraid will happen when you get home? Name it honestly. And then, of everything that happened here, what one thing do you most want to be true in a month’s time?

1Opening the envelope Three-hour reset

Now open the envelope from Session 1. Read the cards. These are the ordinary demands you set down on the first morning. Some will feel exactly as heavy as they did; some, you may notice, feel lighter, or have resolved themselves, or turned out to be smaller than they seemed. Some may have become clearer.

Sort them, using the Session 3 marks:

  • A for something within your responsibility that needs a next faithful action
  • S for something to share
  • E for something to entrust
  • ? for something needing counsel

Do not assume every card must return to you as an action. Some may need delegation, counsel, changed expectations, or no action at all. Anything that no longer needs to be done, set aside.

If a card concerns immediate safety, medical care, suicidal thoughts, abuse, safeguarding, or a genuine crisis, do not place it into the ordinary thirty-day plan. Seek the appropriate help promptly.

You are picking these up again. But you are picking them up after three days of practising how to carry things, and you may find that you hold them differently.

2Self-assessment

In your journal.

  1. Reread the three questions you answered in Lesson 1, and your answers. Then reread your intention from Lesson 7, all four parts. Without forcing it: what has shifted, what has become clearer, and what may still need attention, support, prayer, or discernment after you return? Write honestly, including “nothing yet” if that is true. This is not a test of whether the retreat worked.
  2. Lay out the practical and discernment cards together. What do you notice? Is there a theme, a person who appears more than once, a concern that keeps returning? A repeated theme is worth noticing, but repetition alone does not prove that it is God’s central message. It may indicate anxiety, an intrusive concern, an unmet need, or a genuine priority requiring further discernment.
  3. Was there a moment, exercise, rest period, conversation, or passage that seems especially important? If nothing stands out yet, write that.
  4. What are you afraid will happen when you get home?

Suggested length: ten minutes, or the length you can manage.

This is the last silence of the retreat. Spend it not looking back and not planning ahead. Simply be here, one more time, with the God who was here the whole time. The retreat setting will remain behind, but the practice of returning attention to God can travel with you. It may look smaller and less quiet at home, and it can still be real.

5Journal

  1. What did you notice in the last silence?
  2. Of everything that happened, what one thing do you most want to be true in a month’s time? Write it as a sentence beginning “In a month, I hope…”
  3. Who will you tell about the retreat, and what will you tell them? Who will you not tell yet, and why?
  4. What is the next deliberate pause or form of support you need? It might be a three-hour reset, a day retreat, a counselling appointment, a Sabbath practice, or a protected hour. Write it down. A rhythm needs a next time.

6Four things to take home Three-hour reset

Older Christian traditions sometimes use the phrase “rule of life” for a realistic pattern of prayer, rest, work, worship, and relationship. “Rule” here means a supportive framework, not a system for earning God’s approval. Lessons 12 to 16 will help you build one. Today you take home four things, each on one card.

One thing to entrust.From the Entrust cards, or the envelope, choose the single matter you most need to stop trying to control. Write it on a card with the words “placed in God’s care.”

One action to take.From the Act cards, the purpose card, or the envelope, choose one next faithful step, doable in the first two weeks home. Small, specific, within your control.

One person or source of support.Name the person, or the service, you will contact in the first week. Your support person, your Eli, a counsellor, a doctor, a recovery community. Write when you will contact them.

One sustainable rhythm.Choose one piece of a rule of life and commit to it for thirty days. Choose something small enough for a difficult ordinary week; in a crisis, illness, or emergency, the rhythm may need to pause without guilt. Choose a rhythm compatible with your health, household, work, caregiving responsibilities, and safety.

Options for the rhythm, all adaptable:

  • A daily quiet. Up to ten minutes, at a roughly fixed time, with a psalm and a short silence. The Lesson 8 practice, continued.
  • A weekly hour. One protected hour each week, phone away, alone with God; shorter blocks count if an hour is impossible.
  • A digital boundary. One element of Lesson 9 kept permanently, with emergency access preserved.
  • A protected period of ceasing. A day, or half a day, each week without your ordinary work, adapted for caregivers, shift workers, and medical needs. Lesson 14 gives Sabbath the fuller treatment it deserves; for these thirty days, simply protect the time.
  • A conversation. A regular meeting with a trustworthy and competent person in which you say honestly how you are.
  • A rest that is yours. A named thing that restores you, protected weekly without guilt; recreation, not compulsive escape.

A rhythm is a practice to return to, not a perfect attendance record: “When I miss it, I will start again the next day without guilt.”

Write the rhythm on a card, with the day, the time, and the place, as concretely as you can. Write underneath what you will do when you miss it, because you will: “When I miss it, I will start again the next day without guilt.” Read the four cards to God.

Then sort every card from the retreat into two groups.

Action cardsThe four you have just written, plus any Act, Share, and Seek-counsel cards that are safe, practical reminders. Carry these in an accessible place; the 30-day plan begins with them on Day 1.

Private cardsAnything sensitive, identifying, traumatic, or spiritually intimate, including the sealed envelopes. Seal or securely store these. If your belongings may be searched or monitored, do not carry written material that could place you at risk.

If you deferred sessions, write on a card Optional sessions to revisit and include only those you genuinely want to reconsider. Deferral may be the final wise decision, not incomplete work. The 30-day plan has a place for them if you want it.

8A blessing Three-hour reset

These words were given to Israel’s priests as a blessing over the people, and they have been spoken over God’s people for millennia. Receive them now as Scripture’s ancient words of blessing, aloud if you can.

The Lord bless you and keep you;the Lord make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you;the Lord lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace.Numbers 6:24–26 · ESV

On your plan

Three-day retreat. Day 3, late afternoon or early evening, after a long break and something to eat. At least an unhurried hour. Choose its start by working back from checkout and safe travel; if there is not enough time to complete it without rushing, begin earlier or stay until the following morning. If possible, complete it before the final night and depart after sleep.

One-day retreat. About 16:05, after a twenty-minute break, shortened to about an hour: open the envelope, self-assessment question 1 and journal question 2, choose the four cards, and read the blessing.

Three-hour reset. The last twenty-five minutes: open the envelope, sort the cards, choose one thing to entrust, one action, one person, and one rhythm; read the blessing. Do not rush the blessing.

At-home retreat. Core version: Day 8 (Day 9 if you moved Session 5). Extended version: Day 12. The return deserves its own morning.

Retreat for couples. Each of you does the session on your own. Then the third shared moment: sit together for up to an hour. Each person shares the rhythm they hope to establish and any practical action that will affect the marriage, because a rhythm your spouse does not know about will not survive. The other cards may be shared, but sharing them is optional. Discuss the practical implications of each other’s rhythm, and agree how you will protect each other’s. Then, together, read the blessing.

Pastors and leaders. When you choose your one rhythm, consider a weekly protected period of ceasing that the congregation knows about and a leadership team protects. Ministers who rest in secret do not rest. When you choose your one person, consider a supervisor or a peer group outside your church, not a member of it. Re-entry: as a strong rule, do not preach or publicly announce what you believe you heard for at least a month. Tell the person in leadership who knew you were going. Keep the rest until it has become real.

After this session

The retreat is over. Do not add anything to it. Have a simple meal and pack slowly. Do not rush your departure. Follow the location’s checkout requirements, but delay travel or ask for assistance if you are tired, emotionally overwhelmed, unwell, or unable to drive safely. Tell your support person your travel plan and confirm when you arrive. You do not need to explain the whole retreat immediately; tell them what they need to know to support your return, and save deeper reflection for when you are rested. Lesson 12 begins on your first morning home. Watch it that day if you can, and not before. You have done enough for now.

Do not go straight into a demanding task. Give yourself the half-hour, tell your support person you took the reset, and put the four cards where you will see them.
