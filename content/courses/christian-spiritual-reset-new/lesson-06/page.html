1 · How long, and where?

Welcome to Part 2. Part 1 asked you to be honest about your condition. Part 2 asks you to be practical about your plans. The two belong together.

Many people who truly want unhurried time with God never create it. Not because they lack desire, but because they never settle a few ordinary questions. When? Where? For how long? And what will I do with my phone? This lesson will help you decide the length, the place and the approximate dates. Lesson 9 will deal with the phone.

A retreat does not need to be elaborate. It needs to be protected. So this lesson asks: how long, and where?

2 · Begin where you are

In Lesson 5 you chose one of four paths: to go ahead on your own, to go ahead with support, to use an adapted format, or to postpone for now. This lesson turns that path into a plan: a length, a place, and a target month.

If you chose to postpone, you can still use this lesson. A plan for later is still a plan, and it may help the support you need come sooner.

3 · Come away and rest a while

Our first passage is one we met in Lesson 1. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Mark chapter 6, verses 30 to 32:

"The apostles returned to Jesus and told him all that they had done and taught. And he said to them, “Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while.” For many were coming and going, and they had no leisure even to eat. And they went away in the boat to a desolate place by themselves."

4 · In the field toward evening

And our second passage is a single verse from Genesis, about Isaac, the son of Abraham. Genesis chapter 24, verse 63:

"And Isaac went out to meditate in the field toward evening. And he lifted up his eyes and saw, and behold, there were camels coming."

5 · A boat and an empty shore

Now the background. Jesus had sent the twelve out two by two, to preach and to heal. When they came back and told him everything, they were so surrounded by people that they had no time even to eat. So Jesus said, "Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while."

Notice how simple his plan was. A boat, and a quiet shore. No special building, no long preparation. The word for "desolate place" is the same word we met in Lessons 1 and 3: a place with fewer people and fewer demands.

And notice what happened next. The crowds saw them leave, ran around the lake on foot, and arrived first. As we saw in Lesson 3, Jesus’ quiet was often interrupted. The place was ordinary, and the plan did not go perfectly. The interruption did not make Jesus’ invitation to rest mistaken. It reminds us that even a good plan may be interrupted.

6 · Ordinary places

Genesis tells us that Isaac went out into the field toward evening. The ESV says he went out to meditate, although the Hebrew expression is difficult to interpret and some translations say he went out to walk. We should not build a teaching about retreats on that uncertain word. What we can observe is the ordinary setting: a field, toward evening. Our main example remains Jesus, who deliberately made space away from the crowds.

Scripture is full of people who met God in ordinary places. You do not need a retreat centre, and you should not let the lack of one stop you. The most important elements of a retreat, unhurried time, honesty before God, Scripture and silence, are free. Everything else is helpful but optional.

7 · The short answer

So, how long, and where? Here is the short answer. Choose the length you can actually protect. Choose a place that helps you be quiet and safe. Test it with seven simple questions. And plan a soft landing before and after.

That gives us four points: choose the length you can protect; choose your place; seven questions for any place; and a soft landing.

8 · Point 1: Choose the Length You Can Protect

The first point: choose the length you can protect. This course is built around three days. If you can take them, take them. Three days is the recommended format for this course, not a spiritual requirement or a biblical measure of devotion. The sessions build gradually. Day 1 slows you down and helps you begin to release what you are carrying. Day 2 makes space for deeper honesty, through lament, confession and release. Day 3 turns towards listening, identity and returning.

You will not be expected to resolve every grief, confess every failure or complete forgiveness on a schedule. And there are walks, meals and quiet between the heavier sessions. Compressing all of this into a single day is possible, but it is like reading quickly a letter that deserved to be read slowly.

Three days does not have to mean Friday to Sunday. Some people find three weekdays easier, especially if weekends are the busiest time in their home or ministry. What helps most is three consecutive days, without ordinary work in between.

9 · Other lengths

If three consecutive days are out of reach, and for many people they will be, there are other ways.

A one-day retreat covers the essential sessions across about eight to ten hours, including meals, walks, rest and generous breaks. It should not feel like a ten-hour seminar. A three-hour reset is for someone who needs to stop now and cannot wait. Think of it as an immediate spiritual pause, not the full retreat, and not crisis care. And an at-home retreat spreads seven sessions, chosen from the full retreat, over eight days, one each day with a rest day. It is a selection of sessions, not all ten squeezed into eight days. It works well for parents of young children and for caregivers. It needs more discipline, because ordinary life keeps interrupting, but it is a real retreat.

Choose the length you can actually protect, not the one that sounds most impressive. A one-day retreat that happens is worth more than a three-day retreat that stays on your list for a year.

10 · Not automatically better

One note about going longer. Extended solitude is not automatically better. Three days gives substantial time for prayer, rest and reflection. If you are thinking of more than three days, especially with long silence, fasting or emotionally intense exercises, seek guidance from someone experienced in leading retreats, and remember what we said in Lesson 5. Begin modestly, and learn how you respond.

11 · Point 2: Choose Your Place

The second point: choose your place. The place does matter, because some places make it much harder to be quiet than others.

Your own home, alone, is free and familiar. But it is full of unfinished work: the laundry, the inbox, the neighbour who knocks. If you choose it, set up one room as your retreat space, close the doors on the rest, and follow the digital boundary in Lesson 9 carefully.

Your own home, with the household present, is the hardest option, and many parents and caregivers will have to use it. It works best in the at-home format, with sessions early in the morning or after the children are asleep. It works only if the other adults in the house understand what you are doing and agree to protect the time. You will be interrupted. Lesson 3 told you what to do about that.

A borrowed home is often the best option: a relative’s house while they are away, a friend’s cabin, a church member’s guest room. It is free or cheap, quiet, and free of your own unfinished business. Ask. People are usually glad to help.

12 · More places

A church building can be quiet and inexpensive for a day. Ask your pastor. If you are a pastor, ask a colleague at a different church, so that you are not where everyone can find you. Confirm access and whether you may be alone in the building, and let one responsible person know when you arrive and leave.

A retreat centre or guesthouse is built for silence, and if it is within your means, it is a very good choice for a first three-day retreat, because the place does much of the protecting for you. Some offer a spiritual director or retreat guide. If so, ask about their training, their approach, confidentiality and safeguarding before you arrange to meet.

A simple hotel can work if you are disciplined about the television and the phone. Choose a safe, legitimate place, and tell your support person where you are staying.

And outdoors, a park, a beach, a hillside or a garden, is excellent for parts of a retreat, especially the walking and silence. But it is rarely practical for all of it. Tell someone where you are going, avoid isolated places you do not know, and check the weather and your phone signal. Solitude does not require placing yourself beyond the reach of help.

13 · Point 3: Seven Questions for Any Place

The third point: seven questions for any place. Whichever place you choose, test it with these.

One: can I have protected time here? Two: does this place help me feel safe and settled? Avoid places that bring back distressing memories, or a sense of danger, especially if you will be alone. Three: can I sleep well here? Rest is part of the retreat, not a break from it. Elijah slept before God spoke. Four: can I eat simply here? You do not want to spend your retreat cooking, and Lesson 10 will say more about food.

Five: can I control my connectivity without losing access to help? Reliable reception, with your phone on do not disturb, may be safer than going somewhere with no signal at all. Six: can this place support my physical needs, such as access, medication, temperature and food? A retreat should not require you to ignore your body in order to care for your soul. And seven: can I afford it without stress?

If you answer no to any of these, that question needs a solution, or a different place.

14 · A word about cost

Cost deserves one more word. Some people feel that a proper retreat needs a proper retreat centre, and since they cannot afford one, they never go at all. That is exactly the situation this course was written to address.

A retreat that puts you in debt or causes tension at home has already undermined its purpose. Choose the most affordable place that is safe, practical and supportive enough. More expensive does not mean more spiritual, or more effective. The most important elements of a retreat are free.

15 · Point 4: A Soft Landing

The fourth point: a soft landing. First, if you can, reduce unnecessary demands for a day or two before the retreat. Many people will not have that option, and some will arrive exhausted. If that is you, let yourself sleep. Resting through part of the first day does not mean you have wasted it or failed to follow the plan.

Second, try not to schedule anything demanding for the day straight after. Going straight back into a heavy schedule can make it harder to carry forward what became clear. Lesson 12 will help you come home well. But the simplest help is a soft landing on the calendar.

16 · Your Retreat Plan

To help you put this together, the course offers seven Retreat Plans, as printable guides. Four of them describe how long and where: the three-day retreat, which is recommended; a one-day retreat; a three-hour reset; and an at-home retreat. The other three are guides for particular people: couples, groups and churches, and pastors and leaders. These can be combined with a format. A pastor, for example, might take the three-day retreat and use the pastors’ guide alongside it.

Below this lesson there is a short plan guide. It asks a few practical questions and suggests a format. The suggestion is only a suggestion, not an assignment; you choose, and nothing you answer is saved. You can change your plan at any time. And whichever plan you choose, the sessions in it are offered, not assigned. A session you leave for later is not a missing requirement.

17 · Your step

Here is your step for this lesson. You will find it below this lesson too, and on the Lesson 6 worksheet. Take a few minutes and write down four things.

First, the length: three days, one day, three hours, or the at-home retreat. If three days, which three? Second, the place: which option, and specifically where. If you need to ask someone, write down whom, and when you will ask. Third, go through the seven questions for your chosen place. Any question you answered no to needs a solution or a different place. And fourth, the dates: the retreat itself, and a softer day before and after if you can arrange it.

If you can, choose a format and a target month before you move on. If you cannot decide yet, write down what you still need to arrange, and come back to this step later. A tentative plan can be adjusted. An undefined intention is easy to postpone.

18 · Remember

Let us bring it together. Choose the length you can actually protect, not the one that sounds most impressive. Choose your place; ordinary places are enough, as they were for Jesus and his disciples. Test it with seven questions, so that you are safe, rested, fed and within reach of help. And plan a soft landing, with room before and after.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you said to your tired friends, "Come away and rest a while." Help me to hear that invitation for myself. Give me wisdom to choose a time and a place I can protect, and the humility to ask for help in making it possible. Go before me into the days I am setting aside for you. Amen.
