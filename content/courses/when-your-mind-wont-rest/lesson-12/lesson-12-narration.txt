Welcome to Lesson 12, the last lesson in Module 2. Here is today's question. How many people are you holding up in your mind right now? Most of them do not know they are being carried. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to tell caring from carrying, five questions to ask before you pick up someone else's burden, and how to sort the weight into carry, share, offer, refer or release. Two things first. Please do not stop or reduce childcare, medication support, safeguarding, essential care or professional duties because of this lesson; it is about the unnecessary weight, not the real responsibilities. And if someone is in danger, do not carry that alone. Contact appropriate professional or emergency help. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verses are Galatians 6, verses 2 and 5, in the English Standard Version. "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ." And three verses later: "For each will have to bear his own load." Paul says two things in four verses that sound contradictory. As a pastoral application: love does not require you to carry everyone. It requires you to help carry what is too heavy for others, to carry your own load faithfully, and to recognise that other adults have loads God has given to them and not to you.

Let me tell you about a woman I will call Lydia. She could not sleep, and when I asked what kept her awake she gave me a list. Her adult son's marriage was in difficulty. Her mother, in her eighties, was becoming forgetful and refused to see a doctor. A friend from church had been made redundant and was not answering messages. A colleague going through a divorce had become short-tempered, and Lydia had been covering for her. Her husband had a hospital appointment next month that he was not worried about, but she was. "I feel as though I am holding all of them up," she said. "And if I put any of them down, something will happen." I asked her which of these people had actually asked for her involvement. Her mother's forgetfulness was a real concern, and as a daughter she had a real part, though not the whole. Her husband's appointment was his. The rest she had picked up, one by one, because she loved these people, and it had not occurred to her that there was any other way to love them.

Lydia's pattern has many names: people-pleasing, rescuing, over-functioning. What it has in common everywhere is a confusion between caring about someone and carrying them. Caring notices, feels, prays, and offers what is appropriate: presence, help, a word, a referral. Carrying treats another person's life, feelings or decisions as yours to manage. It plans, worries and anticipates on their behalf, and quietly assumes their agency. It is hard to see because it looks like love, and it often begins as love. It feels necessary. It can be a way of managing your own anxiety. And it can shade into control. Most carriers are among the kindest people in any church. But kindness that has become carrying is not serving the people it carries, and it is slowly consuming the person doing it.

Whether a burden is yours depends on role, need, capacity, consent, safety and consequence together. Five questions make the answer honest. One: did they ask? A request matters, though children, vulnerable adults and people in danger may need action without asking. Two: do I have an established responsibility, as a parent, spouse, carer, employee or leader? Three: is there a serious need they cannot presently address alone? Four: am I the appropriate person, or does this belong to a doctor, a counsellor, a social worker or a service? Five: is the proposed help wanted, safe and sustainable? Lydia's mother met several of these. Her son's marriage met almost none.

With those questions in hand, the weight can be sorted into five kinds. Carry: a responsibility that properly belongs to my role; the question is whether you are carrying only your load, and whether it is supported. Share: too heavy for one, and I can appropriately help in a specific, bounded way; you carry alongside, not instead. Offer: I may offer support, but the other person keeps the choice. Refer: the need requires someone more qualified. Lydia's mother needed a doctor, not a daughter's worry. Release: I have no appropriate action, or my part is complete. Your adult son's marriage is his and his wife's. Release, applied to people, does not mean abandonment. It means entrusting the person to God and to their own responsibility.

"If I stop worrying about them, it means I do not care." Love does feel the weight of other people's difficulties, and indifference is not a Christian option. But worry is not a form of love, and the amount of carrying does not measure the amount of care. Gently: if I need to carry people in order to feel that I love them, or that I am useful, then the carrying is doing something for me as well as for them, and putting it down will feel like a loss even when it is a relief. The people you love are loved by God more than by you. God's care is not limited by your ability to anticipate, control or prevent every outcome.

So the practice is this. Notice: I am carrying. Ask the five questions of that person honestly. Sort the weight, and give each part its response. What is yours to release, release deliberately: say the name, say what you have been carrying, say that it is theirs and God's, and then do not pick it back up when the worry returns. Release is not a feeling that arrives. It is a direction you keep choosing. And about "if I do not, no one will": sometimes it is true, and then you may need help so you are not carrying alone. But do not withdraw abruptly from a real responsibility to test it. Reduce only what is unnecessary, unrequested, or beyond your role: the anxious message, the unasked advice, the mental planning of a capable adult's decisions.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Write the name of one person you have been carrying, and one anxious action you will pause today: the reassurance-seeking message, the unrequested advice, the mental planning of a capable adult's decision. Not a real duty, only the anxious extra. Then pray for them once, by name, and turn to something that genuinely belongs to you. Prayer for someone is not the same as carrying them, though the carrier often confuses the two. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. The people you love are loved by God more than by you. Carry your own load. Help with what is too heavy. And entrust the rest to him. That is the end of Module 2. You have now named the seven patterns: predicting disaster, replaying the past, rehearsing conversations, reading minds, seeking confirmation, perfecting, and carrying.
