Welcome to Lesson 20. Here is today's question. What happens to your tools when life gets too busy to use them? A good tool used only in crisis is still a good tool, but a life with nothing supporting it will keep producing crises. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to build a small, gracious weekly rhythm under five simple headings, and why we call it a trellis rather than a law. One caution before we begin. If a rule like this one ever becomes rigid, if missing an item produces disproportionate distress, or you feel you must complete items in a particular way or order for the day to feel safe, stop using it and speak with a qualified professional who can assess what is happening. And medication, if you take it, is never changed because of anything in this lesson. The support link is under this lesson.

Our passage is Mark chapter 1, verse 35, in the English Standard Version. "And rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed." The night before, Jesus had healed late into a demanding Sabbath in Capernaum, with a whole town at the door. This is not a command to rise before dawn; it is a pattern, seen across that day: withdrawal, prayer, and return to the work he came to do. When Simon found him and said everyone was looking for him, Jesus answered, "Let us go on to the next towns," and kept moving. The rhythm supported the mission. It was not the mission itself.

Let me tell you about a man I will call Jonathan. He had learned nearly everything in this course, and his mind had felt like his own again. Then his small business went through its busiest quarter in years, and the old patterns crept back. He was on his phone at midnight. He had missed church three Sundays running. The walk he used to take had gone. When I asked which tools he had been using, he said, honestly, "They work. I just wait until I need them." Jonathan had not done anything wrong. He had a set of good tools and no life to put them in. What he needed was not another tool. It was a trellis.

That word, "rule," can sound heavy, so it is worth pausing on it. A rule of life is not a legal code to be obeyed or broken. It comes from a word meaning a guiding measure, and the picture I want you to hold is a trellis: a simple structure that helps something living grow toward the light. It does not make the plant grow. It gives what is already alive somewhere to climb. And you will not build a commitment in every area of life at once. Establish one small central practice, then add one or two supporting ones where they genuinely fit your week.

Here are the five areas the rule is arranged under. Centre: prayer and communion with God, the thing everything else is arranged around — not necessarily long or early, but regular, at a time, place or cue the rest of the week knows to protect. Formation: steady, unhurried Scripture reading, and safe, accessible Christian community in whatever form your circumstances permit. Embodiment: sleep, movement and rest, supporting the body rather than treating a disorder. Boundaries: work and digital life, a limit you write for your own week and can actually keep. And Support: the people and professionals you can actually call on, arranged before a crisis, not during one.

A few honest limits, so the rule does not overpromise. Habits like a regular bedtime can support sleep, but they do not guarantee it, and they are not a treatment for insomnia; if sleep problems are severe or persistent, that is a conversation for a healthcare professional. Choose movement that is medically appropriate for your own body; ask your doctor if you are unsure, especially after a long period of inactivity. And on church: the aim is not attendance at any cost. Some readers are housebound, unwell or recovering from a harmful church experience. Seek safe and accessible community in whatever form your circumstances permit, and do not return to an unsafe congregation merely to satisfy a weekly rule.

Here is a belief worth naming: "If I miss a day, the whole rule has failed, and so have I." A rule built to earn peace or measure your worth has stopped being a trellis and become one more source of anxious monitoring, exactly what this whole course has been helping you set down. A trellis that has started to strangle the plant is not doing its work. So the rule includes, from the start, a grace clause: what you will do, gently, when you miss a line. The rule serves you. You do not serve the rule, and missing a day is information, not a verdict.

Here is the practice: your own one-page rule. First, the honest week: write what your week actually contains under the five areas, not what you intend it to contain. Second, choose one line for each of a few areas, not all five: one small practice toward God, one for the body, one boundary, one form of support you could ask for. Third, write a grace clause, what you will do gently when you miss a line, and a review date about a month from now. Keep the whole thing to half a page. Ask yourself: could I actually keep this in a week when everything else went wrong?

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Write one honest sentence about the week you have actually been living, not the one you intend to live. Then choose the area that has slipped the most, Centre, Formation, Embodiment, Boundaries or Support, and write one small line for it: a time, a place or a cue you could actually keep. The full one-page rule worksheet, with all five areas and the grace clause, is under this lesson for when you have more time. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready.

One thing to remember. When you miss a practice, return without a reckoning. The trellis may change, or fall for a season, but God's faithfulness does not depend on your keeping it. Jonathan did not need a harder version of the tools he already had. He needed a small, ordinary rhythm that made the tools less necessary in the first place, and room to return to it gently when a busy season knocked it over. That is what this lesson has offered you: not one more thing to get right, but a place for everything else in this course to live.

Save me from using it to earn peace, measure my worth, or punish my own inconsistency. When I miss a practice, let me return without a reckoning. The trellis may change or temporarily fall.
