Welcome to Module 3, and Lesson 13. In Module 2 we named the patterns. In this module we learn what to do with a thought once you have named it: how to test it, pray it, and decide. Here is today's question. Have you ever tried to fight every thought, and found there were more of them? The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: what "take every thought captive" does and does not mean, and a six-movement practice for a recurring thought that makes a claim you can examine. One thing I must say first. Frequent, distressing intrusive thoughts, thoughts tied to a frightening event you lived through, or distressing thoughts about harm coming to a baby, all deserve assessment by a qualified professional rather than more fighting. And a thought that comes with an urge or intention to act, or with confusion, hallucinations or loss of contact with reality, needs urgent medical help today. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is 2 Corinthians 10, verse 5, in the English Standard Version. "We destroy arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, and take every thought captive to obey Christ." Paul is defending his ministry, and what he is fighting is named: arguments, and lofty opinions raised against the knowledge of God. The picture is a siege, where false reasoning is torn down and led away as a prisoner. Let me be honest. In its original setting, this verse is not a technique for managing anxious or intrusive thoughts. But it would be wrong to say it teaches nothing about our thought lives. As an application, marked as one: it encourages us to bring our reasoning under Christ's authority. It does not teach that every unwanted mental event is an argument, a demon, a sin, or a personal stronghold.

Let me tell you about a young man I will call Benjamin. He came to see me because he was, in his words, losing a war. He had been taught at a conference that a Christian must take every thought captive, and he had set out to do exactly that: every anxious thought, every doubt, every unwanted image, every stray unkind judgement, caught, rebuked and refused. For a few days he felt strong. Then the thoughts came faster, and the harder he fought one, the more often it returned. By the time we spoke he was spending most of each day monitoring his own mind, and dreading the quiet before sleep. "I am doing what the Bible says," he told me, "and it is making me worse." He had never read the verse in its chapter. Almost no one has.

Because the verse is so often misused, it is worth saying what it does not say. It does not say every thought is an enemy; most thoughts are not arguments against the knowledge of God. It does not say the arrival of a thought is a sin. An unwanted thought may arrive uninvited; Scripture treats what we cherish and intend as morally significant, not what merely appeared. It does not say thoughts can be eliminated by force, or that a thought's return is a failure. Deliberately trying to push a thought out of awareness can make it more persistent, which was exactly Benjamin's experience. And it does not say every thought comes from the devil. Treating every anxious thought as an attack tends to produce the exhausting vigilance Benjamin was living in.

"If I were spiritual enough, I would not have these thoughts at all." Benjamin believed this, and it was doing more damage than any of the thoughts. Scripture does not present maturity as mental silence. The Psalms voice fear, grief and confusion, and Jesus was deeply distressed in Gethsemane while remaining perfectly obedient. The presence of a thought you did not choose is not a measure of your spiritual state. What Scripture describes is people who bring their thoughts to God, answer falsehood with truth, and choose what to attend to and obey. That is the maturity on offer: not a quiet mind, which is not promised, but a faithful response to a noisy one. And there is a further danger. If unwanted thoughts are evidence of failure, you will monitor your mind for evidence, and monitoring tends to produce more of what it monitors.

So here is the practice, in six movements, for a recurring thought that makes a claim you can examine. One qualification first: some thoughts are images, memories, sensations or mental noise, and may not benefit from being analysed. Use this only where a claim can responsibly be examined. Notice: see it as a thought rather than as reality. "I am going to fail" feels like a fact; noticing turns it back into a sentence the mind has produced. Name: say what kind it is, a prediction, a replay, a rehearsal, mind-reading, a demand for certainty. Test: is the claim true? Is it the whole truth? Is it consistent with what God has said? What evidence supports it? Refuse: decline to organise your life around a claim shown to be false or unsupported. This is not eradicating the thought. The thought may remain; it has been refused its authority. Reorient: turn your attention towards relevant truth, present responsibility, or an ordinary activity. And act: do the next faithful thing. Very often a thought loses its grip not when it is argued with, but when the body moves.

Here is the main truth of this lesson. Refuse the thought its authority. You cannot reliably refuse its existence, and trying to may make it stronger. "The thought says I will certainly fail. I do not know that, and I will not plan my day around it." The thought may still be there while you make breakfast. That is not failure. It is a thought that has been demoted.

Two cautions. This is not a checklist to run on every mental event; most thoughts do not need it. And a thought may return many times. You do not need to perform the six movements each time. After examining the claim once, briefly acknowledge its return and redirect your attention, without creating another ritual. If you notice, as Benjamin did, that you have begun to monitor your mind constantly, that is the signal to step back rather than press on. Put the practice away for a day or two and do something ordinary. Attention that is locked inward needs somewhere else to go. That is part of the practice.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Take one recurring thought that makes a claim. Write the claim as a sentence, and name which pattern it is. Then write your refusal in one sentence: "I do not know that, and I will not organise today around it." Once. Then, after this lesson, do something for half an hour that needs your attention outside your own mind: a conversation, a task with your hands, a walk with something to look at. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. A noisy mind is not a lost war. A faithful response is the victory on offer. Bring your reasoning under Christ. Refuse false claims their authority. And then get up and do the next faithful thing.

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, I bring my thoughts to you, the ones I chose and the ones I did not. Free me from the war I was never asked to fight. Teach me to answer falsehood with truth, to refuse what is not true its authority, and then to get up and move. My mind is noisy; my heart is yours. In Jesus' name. Amen. ...
