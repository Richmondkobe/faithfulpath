Welcome to Lesson 14. Here is today's question. Have you ever said all the right things, and felt like a liar? "It will be fine." "God will come through." "I refuse to think about failure." And underneath, more frightened than before. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to tell forced optimism and unsupported reassurance from biblical renewal, and how to replace one reassurance you cannot support with a sentence grounded in truth. One thing first. Persistent low mood, hopelessness, or difficulty functioning deserves assessment by a doctor rather than another attempt at thinking well. And hopelessness that includes thoughts of suicide or self-harm needs urgent help today; the "Get help now" link is under this lesson.

Our verse is Romans 12, verse 2, in the English Standard Version. "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God." Three things matter here. "Be transformed" is passive: renewal is God's work, in which we actively take part. The renewal is aimed at discernment: a renewed mind is one that can see clearly and judge rightly, not one trained to see only what is pleasant. And Paul's vision is larger than private self-talk. The renewed mind appears in sincere love, patience in tribulation, and weeping with those who weep. It is not a mind that has stopped weeping. It is one that weeps truthfully and hopes truthfully at the same time.

Let me tell you about a woman I will call Naomi. Her husband's business had begun to fail. The accountant had said plainly that without new work the company would not survive the year. But when she came to see me, she was distressed about her faith. A friend at church had told her she must not speak negatively over the situation, that fear was a lack of faith, and that if she declared God's provision and refused every thought of failure, the breakthrough would come. Naomi had tried. She refused to look at the accounts. She stopped her husband from discussing what they would do if the company closed. And underneath the declarations she was more frightened than she had ever been, because now she was frightened of the failure, and frightened that her fear was causing it. "I am saying all the right things," she told me, "and I feel like a liar. And I cannot plan anything, because planning would be doubting."

Four things are sometimes mistaken for biblical renewal. Denial refuses to look at what is true because it is painful, and Scripture never commends it: Jesus wept at a grave he was about to empty. False reassurance says something comforting that may not be true. A frightened mind usually knows the reassurance is unsupported, so the sentence has to be repeated, louder, to hold the fear down, which is where Naomi had arrived. Forced optimism treats any negative expectation as a failure of faith, and so cannot plan for a possibility it cannot name. Suppression pushes the unwanted thought down and lays a hopeful sentence over it. Each of these makes peace depend on the situation turning out well. Biblical renewal makes peace depend on God, and is therefore free to be honest about the situation.

"Faith means refusing to say anything negative. If I acknowledge the problem, I am agreeing with it." Scripture does not teach that acknowledging an unwelcome fact causes it. Naomi's fear did not lose the contracts. It confuses acknowledging a fact with endorsing it. To say "the company may close" is to describe a possibility accurately, so that it can be prayed about and prepared for. And the belief misplaces the weight of faith. The person who says "God will certainly save the business" has staked their faith on something God has not said. The person who says "I do not know what will happen, and God is faithful" has staked it where it belongs, and will still be standing whichever way the year goes.

So what does renewal sound like? A reassurance is a sentence that makes you feel better and may not be true. A biblical truth is a sentence that is true whether or not you feel better, and it is more durable because it does not have to be defended. "It will be fine" is a prediction you do not know. The truth is: "I do not know how this will turn out. God knows, and he is with me in it." "I must not think about failure" is suppression. The truth is: "The company may close. If it does, we will grieve, we will need a plan, and God will still be God." Notice what the true sentences share. They are honest about uncertainty and loss. They locate hope in God's character. And they leave room for grief, planning, and prayer that says what is actually the case.

A caution about promises. Not every promise in Scripture was made to every reader in every circumstance, and treating one made to someone else as a guarantee for your business or your health can collapse your faith when it does not come true. Scripture's promises are differently shaped from our reassurances. Not every desired outcome, but God's presence, forgiveness, sustaining grace, resurrection, and life with Christ. Those hold whichever way the year goes.

The practice, then. Listen to the sentences you have been using to comfort yourself, and write them down exactly. Many people are surprised to find their comforts are mostly predictions they cannot support. Test each one with the questions from Lesson 13: is this true, is it the whole truth, has God actually said this? If a sentence fails, rewrite it as one that is true. Then do what the true sentence permits, because very often it opens a door the reassurance had shut. "The company may close" allowed Naomi to open the accounts, to let her husband plan, and to pray for provision without demanding a particular form of it. And be patient. Transformation is slow. Naomi opened the accounts, and the company closed in the autumn. She told me later that the closure was hard, but that the worst part of the year had been the months of declaring that it would not happen.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Write one reassurance you have been repeating to yourself, exactly as you say it. Under it, write the sentence that is actually true: honest about what you do not know, and honest about who God is. Then name one step the true sentence permits: a conversation, a look at the letter, a plan, an honest prayer. If the situation involves abuse, immediate danger, or a serious medical, legal or financial matter, that step is asking the right person for help, not facing it alone. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. A renewed mind weeps truthfully and hopes truthfully at the same time. Peace that depends on the outcome has to be defended. Peace that depends on God is free to be honest.

Let us pray. Father, I do not know how this will turn out. You know, and you are with me in it. Forgive me for the sentences I have used to hold my fear down, and give me true ones instead. Renew my mind, so that I can see clearly, plan honestly, grieve where I must, and still hope in you. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do.
