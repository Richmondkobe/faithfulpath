Welcome to Lesson 10. Here is today's question. Have you ever asked God for a clear answer, and then a clearer one? You prayed, you asked wise people, and you still could not move, because what you wanted was not wisdom. It was a guarantee. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to tell discernment from confirmation-seeking, how to weigh the ordinary means God has given, and how to answer honestly whether you need more information or more courage. One thing first. If decisions large and small keep becoming impossible, if you feel unable to act without certainty that a choice is God's will, or you cannot stop reviewing a decision after it is made, please consider speaking with a mental-health professional who understands obsessive symptoms and respects Christian faith. That is not weak faith, and the support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is James 1, verse 5, in the English Standard Version. "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him." This verse is a great comfort and, misread, a great burden. The comfort: God gives wisdom generously, to all who ask, without reproach. Notice what is promised. Wisdom. Not a specific sign, not an unmistakable answer. The burden comes when the next verses, about doubting and being double-minded, are read as condemning ordinary uncertainty. James's concern is a person divided in allegiance, not an anxious believer with an honest question. If you have read it that way, please set that reading down.

Let me tell you about a nurse I will call Rebecca. She was offered a post in another city: better hours, the specialism she had wanted for years, a good church nearby. She had three weeks to accept. She prayed. Her pastor, her small group and her parents all said it sounded like a good opportunity. She asked two friends who had made similar moves, then two more. A sermon mentioned Abraham leaving Ur, and she took it as a sign. On Tuesday a song about staying rooted, and she took that as a sign too. She asked God for a clear word, then a clearer one. On the day the offer expired she had not decided, and it lapsed. She told me she felt she had failed God by not hearing him. I asked what she had wanted to do. "To go," she said, without hesitation. She had wanted to go from the first day. Rebecca did not lack the ordinary information for a responsible choice. She lacked a certainty no adviser, sign or process could provide, and she had spent three weeks calling the search for it discernment.

Discernment is a real discipline, and this lesson is not against it. It uses the ordinary means: Scripture, wisdom, trusted people, circumstances, and what you actually want. And it is bounded: it gathers what can reasonably be gathered, and then it decides. Confirmation-seeking uses the same means, but cannot stop. It asks the same question of another person, and another, hoping for a more certain answer. It reads a coincidence as a sign and the opposite coincidence as a counter-sign. It is not seeking guidance. It is seeking the removal of risk, and guidance cannot provide that. It is hard to spot because it looks devout, feels responsible, and postpones the cost of choosing. While she was still discerning, Rebecca did not yet have to give anything up.

"God has one right choice for me, and if I miss it I will be outside his will." Christians hold different views of how God guides, and I will not settle that here. But whatever your view, three things can be said with confidence. God is not a cruel examiner who condemns sincere believers for failing to decode a hidden message. God's providence is larger than our decisions; Scripture is full of people who chose badly, or without a clear word, and whom he led, corrected and used. And many decisions are between morally permissible options, where Scripture leaves real freedom to choose wisely, even though the options carry different consequences. Rebecca was choosing between two permissible cities. And on signs: an impression or a sermon is weighed, not obeyed automatically. If you are collecting signs in both directions, the signs are not deciding the matter.

When your mind will not settle over a decision, there are four faithful responses. Act: you already have what you need; the moral question is clear or absent, counsel has been given, circumstances permit, you know what you want. Wait: there is a genuine reason, information you can obtain, a circumstance about to change, a person still to consult. Waiting is faithful when it has a reason and an end. Seek help: you need expertise you do not have, a doctor, a lawyer, a mature Christian who knows you. Surrender: the decision is not yours, or the outcome is beyond your control, or you have done all that can be done.

The tool is one honest question. What information is genuinely missing, and what courage will still be required after I have it? First, notice and name it: I am seeking confirmation. Then take stock of the ordinary means, and include your desire. What you want is relevant information, to be weighed alongside character, responsibility and consequences. It is not divine authorisation, but the overthinker often discounts it entirely, and that is its own error. Once a suitable person has given a careful answer, write it down and use it, instead of asking the next person the same question.

Then answer which fits. I need specific information: name what is missing, where it can be found, and by when, and go and get it. I have enough information and I need courage: you may have reached the point where further discernment is no longer adding anything relevant, and unless circumstances materially change, what remains is the courage to decide, which is itself an act of faith. Or I need both: get the information, and know that when you have it the courage will still be required, so you do not mistake the next piece of information for the last one. 

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Take one decision you have been circling, and write which you need: information, courage, or both. If it is information, name exactly what is missing and the date you will have it. If it is courage, write what you actually want to do. Just the sentence; you do not have to act on it today. If the decision is major or hard to reverse, review the facts with one appropriate adviser before you act, once. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. God promised wisdom, not a guarantee. Deciding is an act of faith. You can make a decision with God rather than waiting for him to make it for you, trusting that if it proves unwise, he will remain faithful, and asking for the humility to recognise it and respond well.

Let us pray. Father, I have asked for wisdom, and I trust that you are giving it. I have used the means you gave me. This is what I intend to do. If it proves unwise, you will remain faithful, and I ask for the humility to recognise it, to take responsibility for what follows, and to respond wisely. Free me from the search for a certainty you never promised. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
