Welcome to Lesson 3. Let me ask you a gentle question. Have you ever felt ashamed of being anxious? Not just afraid, but ashamed of the fear, as though a better Christian would not feel it? If so, this lesson is for you. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today. First, how to separate what you feel from what you choose. Second, how to hear the sentences that turn anxiety into shame, and test them against what God has actually said. One honest word before we start. In this lesson, "anxiety" means the ordinary experience of worry, fear and bodily alarm. If anxiety is persistent, or it is affecting your sleep, work or daily life, please also speak with a doctor or a mental-health professional. Faith and treatment are not rivals. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is Psalm 56, verse 3, in the English Standard Version. "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." The heading of this psalm connects it to the day David was seized by the Philistines in Gath. If that is the setting, his fear came from real danger, not imagination. Notice the order of the words. It does not say, "Because I trust you, I am never afraid." It says, "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." Fear comes first. Trust is what David does with it.

Let me tell you about a woman I will call Ruth. She waited until the end of a church meeting, when almost everyone had gone home, before she came to me. She had been a member for eleven years. She led a home group. "Pastor, I need to ask you something, and I need you not to think less of me. I have been anxious for months. I know all the verses. I have cast my cares on him, over and over, and they keep coming back. So either the verses don't work, or I don't have real faith. And I don't know which is worse." Ruth was carrying two burdens. The first was anxiety. The second was shame about the anxiety. And the shame was cutting her off from the very people who could have helped her carry the first.

So let us name the problem clearly. Anxiety is one burden. Shame about anxiety is a second burden, laid on top of the first. This lesson is not going to make you stop feeling anxious. It is about the second burden, because that one you may be able to put down.

Here is the main truth. Fear is an experience. Trust is a direction. In Psalm 56, David begins afraid, turns towards God, and arrives, for now, at a place where fear does not have the last word. Then, if the rest of the psalm is any guide, he does it again, because his enemies have not gone anywhere. This psalm was kept in Israel's songbook, not as a confession of weak faith, but as a picture of what faith does when it is frightened. Fear and trust can live in the same prayer at the same time. So the question is not only, "Do I feel afraid?" A better question is, "What am I doing with the fear I feel?"

Here is the tool. Four things that are not the same. One, a feeling: fear, dread, worry. It arrives; you did not summon it, and you usually cannot dismiss it by deciding to. Two, a body reaction: a racing heart, a tight chest, a night without sleep. Your body's alarm is not a moral act. Three, an unwanted thought: "God has left me," "Nothing I do will matter." A thought arriving is not the same as agreeing with it. Jesus was truly tempted, yet without sin. Four, a chosen response: what you do next. Do you seek truth, accept support, take one faithful step? This is where trust lives. Most shame comes from judging the first three as if they were the fourth.

Over the years I have heard the same sentences from anxious Christians. "If I trusted God, I would not feel this way." "I should be able to pray this away." "A real Christian should not be anxious." Each one sounds biblical. Some even contain words of Scripture. But look at what each one does. It takes a feeling, or a body reaction, and judges it as if it were a choice. When Ruth said, "Either the verses don't work or I don't have real faith," she was judging her faith by her pulse. But her faith did not live in her pulse. It lived in the fact that she was standing in front of her pastor, frightened and ashamed, and still asking for help. That is what trust looks like in the middle of fear. It looks like David in Gath.

But what about Paul's words, "Do not be anxious about anything"? Notice what he says next. He does not say, "So stop." He says, "But in everything, by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God." He assumes there are real needs, and he gives anxious people something to do with them. Instruction is not condemnation. The Bible also says, "Rejoice always," and nobody thinks a grieving Christian has sinned by feeling sorrow. These commands point us in a direction. They do not pass judgement on every feeling that has not yet followed. Can anxiety sometimes be connected to sin, to a refusal to trust, or something we are protecting? Yes. But that is something to discover honestly, in prayer and with wise counsel, not something to assume every time your heart races.

Now it is your turn, and it is small. Take your pen. Write one sentence you have told yourself about your faith because of anxiety. Write it exactly as it sounds in your head, however harsh. Then rewrite it truthfully. For example, "If I trusted God, I would not feel this way" becomes "I feel afraid, and I am choosing to trust God with it." Or, "I should be able to pray this away" becomes "Prayer is where I bring this, not a switch that removes it." Do it once. Do not go back to check whether you got it right; your standing with God does not depend on a sentence. Pause the audio now and write your sentence. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. Your faith does not live in your pulse. Your feelings do not, by themselves, define your faith, and your body's alarm does not prove that God has rejected you. Hear the shaming sentences as sentences again. Ask where they came from. And test them against what God has actually said: "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." "Cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you." "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."

Let us pray. Father, when I am afraid, I put my trust in you. You know my racing heart and my sleepless nights, and you do not turn away. Take the shame I have added to my fear. You do not condemn me; teach me not to condemn myself. Let me bring my anxiety to you as it is, and find that you care for me. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
