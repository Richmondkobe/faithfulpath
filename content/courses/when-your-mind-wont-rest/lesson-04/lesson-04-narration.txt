Welcome to Lesson 4. Here is today's question. Why does a thought keep coming back, even after you have looked at the facts and done what you reasonably can? Sometimes the answer is that the thought was never only about the thing on the surface. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end there is one small, optional writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: one question that helps you ask, gently, whether a repeated thought is connected to a deeper fear. I say gently on purpose. This is a possibility to explore, not a hidden law of the mind. Sometimes a budget is simply a budget.

Our verse is Psalm 139, verse 23, in the English Standard Version. "Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts!" This comes at the end of a psalm in which David marvels that God already knows him completely. So why ask to be searched? Because there is a difference between being known and knowing that you are known. God already sees what is underneath our thoughts. We often do not. This is a prayer overthinkers can learn by heart: not only "Lord, make the thoughts stop," but "Lord, help me see what these thoughts may be about."

Let me tell you about a man I will call Samuel. He was the church treasurer, careful and capable, and he had been thinking about the annual budget for three weeks. The numbers were not the problem. Giving was slightly down, the shortfall was manageable, and he had already found two sensible adjustments. Yet he could not stop. He recalculated figures he had already confirmed. He rehearsed his presentation to the elders again and again. So I asked him a question I have learnt to ask. "Suppose the elders looked at the numbers and were unhappy. What would that mean to you?" "That I'd let them down." "And if you had?" "That I'm not as capable as they think I am." "And if that were true?" A long pause. "That they wouldn't want me." Samuel had not only been thinking about a budget. He had been guarding a door, and behind it was a fear of being found inadequate and set aside.

Notice both halves of that. The spreadsheet was real, and it still needed Samuel's attention. But it had also become the place where a deeper fear had chosen to live. That is why solving the surface problem did not settle him. You can show Grace, from Lesson 1, that her manager was not upset, and tomorrow her mind will find a different colleague to worry about, because the fear of being found inadequate has not been touched.

Here is the main truth, stated carefully. When a thought keeps returning after the facts have been considered and reasonable action has been taken, the surface subject may not be the whole issue. Sometimes the mind is also responding to what the situation seems to mean about your worth, your safety, your future, or your trust in God. I want to say again what this is not. It is not a claim that every thought hides a childhood wound. Sometimes a loss simply needs to be grieved. Sometimes a dangerous situation needs action, not interpretation. And sometimes repeated thoughts need a professional's help. The purpose is only to help you notice when a surface concern has become connected to a deeper fear.

The tool is one question, asked downwards. "If this happened, what would it mean to me?" Take your answer and ask again: "And if that were true, what would it mean to me?" Keep going until the sentence is no longer about the situation and is about you: your worth, your safety, your future, or God. Three cautions. Ask it no more than three times. Stop earlier if a fear becomes clear, if nothing more comes, or if discomfort starts to rise. And "no deeper fear appeared" is a complete, legitimate answer. Do not go back later looking for a better one.

A small number of fears appear again and again in these conversations. I list them not so you can diagnose yourself, but so you can recognise what you find. Fear of rejection: if they really knew me, they would not want me. Fear of failure. Fear of losing money. Fear of disappointing people. Fear of choosing wrongly and having only yourself to blame. Fear of losing control: if I stop managing everything, something terrible will happen. Fear that God will not come through, which is often the hardest to admit. And fear of the past repeating: what happened before will happen again, and I will not survive it twice. You may recognise one, or several, or none. Do not force a choice if nothing is clear.

Finding the fear changed what Samuel's work was. He still finished the spreadsheet, made his adjustments and gave his presentation. What changed was that he stopped expecting the spreadsheet to solve a fear of rejection. For the fear, he began asking different questions. Is this fear telling the truth? Who has actually rejected me, and who has not? What does God say about it? "Will the elders be happy with the numbers?" cannot be settled in advance, so the mind circles it. "Am I secure in Christ whether or not the elders are happy?" can be answered, and Scripture answers it. That does not make rejection painless. It means another person's response is not the final verdict on your worth. When Samuel finally said the sentence out loud, "They wouldn't want me," he could see it did not match what he actually knew. His elders had shown him nothing but confidence for years. The fear had been enormous in the dark, and became something he could examine in the light.

Now, if it feels manageable, your turn. This practice is optional and private. Take one thought that keeps returning, and ask once: if this happened, what would it mean to me? Ask it of your answer, up to three times. Then write one sentence: "I am afraid that..." or, just as good, "No deeper fear appeared." Then come back to the present: say where you are, and one thing that is true right now. If at any point it becomes too much, if you feel panicked, or far away, or unable to return to the present, put the page aside, contact someone safe, and use the support link under this lesson. You do not need to uncover the deepest explanation today. Pause the audio now, if you choose to. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. A fear that has been named can be brought to God as a request, instead of hidden from him as a problem. Not only "Help me with the budget," but "Father, I am afraid of being set aside." He has already searched you and knows you, and he does not turn away from what he finds.

Let us pray. Search me, O God, and know my heart. Try me and know my anxious thoughts. You see the fear beneath my thoughts before I do. Give me honesty to see it, wisdom to know what it means, and gentleness with myself as I look. I bring it to you as it is, and I am not turned away. Lead me in the way everlasting. In Jesus' name.
