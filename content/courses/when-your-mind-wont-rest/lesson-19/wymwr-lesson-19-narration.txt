Welcome to Lesson 19, the first lesson of Module 4. Here is today's question. What are you waiting to know before you let yourself rest? Some of us have made peace conditional on an answer, and the answer keeps moving. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how Christ's peace is given before the answer arrives, not after it, and how to tell necessary watching from watching that only exhausts you. One thing first. Waiting for a serious result, a diagnosis, a verdict, news of someone you love, is one of the hardest things a person does, and heightened anxiety during such a wait is an ordinary response, not a spiritual failure. If the wait has disrupted your sleep, eating or ability to function for more than a couple of weeks, please tell your doctor or the clinical team; many hospitals support the families of patients too, and using that support is not weakness. The support link is under this lesson.

Our passage is John chapter 14, verse 27, in the English Standard Version. "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. Not as the world gives do I give to you. Let not your hearts be troubled, neither let them be afraid." It is the night before the crucifixion. Jesus has washed the disciples' feet, told them one will betray him and one will deny him, and said he is going where they cannot yet follow. Nothing about that night is resolved for the disciples. And it is into that room, on that night, that he gives them peace, before a single one of their questions has been answered.

Let me tell you about a man I will call Caleb. His wife had finished cancer treatment, and the scan that would show whether it worked was six weeks away. Caleb stopped sleeping well. He stopped coming to the midweek group, and said, with complete sincerity, that he would come back when they knew. "I can't rest until we know," he told me. "It would be like leaving my post." The scan came back encouraging but not conclusive, and another was booked for three months out. Caleb said, in the same voice, that he would breathe in three months. I asked what he would do if that scan was also inconclusive. He did not answer, because he could see where the question went.

Caleb's sentence was "I can't rest until we know," and most of us have a sentence like it: I will permit myself peace when I know that such-and-such. That structure feels responsible, but it has real problems. The answer keeps moving: one scan leads to another, and each answer reveals the next thing to be known. Some answers never come at all. The condition trains the mind to keep scanning, and the watching does not shorten the wait or improve the outcome; it only ensures exhaustion when the answer arrives. And deferred rest becomes a deferred life. If the scans stayed inconclusive for twenty years, Caleb would have spent twenty years at his post, guarding a life he was not living.

Because this lesson promises peace before answers, I need to say what kind of peace is meant, or it will be misheard. It is not the absence of concern; Jesus gave his peace to men he had just told to expect trouble. It is not a guaranteed feeling; some feel a settled calm, some feel steadiness beneath the fear, and some, in depression or exhaustion, feel nothing at all and must trust it is theirs on his word rather than their emotions. It is not certainty about outcomes; it does not tell you what the scan will show, but that neither you nor the one you love is beyond God's care, whatever it shows. And it is not resignation. Caleb kept praying for his wife's healing. Peace is what lets hope wait without tearing itself apart.

There are two kinds of watching, and this lesson only asks you to set down one of them. Necessary watching is the appointments, the questions, the practical support, and following the clinical team's guidance; that genuinely matters and remains yours to do. Anxious watching is the mental surveillance beyond that, the hours of scanning for danger that read no scan and change no outcome. Caleb's vigilance did not adjust his wife's treatment by a day. What it did was remove him from the meals, the conversations, and the ordinary tenderness that were the actual content of the months they had together. That watching was not care. Care was what it was preventing.

"Resting before it is resolved would be irresponsible. If I stop watching, it means I do not care." This belief has a moral force that makes it hard to argue with; who wants to be the person who slept while someone they love was in danger? But Scripture does not connect love with sleeplessness. The psalmist says God gives sleep to those he loves. Jesus slept in the stern of a boat during a storm that terrified experienced fishermen. Rest in the middle of an unresolved situation is not, in itself, negligence. The responsible thing in a wait you cannot shorten is to do what is actually yours, and then to live, because the people you love need you present more than they need you vigilant.

Here is the practice I worked through with Caleb. First, find the sentence: write it exactly, "I will permit myself peace when I know that ______," including the part that sounds unreasonable once it is on paper. Second, run the horizon test: if that answer came, and came well, what would be the next thing you needed to know? If you can see one, then the peace you are waiting for is not actually on the far side of any answer. Third, write two short lists: what I do not know, honestly, and what is already settled, both in your circumstances and in what Christ has given, which no outcome changes. The second list is where his peace is drawn from.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Write the sentence you have been living by: I will permit myself peace when I know that ______. Run the horizon test on it. Then write what you have suspended while you wait: sleep, church, friendship, an ordinary evening. Choose one to resume this week, not as a reward for a good result, but because it is yours to live now, whatever the answer turns out to be. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready.

One thing to remember. Christ's peace was given to the disciples before the answers, on the night when nothing was resolved, and he has not made it conditional on what you know. You may fear for what you love and still rest, because your watching does not change the outcome, and your presence is what the people you love actually need. Caleb went back to the midweek group the following Wednesday. He did not say he felt better. He said it was the first evening in two months he had not spent at his post, and that his wife had noticed.

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, on the night before the cross, with nothing resolved, you gave your peace to men who did not yet understand. I confess I have waited to understand before receiving it. I bring you what is unresolved, and I do not pretend it is settled. I go on asking for the outcome I long for. And I receive, before I know, the peace you already gave. Teach me to live the days I have while I wait for the ones I cannot see. In Jesus' name. Amen. You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
