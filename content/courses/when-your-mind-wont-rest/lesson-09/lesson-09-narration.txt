Welcome to Lesson 9. Here is today's question. How much of what you "know" about other people did they actually tell you? A short reply, a look across a room, three weeks of silence. We build whole accounts of what people think of us, and most of the material is ours. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to tell an observation from an interpretation, how to consider other explanations, and how to choose whether to ask, wait or let go, rather than acting on a conclusion the evidence does not support. One thing to say clearly at the start. This lesson is about stories built from too little evidence. Where there is repeated unkindness, broken promises, contempt or control, that is not a story; it is a pattern, and it is a reason to seek help, not to keep interpreting. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is 1 Corinthians 13, verse 7, in the English Standard Version. "Love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things." This needs careful handling. Just before it, Paul says love "rejoices with the truth," so "believes all things" cannot mean accepting every claim, ignoring evidence of wrongdoing, or staying in danger. What the phrase challenges is the reflex of suspicion. Love does not rush to convict where the evidence is incomplete. It does not ask you to invent a positive story. It asks you not to treat a painful negative story as established fact.

Let me tell you about a man I will call Mark. He had a close friend from his church home group, someone he had prayed with weekly for six years. One spring the friend's replies became shorter, then slower, then, for three weeks, stopped. Mark went back through their last conversations looking for the offence. He remembered the friend had grown close to another man in the group, and concluded he had been replaced. By the end of the third week he had assembled, out of silence, a complete account of what his friend thought of him. He stopped messaging. Two months later he learnt that his friend's father had been diagnosed with a serious illness that spring. The friend had spent the season driving between the hospital and his parents' home, and had told almost no one, because he could not face talking about it. He had noticed Mark's withdrawal, and assumed Mark had grown tired of him. Two men who loved each other spent a whole season apart, each certain he knew what the other was thinking, and each wrong.

Mark's habit is sometimes called mind-reading: assuming you know what another person thinks, feels or intends, without enough evidence, and responding to the assumption as fact. Three things make it hard to see. It leans towards the outcomes we most dread: judgement, rejection. It fills silence, which is the easiest material to build with. And it changes our behaviour. This is the costly part. Mark's assumption led him to withdraw, and his withdrawal became evidence to his friend of the very thing Mark feared. A mind-reading error, acted on, can produce the outcome it predicted. And it feels like insight. But being right sometimes is not the same as knowing.

Here is the main truth. There are four different kinds of "knowing," and the habit consists in forgetting the difference. Observation is what was said or done, with no motive attached: "He has not replied to my last four messages." The moment a word like "ignored," "snubbed" or "dismissive" enters the sentence, you have moved to the next level. Interpretation is what you think the behaviour may mean: "He is avoiding me." Not necessarily wrong, but not an observation. Pattern is what emerges when observations repeat. One short reply supports many interpretations; six months of short replies, missed commitments and coldness is a different kind of evidence, and charity does not ask you to keep explaining a real pattern away. Knowledge is what has been established through evidence, disclosure or reliable confirmation. Only God has complete access to a heart.

Beside those four sits one more category: other possible explanations, the accounts that would fit the same observations equally well. He is overwhelmed by something I do not know about. She took the nearest seat. The point of generating them is not to prove the kind one true. It is to show yourself that the unkind one is not the only one, and therefore that you do not know. Generate two or three, then stop. If this becomes a search for the perfectly reassuring explanation, come back to "I do not know." Mark had one observation, three weeks of silence, and never generated a single alternative. The true one, which had nothing to do with him, never occurred to him.

"I am not assuming. I am discerning. I can tell what people think of me." Perceptiveness is a real gift. But discernment and mind-reading feel identical from the inside and behave very differently. Discernment notices, and holds what it noticed loosely. It tests its impression, is willing to ask, and is willing to be wrong, because it wants truth rather than confirmation. Mind-reading notices, concludes, and closes. It does not test, rarely asks, and is reluctant to be wrong, because the conclusion has usually already been acted on. The test is simple: can I state the evidence, stay open to correction, and revise when new information appears?

Then choose one of three responses. Ask, where the relationship is safe and the matter is worth it: "I noticed you have been quiet lately. Is everything all right?" One compassionate message might have interrupted Mark's whole season. Treat the answer as new evidence, and do not keep asking the same question just to get more certainty or a moment's reassurance. Wait, when asking is not yet appropriate, behaving meanwhile with ordinary respect: not punishing the person for an unverified interpretation, and not ignoring real evidence either. Or let it go, when the honest conclusion is that you do not know, it does not much matter, and you have given a great deal of attention to a story with one observation in it.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Take one person you have been "reading" this week. On one line, write the observation: only what they said or did, with no motive in the sentence. On the next line, write your interpretation. Then write two other explanations that would fit the same observation. Then one word: Ask, Wait, or Let go. If it is Ask, and the relationship is safe, write the sentence you will use, once. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. Charity fills the gaps in the evidence. It is not a substitute for it. Where the evidence is silence, love declines to convict. Where the evidence is a real pattern of harm, love takes it seriously, sets boundaries, and seeks help. And in every case, only God reads hearts. The rest of us have to ask.

Let us pray. Father, you alone see the heart. Forgive me for the verdicts I have passed on silence, and the distance I have created from stories I wrote myself. Give me the humility to say "I do not know," and the courage to ask. Where there is real harm, give me wisdom and help. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
