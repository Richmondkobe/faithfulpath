# What Was Said and What Did I Imagine?

*Worksheet 8 in the printed toolkit · Lesson 8*

> **Before you begin.** If the person has been abusive, coercive or unsafe, do not use this worksheet to prepare direct contact. Seek guidance first from a specialist domestic-abuse service, a safeguarding professional or another appropriately qualified professional; the responsible decision may be not to have the conversation at all. Complete this worksheet once for one conversation, during the day; do not use it in the middle of the night, and do not repeatedly rewrite your prepared sentences to make them perfect. If the conversation materially changes you may update the relevant facts; the return of anxiety alone is not a reason to repeat the exercise.

Use this exercise for one rehearsed conversation at a time. Choose the one that has been running most often.

Step 1. The conversation in my head.

Briefly record the main version of the conversation as you have been rehearsing it: no more than ten lines, or five minutes. The purpose is to identify the pattern, not to reproduce every imagined exchange. If writing the dialogue increases distress or creates an urge to perfect it, stop and summarise it in one sentence instead.

Step 2. What was actually said.

Now, on a separate part of the page, write only what this person has actually said to you about this matter, and when. Memory is not a recording. If you are uncertain about the exact words, describe the substance and clearly mark what you do not remember. If you can recall nothing specific, write that. If it was a look, a tone or a silence rather than words, write that honestly too, and note that you are interpreting it.

Step 3. What I imagined.

Go back to Step 1 and mark every line you gave to the other person. For each, ask: did they say this, or did I write it for them? Write one sentence about what you notice. (Everything after her first remark is mine.)

Step 4. Is a real conversation needed?

Answer one of the following, and write one sentence explaining why.

Yes, and I will have it. The matter is real and affects the relationship. Note the time and method that would be appropriate.

Not yet. Something needs to settle first, I need to understand more, or the setting is not right.

No. The matter is small enough to let go, or it is not mine to raise.

I am not sure, and I should ask someone. Write who.

Step 5. My part, prepared once.

If a conversation is needed, write what belongs to you: how you will open, the one thing you most want the other person to understand, and any facts, questions or boundaries the situation genuinely requires. If it helps, note one or two realistic ways the other person might respond, marked clearly as possibilities. Do not write out their side, and do not write your reply to their reply. When this is written, your preparation is finished.

Step 6. How I will hear.

Write one sentence about how you will listen. (I will let her finish. I will ask one question before I answer.)

Step 7. Release the rest.

Write one sentence handing the other person’s part, and the outcome, to God.

Once is enough. Complete this exercise once per conversation. If the rehearsal returns, remind yourself of Step 5 and Step 6 and turn your attention elsewhere. Do not rewrite your sentences repeatedly in search of the perfect wording; a plain, sincere version is enough. Keep this page. You will use the distinction between what was said and what was imagined again in Lesson 9, and the Facts, Assumptions and Other Possible Explanations worksheet in the Course Journal and Practical Toolkit is built on it.

---

*Complete this once for one matter, then stop. If you find yourself redoing it to feel certain the answer is right, that is a signal to put it down, not to try again. This worksheet does not treat an anxiety disorder, depression, OCD or trauma; if a professional is supporting you, their guidance takes priority.*
