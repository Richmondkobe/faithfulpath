# Facts, Assumptions and Other Possible Explanations

*Worksheet 9 in the printed toolkit · Lesson 9*

> **Before you begin.** This worksheet is not designed to explain away repeated harm. Asking is optional and only appropriate in a safe relationship, and direct communication does not guarantee truthful disclosure. Complete it once for one person and one situation, during the day; do not use it in the middle of the night.

Use this exercise for one person and one situation at a time. Choose the one that has been occupying you most.

**Before you begin.** If the person concerned has a pattern of harmful behaviour towards you, this exercise is not asking you to explain that pattern away. Record what you have observed honestly in Step 1; a pattern of observations is itself evidence. Then take the situation to someone qualified rather than trying to generate kinder interpretations of harm.

Step 1. What I have observed.

Write only what was said, done or recorded, without assigning a motive. (He has not replied since the 12th. She left straight after the service.) Where your memory is uncertain, say so. If you find an interpreting word creeping in—ignored, snubbed, cold—cross it out and describe the behaviour instead.

Step 2. My interpretation.

Write, in one sentence, what you have concluded the person thinks or feels. Be honest about how certain you have been feeling.

Step 3. Single event or pattern?

Is what you observed a single event, or has it repeated over time? If it has repeated, briefly note how often and for how long. A single event supports many interpretations; a sustained pattern is stronger evidence and should be taken seriously.

Step 4. Other possible explanations.

Write two or three other plausible accounts that would fit the observations in Step 1 equally well, then stop. Include at least one that has nothing to do with you. You are not being asked to believe them, only to show yourself that they exist. If this step becomes a search for the perfectly reassuring explanation, return to “I do not know” and move on.

Step 5. What I actually know.

Write one sentence beginning “What I actually know is…” and complete it with the observations alone. Then write a second sentence: “What I do not know is…”

Step 6. Ask, wait or let go.

Choose one and write a sentence explaining why.

Ask clearly. Write the question you will ask, keeping it short and free of accusation, and note when you will ask it. Choose this only if the relationship is safe. Reasonable clarification or follow-up may be appropriate when an answer is incomplete or new information emerges.

Wait. Write how you will behave towards the person in the meantime: ordinary respect, neither punishing nor pretending.

Let go. Write what you are setting down.

Step 7. Release.

Write one sentence entrusting the other person’s heart, and your own, to the God who searches both.

Once is enough. Complete this exercise once per situation. If the interpretation returns, reread Step 5 and turn your attention elsewhere. If you chose Ask, do not run the exercise again merely to reduce uncertainty while waiting. Receive the person’s answer as evidence. Seek further clarification only when there is a genuine gap, a new fact or a practical reason to continue the conversation, rather than solely to obtain certainty. Keep this page. The Facts, Assumptions and Other Possible Explanations worksheet in the Course Journal and Practical Toolkit is a shorter version of this exercise for repeated use.

---

*Complete this once for one matter, then stop. If you find yourself redoing it to feel certain the answer is right, that is a signal to put it down, not to try again. This worksheet does not treat an anxiety disorder, depression, OCD or trauma; if a professional is supporting you, their guidance takes priority.*
