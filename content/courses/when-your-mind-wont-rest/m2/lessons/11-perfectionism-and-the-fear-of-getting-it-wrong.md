# Lesson 11: Perfectionism and the Fear of Getting It Wrong

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish a required standard from a perfectionistic one, define "finished" before beginning a suitable low-risk task, and practise stopping when that standard has been reached.

## Before you begin

If checking feels compulsive, produces severe distress, or is connected to diagnosed or suspected OCD, do not use this lesson to remove checks or resist compulsions on your own. A qualified mental-health professional can assess the pattern and, where appropriate, develop a gradual treatment plan with you. You may still read the teaching, but leave the practical experiment and worksheet unused unless the professional supporting you considers them suitable. Checking, redoing or repeating things far beyond what a task requires can occur with obsessive-compulsive symptoms; effective treatments are available, but this course cannot determine whether the checking is OCD or provide treatment for it. If concerns about food, weight, body image or exercise are controlling your eating, driving excessive exercise, substantially affecting daily life or causing concern about your physical health, speak with a doctor or an appropriate eating-disorder specialist. If perfectionism has led to exhaustion, hopelessness or a sense that you are worthless when you fail, raise that with a doctor. If feelings of failure leave you thinking that life is not worth living, open Finding Help Where You Live and contact appropriate support today; if you may act on thoughts of harming yourself, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact an emergency or crisis service now and involve a trusted person if you can. Opening the support page does not alert Faithful Path or contact help for you. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for one suitable low-risk task; do not use it in the middle of the night, and do not use it for safety-critical, regulated, medical, financial or legally significant work. Nothing here asks you to lower a standard that a task, a profession or someone's safety genuinely sets.

## Key Scripture: Ecclesiastes 11:4 (ESV)

> He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap.

The Preacher's farmer, for whom there is always a reason not to sow. The surrounding verses repeat "you do not know" and conclude that we must sow anyway.

## One truth to carry with you

Your acceptance before God rests in Christ, not in flawless performance. Jesus said "It is finished" of the one work that saves, and no human achievement can add to it. The perfectionist's measure of finished is "nothing could be improved," and no human work has ever met it. In ordinary human work, faithfulness does not require flawlessness: we are called to work carefully and honestly, while recognising that our acceptance before God rests in Christ rather than in error-free performance. Faithful work can be finished. The farmer who waits for perfect conditions never sows. You can do a task well, with care, for God, and then stop, and rest, and let it be what it is. That is not lowering the bar. It is recognising the difference between faithful care and a demand for certainty that no human work can satisfy.

## The lesson

A woman I will call Sarah kept the books for three small businesses and did it very well; her clients had never found an error. What they did not see was how the accuracy was produced. Sarah checked every invoice once, as her training required, then again, and then, most evenings, a third time before she could close the file. A month-end report a competent colleague would finish in an afternoon took her two days. The later checks were not making her more accurate; they had no purpose the first check had not served. But stopping meant sending something into the world that might be wrong, and that felt unbearable. Sarah came to talk to me not about her work but about her prayer life, which had dried up. The pattern was the same: she could not pray without wondering whether she had prayed properly. "I do not think I have ever finished anything," she said. "I have only ever stopped."

Ecclesiastes 11:4 pictures a farmer for whom there is always a reason not to sow: the wind is a little strong, there is a cloud in the west. The passage is about acting under uncertainty rather than perfectionism as we now use the word, and I mark the application: the perfectionist is the farmer who observes the wind. There is always some condition not quite right, some check not yet made, and so the work is not sown or not reaped. Ecclesiastes tells him the conditions will never be perfect and he must sow anyway, because sowing under uncertainty is what faithful work looks like. Beside it, with care, stands "It is finished" (John 19:30). We should not equate Christ's unique mission with our daily work; the narrower implication is that because our acceptance rests in Christ, we are free to do faithful work without making it the basis of our worth.

Perfectionism is not high standards. Some work must meet exact safety, legal or professional standards: a medication calculation must be correct and checked according to clinical procedure; a pilot's checklist must be completed according to aviation requirements; a legal translation must meet the applicable professional standard. Those standards are set by the task, and meeting them is competence. The distinction is this. Excellence asks what improvements meaningfully serve the purpose of the work, and can recognise completion. Perfectionism asks what might protect me from every error, criticism or feeling of uncertainty, and keeps moving the standard, because its real goal, certainty about how the work will be received, cannot be produced by the work. Its marks: an unreachable standard ("certainly accurate" rather than "accurate"); checking that no longer serves a purpose; a disproportionate cost; finishing that brings no satisfaction; mistakes that feel like verdicts on worth; and a spread, until even prayer can be done wrong. Perfectionism should not automatically be labelled a sin; it is often fear-driven or compulsive, though like any pattern it can become entangled with pride or avoidance.

"Jesus said, 'You must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect.' Perfectionism is just taking that seriously." Matthew 5:48 deserves a careful reading. The Greek word can mean complete, mature or whole, and its context is the Father's expansive love and Jesus' command to love even enemies. Whatever else it means, it is not a command to perform every ordinary task without error. More broadly, our standing before God rests on Christ's finished work, and perfectionism can cause a Christian to live functionally as though acceptance depended on flawless performance even while sincerely believing the gospel. That contradiction is an invitation to receive grace more deeply, not evidence that the faith is false. A person whose acceptance rests in Christ can acknowledge and repair inevitable human errors without treating them as verdicts on personal worth. In ordinary human work, faithfulness does not require flawlessness.

It helps to have names for the standards we use, because the perfectionist has only one. Required: what must be done for safety, ethics or competence, set by the task or profession, not negotiable, and not perfectionism. Excellent: what meaningful improvement would serve the purpose, the extra draft that makes the talk clearer, the read that makes the letter kinder. Wise: is the additional effort proportionate to its benefit and cost, weighed against time, other responsibilities and the people waiting? Perfectionistic: am I pursuing certainty, or protection from criticism, that the task cannot provide? The first three are high and finite, and finite standards can be reached. A caution: a perfectionist can turn "Have I been wise enough?" into a new unreachable standard; if that happens, make a reasonable judgement and stop. Answering these questions does not remove the discomfort of stopping. The feeling that it is not ready will arrive whether the work is excellent or not, which is exactly why it cannot decide when the work is finished. The standard decides that.

The practice is to define "finished" before you start, and stop when you reach it. Notice: I am perfecting. Write down what finished looks like: what is required, what would meaningfully improve it, how much time is proportionate, what check the task genuinely requires. Do the work to that standard and stop, not when it feels ready, because it will not, but when the standard you wrote is met. Once the predefined standard and required checks have been completed, close or submit the work, and do not reopen it solely to obtain reassurance; if materially new information, a genuine error report or an authorised quality-control process requires review, respond appropriately. Let the fear say its sentences (they might notice; look once more) without arguing and without acting on them. If no new evidence, requirement or quality-control purpose justifies another check, the urge is seeking reassurance, and a further check cannot permanently supply it. Where you are unsure whether a standard is set by the task or by your fear, consult the documented procedure, the regulatory requirement or an appropriately authorised supervisor; in safety-critical or regulated work, follow the official process rather than using this worksheet to decide how many checks are necessary. If an error comes to light later, use Lesson 7: repair, learn, continue, release. A mistake is a mistake; it is not a verdict on your worth. And where perfectionism has reached into prayer, as it had for Sarah: God is not marking it. Pray very short, very plain prayers, deliberately imperfect, and let them stand. Father, here I am. This is what I have. Thank you. That is a finished prayer.

A woman I will call Sarah kept the books for three small businesses and did it very well. Her clients had never found an error. She was proud of that, and she had reason to be.

What her clients did not see was how the accuracy was produced. Sarah checked every invoice against its source once, as her training required, and then again, and then, most evenings, a third time before she could close the file. A month-end report that a competent colleague would have finished in an afternoon took her two days. She stayed at her desk after her children were in bed, not because there was more work but because the work she had done did not yet feel safe. When she finally sent a report, she reread the sent copy. Twice.

The later checks were not making her more accurate. They had no quality-control purpose that the first check had not already served. But they had become impossible to stop, because stopping meant sending something into the world that might be wrong, and that felt unbearable.

Sarah came to talk to me not about her work but about her prayer life, which she said had dried up. As we talked, it became clear that the pattern was the same. She could not pray without wondering whether she had prayed properly. She could not read her Bible without feeling she had not read enough. She had, she said, never once finished a day feeling that she had done what she should.

“I do not think I have ever finished anything,” she said. “I have only ever stopped.”

### Central Scripture: Ecclesiastes 11:4

He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap. (Ecclesiastes 11:4, ESV) The Preacher is writing about a farmer, and the picture is exact. There is always a reason not to sow. The wind is a little strong today; it may scatter the seed. There is a cloud in the west; it may rain, or it may not. A farmer who waits for perfect conditions will never put seed in the ground, and a farmer who waits for a guaranteed dry day will never bring the harvest in. The surrounding verses press the point: “you do not know” is repeated, and the conclusion is that we should sow in the morning and in the evening, because we cannot know which will prosper.

The verse is not, in its own setting, about perfectionism as we now use the word. It is about acting under uncertainty. But the application is close enough to state plainly, and I mark it as an application. The perfectionist is the farmer who observes the wind. There is always something that could go wrong, some condition that is not quite right, some check not yet made, and so the work is not sown or not reaped. Ecclesiastes does not tell the farmer to be careless. It tells him that the conditions will never be perfect, that he cannot know the outcome, and that he must sow anyway, because sowing under uncertainty is what faithful work looks like in a world we do not control.

There is a second passage worth setting beside this one, with care. On the cross Jesus said, “It is finished” (John 19:30). In John’s Gospel, these words announce the completion of the unique work for which he came, culminating in his sacrificial death. Christians rightly locate their salvation and peace in Christ’s completed work, to which no human achievement can add. That is the gospel, and it is where every Christian’s peace ultimately rests.

The night before, Jesus had prayed, “I glorified you on earth, having accomplished the work that you gave me to do” (John 17:4). Jesus’ mission was not measured by whether every possible human need had been addressed during his earthly ministry. It was measured by faithful obedience to the work the Father entrusted to him. His mission is unique and cannot be reduced to a productivity lesson. Nevertheless, it reminds us that completion is defined by a true assignment, not by every task that could conceivably be attempted.

We should not make a direct equivalence between Christ’s redemptive mission and our daily work. The pastoral implication is narrower, and I offer it as such: because our acceptance rests in Christ rather than in flawless performance, we are free to pursue faithful work without trying to make it the basis of our worth. The perfectionist’s measure of finished is “nothing could be improved.” That is not a measure any human work has ever met, and the gospel does not ask us to meet it.

### What Perfectionism Is, and What It Is Not

Perfectionism is often misunderstood, in both directions, so it is worth being precise.

Perfectionism is not the same as high standards. Some work must be very good. Sarah’s clients were right to expect accurate books. A nurse’s drug calculations, a pilot’s checklist, a translator’s rendering of a legal contract: these have real standards, set by the task, and meeting them is not perfectionism but competence. This chapter is not asking anyone to care less about quality where quality matters, and it is not suspicious of excellence.

The distinction is this. Excellence asks what improvements meaningfully serve the purpose of the work. Perfectionism asks what might protect me from every error, criticism or feeling of uncertainty. Excellence can recognise completion. Perfectionism keeps moving the standard because its real goal, certainty about how the work will be received, cannot be produced by the work.

Perfectionism has some recognisable features.

The standard is unreachable. Not “accurate,” which can be achieved and confirmed, but “certainly accurate,” which cannot. Not “a good talk,” but “a talk no one could fault.” Because the standard cannot be met, the work cannot be finished, only abandoned.

The checking has stopped serving a purpose. Sarah’s first check was required by her professional process. The later checks appeared to be driven primarily by the need to feel certain rather than by any new quality-control purpose. The relevant question is not simply whether an extra check has ever found an error. Ask whether the check is required by the task, proportionate to the risk, recommended by appropriate professional practice, and meaningfully different from checks already completed.

The cost is disproportionate. A task that should take an afternoon takes two days. A message that should take five minutes takes an hour. The time is taken from other things, and the other things are usually the people and the rest that the person says they value more.

Finishing brings no satisfaction. The perfectionist does not end a piece of work feeling that it is done well. They end it feeling that it has been let go before it was ready, and the relief is mixed with dread.

Mistakes feel catastrophic. An error is not an error but a verdict: on competence, on worth, sometimes on standing before God. We will come back to this.

It spreads. What begins in one area, work or study or home, tends to colonise others, until, as with Sarah, even prayer is something that can be done wrong.

Perfectionism should not automatically be labelled a sin. It is often a fear-driven or compulsive pattern, and for some people it is connected to a mental-health condition; the box below is about that. At the same time, like any pattern, it may become entangled with pride, control, avoidance or neglect of other responsibilities. Those dimensions should be addressed honestly, without turning every urge to check into a moral failure.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“Jesus said, ‘You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect.’ Perfectionism is just taking that seriously.”

The verse is Matthew 5:48, and it deserves a careful reading, because a great many conscientious Christians have carried it as a burden.

The Greek word translated “perfect” can carry senses such as complete, mature or whole. Its immediate context concerns the expansive love of the Father, who gives sun and rain to both the just and the unjust, and Jesus’ command that his disciples love even their enemies. Whatever broader theological implications the verse carries, and it has been discussed at length, it is not a command to perform every ordinary task without error. Whatever Jesus meant, he did not mean that his followers should check their invoices three times.

More broadly, the gospel that Sarah believed, and that this course assumes, is that our standing before God does not rest on our performance. It rests on the finished work of Christ. Perfectionism can cause a Christian to live functionally as though acceptance depended on flawless performance, even when they sincerely believe the gospel. That contradiction is an invitation to receive grace more deeply, not evidence that their faith is false. The person who has taken hold of their acceptance can afford to make mistakes. The person still living as though it must be earned finds it hard even to finish.

That does not mean God is indifferent to how we work. Scripture calls us to work heartily, as for the Lord, and to do things well. But the standard is faithfulness, not flawlessness, and faithful work can be finished. You can do a task well, with care, for God, and then stop, and rest, and let it be what it is. That is not lowering the bar. It is putting the bar where God put it.

### Faithful, Wise or Perfect?

It helps to have names for the standards we actually use, because the perfectionist usually has only one, and it is the wrong one. Faithfulness, doing what has been given to you with care and for God, is the overarching standard Scripture commends, and it governs the four questions below rather than being a separate measurement of its own.

Required. What must be done for safety, ethics or professional competence? This is set by the task, the profession, the client or the person affected. It is not negotiable, and it is not perfectionism.

Excellent. What meaningful improvement would serve the purpose of the work? Excellence is a good thing. It is the extra draft that makes the talk clearer, the additional read that makes the letter kinder. It is oriented towards the purpose, and it can recognise when the purpose has been served.

Wise. Is the additional effort proportionate to its benefit and its cost? This is the standard of the Proverbs and of Ecclesiastes 11. It weighs the work against the time, the other responsibilities, and the people waiting.

Perfectionistic. Am I pursuing certainty, or protection from criticism, that the task cannot provide? This is the question that exposes the third check. The answer is not always yes, but when it is, the effort has stopped serving the work.

The first three standards are high. They are not an excuse for carelessness. But they are finite, and finite standards can be reached. Two cautions belong here. These are not mathematical thresholds that produce absolute certainty; they are responsible judgements made with the information, time and capacity available. And the aim is not to become perfectly certain that you have been faithful and wise. A perfectionist can turn “Have I been wise enough?” into a new unreachable standard. If you notice that happening, the question has been captured, and the answer is to make a reasonable judgement and stop.

For Sarah’s books, the required standard was: accurate, checked as her professional process specified, delivered on time. Excellent might have been a clear covering note. Wise was: finished within the working day, with the evening left for her family. Those are demanding standards. They are also finite, which is the whole point. The phrase “good enough” makes the perfectionist wince, because it sounds like settling. What it actually means is “required, excellent where excellence serves, and wise”: what the task genuinely needs, done with care, and then finished.

Answering these questions does not remove the discomfort of stopping. The perfectionist will still feel, at the moment of sending, that it is not ready. That feeling will arrive whether the work is excellent or not, which is exactly why it cannot be allowed to decide when the work is finished. The standard decides that. The feeling follows later, or does not, and either way the work is done.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is to define “finished” before you start, and then to stop when you reach it.

Begin, as always, by noticing. When you find yourself checking again, redrafting again, or unable to send, name it: I am perfecting. The naming is not a rebuke. It is a reminder to ask which standard is now running.

Then, before beginning a task, or as soon as you catch yourself in one, define what finished looks like. Write it down. What is required? What would meaningfully improve it? How much time is proportionate? If the task has a required check, note what it is.

Then do the work to that standard, and stop. Not when it feels ready, because it will not, but when the standard you wrote down has been met. If you are able, send it, submit it, or close it, before the urge to reopen it has time to gather.

Then notice what the fear says, and let it say it. It might be wrong. They might notice. You should look once more. You do not have to argue with these sentences. You only have to decline to act on them. If no new evidence, requirement or identifiable quality-control purpose justifies another check, the urge may be seeking reassurance rather than accuracy, and reassurance is not something a further check can permanently supply.

If an error does come to light later, handle it as Lesson 7 taught: repair what can be repaired, learn what can be learned, continue any responsibility that remains, and release the rest. A mistake is a mistake. It may require repair. It is not a verdict on your worth.

A word about the spread into prayer and Scripture, since that was where Sarah felt it most. Prayer is not assessed like a technical task. Scripture does address our motives and posture in prayer, but God is not waiting to reject a sincere prayer because its wording, length or emotional tone was imperfect. The Father who hears it is not marking it. If you have been unable to pray for fear of praying badly, you may find it helps to pray very short, very plain prayers, deliberately imperfect, and to let them stand. Father, here I am. This is what I have. Thank you. That is a finished prayer.

> **A note on work that must be very good**
>
> Nothing in this chapter asks you to lower a standard that the task, your profession, or someone’s safety genuinely sets. A required check is required. A regulated process is regulated. If you are unsure whether a standard is set by the task or by your fear, ask a competent colleague or supervisor what they would do, and treat their answer as the standard. What this chapter questions is the checking, redoing and delaying that go beyond what any careful professional would consider necessary.
