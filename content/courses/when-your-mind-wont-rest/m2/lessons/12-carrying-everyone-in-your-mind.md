# Lesson 12: Carrying Everyone in Your Mind

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to tell caring from carrying, ask five questions before picking up someone else's burden, and sort the weight into carry, share, offer, refer or release.

## Before you begin

Some readers are carrying a genuine, heavy load: a dependent child, a parent with dementia, a spouse with a serious illness. This lesson is not asking you to put that down; it asks whether you are also carrying things that are not yours, and whether the load that is yours has any support. If caregiving is affecting your sleep, mood, health or ability to function, speak with a doctor or qualified healthcare professional, and ask a recognised health, social-care or caregiver-support service what practical assistance, respite or assessment is available where you live. If the person is in immediate danger, may act on thoughts of harming themselves or cannot remain safe, contact an appropriate emergency or crisis service now; do not try to manage the danger alone. Open Finding Help Where You Live for current contact routes; opening that page does not alert Faithful Path or contact help for you. Where danger is not immediate, seek appropriate professional advice and shared support. If someone makes you responsible for their moods, decisions or behaviour and frightens, controls, threatens or punishes you when you fail, consider contacting a specialist domestic-abuse service or safeguarding professional; do not confront the person or abruptly change an established arrangement if doing so may increase danger, and seek safety guidance first. And do not withdraw abruptly from an established responsibility, childcare, medication management, safeguarding, essential care, to test whether it is yours. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for each person and situation you need to consider, beginning with one person rather than sorting everyone at once; do not use it in the middle of the night, and do not repeat it because anxiety has returned.

## Key Scripture: Galatians 6:2–5 (ESV)

> Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ. For if anyone thinks he is something, when he is nothing, he deceives himself. But let each one test his own work, and then his reason to boast will be in himself alone and not in his neighbor. For each will have to bear his own load.

Within four verses Paul says both things. The community helps carry what is too heavy for one; each person also has their own work to test and their own life to answer for. Neither cancels the other.

## One truth to carry with you

You are not responsible for controlling every person or preventing every painful outcome. Love does not require you to carry everyone. It requires you to help carry what is too heavy for others, to carry your own load faithfully, and to recognise that other adults retain agency and responsibilities appropriate to their capacity and circumstances. Loving support does not automatically make every part of another person's life yours to manage. Entrusting someone to God does not mean that nothing painful will happen. It means recognising that God's care is not limited by your ability to anticipate, control or prevent every outcome. If the weight returns to your mind later, its return does not mean that you made the wrong decision or failed to entrust the person to God.

## The lesson

A woman I will call Lydia could not sleep, and when I asked what kept her awake she gave me a list. Her adult son's marriage was in difficulty. Her mother, in her eighties, was becoming forgetful and refused to see a doctor. A friend from church had been made redundant and was not answering messages. A colleague going through a divorce had become short-tempered, and Lydia had been covering for her. Her husband had a hospital appointment next month that he was not worried about, but she was. "I feel as though I am holding all of them up," she said. "And if I put any of them down, something will happen." I asked her which of these people had actually asked for her involvement, and which responsibilities arose from an established role. Her mother's forgetfulness was a genuine concern, and as a daughter she had a real part, though not the whole. Her husband's appointment was his, though marriage gave her a place beside him. The rest she had picked up, one by one, because she loved these people and it had not occurred to her that there was any other way to love them. Now she lay awake under the weight of six people, most of whom did not know they were being carried.

Paul says two things in four verses that sound contradictory: bear one another's burdens; each will have to bear his own load. The point of the passage as a whole is that believers are responsible both to support one another and to examine their own conduct before God. Applying it directly to compulsive rescuing would go beyond what the passage states, so I will not. As a pastoral application: love does not require you to carry everyone. It requires you to help carry what is too heavy for others, to carry your own load faithfully, and to recognise that other adults have loads God has given to them and not to you.

Lydia's pattern has many names, people-pleasing, rescuing, over-functioning; I will not use a clinical label. What it has in common everywhere is a confusion between caring about someone and carrying them. Caring notices, feels, prays, and offers what is appropriate: presence, help, a word, a referral. Carrying treats another person's life, feelings or decisions as yours to manage. It plans, worries and anticipates on their behalf, and quietly assumes their agency. It is hard to see because it looks like love and often begins as love; because it feels necessary ("if I do not, no one will"); because it can be a way of managing one's own anxiety; and because it can shade into control, when the carrier begins to need the other person to be helped in the way the carrier has decided. Most carriers are among the kindest people in any church. But kindness that has become carrying is not serving the people it carries, and it is slowly consuming the person doing it.

Whether a burden is yours depends on role, need, capacity, consent, safety and consequence together. Five questions make the answer honest. Did they ask? A request matters, though children, vulnerable adults and people in danger may need action without asking, and some requests are inappropriate. Do I have an established responsibility, as parent, spouse, carer, employee or leader? Is there a serious need they cannot presently address alone? Am I the appropriate person, or does this belong to a doctor, a counsellor, a social worker or a service? Is the proposed help wanted, safe and sustainable? Lydia's mother met several of these. Her son's marriage met almost none. With those questions in hand the weight can be sorted into five kinds. Carry: a responsibility that properly belongs to my role; the question is whether you are carrying only your load, and whether it is supported. Share: too heavy for one and I can appropriately help, in a specific, bounded way; you carry alongside, not instead. Offer: I may offer support, but the other person keeps the choice. Refer: the need requires someone more qualified; Lydia's mother needed a doctor, not a daughter's worry. Release: I have no appropriate action, or my part is complete; your adult son's marriage is his and his wife's. Release, applied to people, means not abandonment but entrusting the person to God and to their own responsibility.

"If I stop worrying about them, it means I do not care." Love does feel the weight of other people's difficulties, and indifference is not a Christian option. But worry is not a form of love, and the amount of carrying does not measure the amount of care. Gently: if I need to carry people in order to feel that I love them, or that I am useful, then the carrying is doing something for me as well as for them, and putting it down will feel like a loss even when it is a relief. The people you love are loved by God more than by you. Entrusting someone to God does not mean that nothing painful will happen; it means recognising that God's care is not limited by your ability to anticipate, control or prevent every outcome.

The practice is to notice, I am carrying, ask the five questions of that person honestly in both directions, sort the weight, and give each its response. What is yours to carry, do today, and no more than today requires, and ask what support it needs. What is yours to share or offer, make specific and bounded, and sometimes wait until help is asked for. What is yours to release, release deliberately: say the name, say what you have been carrying, say that it is theirs and God's, and then do not pick it back up when the worry returns. Release is not a feeling that arrives; it is a direction you keep choosing. Use a brief reminder rather than repeating the worksheet or the prayer until you feel relief: I have identified my responsibility; I will do what is mine and leave what is not. The return of anxiety alone is not new information. About "if I do not, no one will": sometimes it is true, and then you may need to seek help so you are not carrying alone. But do not withdraw abruptly from an established responsibility to test it; reduce only the actions that are unnecessary, unrequested or beyond your role, the anxious message, the unasked advice, the mental planning of a capable adult's decisions. Finally, bring the people to God by name. Prayer for someone is not the same as carrying them, though the carrier often confuses the two.

A woman I will call Lydia came to see me because she could not sleep, and when I asked what was keeping her awake she gave me a list.

Her adult son’s marriage was in difficulty, and she lay awake working out what she could say to him, and to his wife, and whether she should say anything at all. Her mother, in her eighties, was becoming forgetful and refused to see a doctor. A friend from church had been made redundant and was not answering messages. The small group Lydia led had lost two members, and she wondered what she had done. A colleague at the school office where she worked was going through a divorce and had become short- tempered, and Lydia had been absorbing it and covering for her. Her husband, she added almost as an afterthought, had a hospital appointment next month that he was not worried about, but she was.

“I feel,” she said, “as though I am holding all of them up. And if I put any of them down, something will happen.”

I asked her some questions she found difficult. Which of these people had actually requested her involvement? Which responsibilities arose from an established role or commitment—as a daughter, a wife, a group leader, an employee? And which burdens had she assumed without either a request or a responsibility?

She thought for a long time. Her mother’s forgetfulness was a genuine concern, and as a daughter she had a real part to play, though not the whole of it. Her husband’s appointment was his, though marriage gave her a place beside him. The small group was her responsibility to lead, but not to keep from ever shrinking. The rest—her son’s marriage, her friend’s redundancy, her colleague’s divorce—she had picked up, one by one, because she loved these people and because it did not occur to her that there was any other way to love them. And now she was lying awake under the weight of six people, most of whom did not know they were being carried.

### Central Scripture: Galatians 6:2–5

Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ. For if anyone thinks he is something, when he is nothing, he deceives himself. But let each one test his own work, and then his reason to boast will be in himself alone and not in his neighbor. For each will have to bear his own load. (Galatians 6:2–5, ESV) Within four verses, Paul says two things that sound, on first reading, as though they contradict each other. Bear one another’s burdens. Each will have to bear his own load.

Paul uses two different Greek terms here, but their meanings overlap, so we should not construct a rigid distinction based on vocabulary alone. The clearer point arises from the passage as a whole: believers are responsible both to support one another and to examine their own conduct before God. There are burdens that the community is called to help carry. And there is a load that belongs to each person: their own work to test, their own life to answer for. Both are true. Neither cancels the other.

The context helps. Paul has just described how to respond to a brother caught in sin: restore him gently, watching yourself. He is writing about a community in which people are genuinely involved in one another’s lives, and he guards against two failures: indifference, in which each person leaves the others to sink; and self-deception, in which a person loses sight of his own work. His warning against thinking oneself “something” calls each person away from self-deception and comparison. In context, it may particularly challenge the pride that refuses gentle burden-bearing while remaining preoccupied with another person’s failure. Applying it directly to compulsive rescuing would go beyond what the passage states, so I will not.

As a pastoral application, then: love does not require you to carry everyone. It requires you to help carry what is too heavy for others, to carry your own load faithfully, and to recognise that other adults have loads of their own that God has given to them and not to you. The categories offered later in this chapter are a pastoral tool for applying those two responsibilities; they are not a direct outline supplied by the passage.

### Caring and Carrying

The pattern in Lydia’s story has several names in different settings: people-pleasing, rescuing, over-functioning, the caretaker role. I will not use a clinical label, because the pattern is broader than any diagnosis. What it has in common, wherever it appears, is a confusion between caring about someone and carrying them.

Caring notices another person’s difficulty, feels it, prays about it, and offers what is appropriate: presence, help, a word, practical support, a referral to someone better placed. Healthy care respects the other person’s agency, the roles involved, and the carer’s own limits, although even appropriate care can become exhausting and require additional support or respite.

Carrying treats another person’s life, feelings or decisions as though they have become yours to manage. It plans on their behalf, worries on their behalf, anticipates on their behalf. It may involve acting without consent when no emergency or established responsibility justifies doing so. It does not leave the other person their agency, because the carrier has quietly assumed it. It is rarely sustainable, and it usually ends in exhaustion, resentment or both.

Some things make carrying hard to see for what it is.

It looks like love, and often begins as love. Lydia carried people because she loved them. The error was not in the love but in the form it had taken.

It feels necessary. “If I do not, no one will.” Sometimes that is true, and we will come to those cases. Often it is an assumption that has never been examined against what the person’s situation actually requires and who else is placed to help.

It is rewarded. Churches, workplaces and families tend to praise the person who carries everyone, and to depend on them, which makes it difficult to stop.

It can be a way of managing one’s own anxiety. Carrying someone else’s problem gives the mind something to do with its fear. Lydia’s worry about her husband’s appointment was, in part, fear she could not resolve; carrying her colleague’s divorce was something she could at least be busy with.

It can shade into control. The carrier, without meaning to, begins to need the other person to be helped in the way the carrier has decided. A son who does not take the advice becomes a source of hurt. This is where love, which seeks the other’s good, quietly becomes something that seeks the carrier’s relief.

None of this is said to shame anyone who carries. Most carriers are among the kindest people in any church. But kindness that has become carrying is not serving the people it carries, and it is slowly consuming the person doing it.

### Five Questions Before You Pick Something Up

Whether a burden is yours does not depend on any single test. It depends on role, need, capacity, consent, safety and consequence together. Before assuming responsibility for someone else’s difficulty, or while examining one you have already assumed, it helps to ask five questions.

Did they ask? A request matters, though it is not decisive. Children, vulnerable adults, people in danger and people who cannot articulate their needs may require action without asking. And someone may ask for help that is manipulative, inappropriate or beyond your role.

Do I have an established responsibility? Some duties arise from a role or a commitment: parent, spouse, carer, employee, leader. Those are yours whether or not anyone asks.

Is there a serious need they cannot presently address alone? A capable adult managing an ordinary difficulty is in a different position from someone in crisis, ill, or genuinely without resources.

Am I the appropriate person to respond? Sometimes the need is real but belongs to a doctor, a counsellor, a pastor, a social worker or a service, and the loving thing is to refer rather than to become the service yourself.

Is the proposed help wanted, safe and sustainable? Help that the person does not want, that puts anyone at risk, or that you cannot maintain is not help.

These questions do not make the answer automatic, but they make it honest. Lydia’s mother met several of them. Her son’s marriage met almost none.

### Five Kinds of Weight

With those questions in hand, the weight can be sorted. I offer five categories, which is more than the mind wants at midnight but fewer than the situations actually require.

Carry. A responsibility properly belongs to my role. Some things are genuinely yours and no one else’s: your own conduct, your own decisions, your relationship with God, and the care of those who actually depend on you. If a child or dependent person is in your care, you have real responsibilities appropriate to their age, their needs and your role. Those responsibilities may be shared with another parent, family members or professionals, and they should increasingly respect the person’s developing agency. A commitment to help care for a parent matters, but no promise makes one person capable of providing unlimited care; changing medical needs, safety, capacity and the availability of appropriate services may require the arrangement to be reconsidered. The question is not whether to carry your load, but whether you are carrying only your load, and whether it is supported.

Share. The burden is too heavy for one person and I can appropriately help. A friend in grief, a family in crisis, a brother caught in sin: love comes alongside. Appropriate shared support can genuinely lighten what is too heavy for one person. But sharing is specific and bounded: a meal, a visit, a practical task, a season of support. The friend still grieves her own grief; you carry alongside her, not instead of her.

Offer. I may offer support, but the other person retains the choice. This covers a great deal of ordinary Christian love: the conversation you make yourself available for, the advice you give once if asked, the practical help you propose and then leave with them. Your spouse’s medical care remains principally their responsibility and should respect their agency, but marriage may give you an appropriate supporting role; you can accompany, listen and help without taking control of the outcome. Offering respects the person’s agency in a way that carrying does not.

Refer. The need requires someone more qualified or authorised. Lydia’s mother needed a doctor, not a daughter’s worry. A person in danger needs the relevant service. A friend in prolonged low mood may need a professional. Referring is not passing the buck; it is recognising that love sometimes means knowing who else to call.

Release. I have no appropriate action, or my part is complete. Your adult son’s marriage is his and his wife’s. Your friend’s redundancy is hers. You may care, pray, and offer what is appropriate to offer, once. But the weight belongs to the person to whom God has given it. Taking responsibility that belongs to another person may fail to help them and can create a second burden in you.

The reader who has followed this course will recognise the shape of this. The four faithful responses introduced in Lesson 2 and developed in Lesson 10 are act, wait, seek help, surrender. Sorting the weight applies them to people. Carry calls for action. Share and offer may call for action, or for waiting until help is wanted. Refer is seeking help on someone’s behalf. Release is surrender, which, applied to people, has a particular meaning: not abandonment, but entrusting the person to God and to their own responsibility, and continuing to love them without holding them up.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I stop worrying about them, it means I do not care. Carrying people is what love looks like.”

This belief is deeply held by most carriers, and it has just enough truth in it to be persuasive.

It is true that love feels the weight of other people’s difficulties, and that indifference is not a Christian option. But it does not follow that worry is a form of love, or that the amount of carrying measures the amount of care. Lydia’s sleepless nights were not helping her son’s marriage. They were not helping her friend, who had not asked to be helped and might not have wanted to be. Her carrying was not reaching the people she carried. It was reaching only Lydia.

There is also something in this belief that deserves gentle examination. If I need to carry people in order to feel that I love them, or in order to feel that I am useful, or loved, or safe, then the carrying is doing something for me as well as for them. That is not a reason for shame, but it is worth knowing, because it means that putting the burden down will feel like a loss even when it is a relief, and it is better to expect that.

Finally, the belief misunderstands what God has asked of us. Scripture tells us to cast our anxieties on him because he cares for us. It does not tell us to gather up everyone else’s anxieties first. The people you love are loved by God more than by you. To release them to him is not to drop them. It is to hand them to someone who will not.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is to name the people you are carrying, ask the five questions, sort the weight, and give each its faithful response.

Begin by noticing. When you find your mind running through someone else’s difficulties, planning their solutions, anticipating their crises, name it: I am carrying. Then ask the five questions of that person and that burden, honestly, in both directions. The carrier’s temptation is to call everything “mine”; but the sorting must not become an excuse for putting down what genuinely is yours.

Then give each its response.

If it is yours to carry, do what it requires, today, and no more than today requires; and if the load is heavy, ask what support it needs.

If it is yours to share or offer, decide what specific, bounded help is appropriate, whether it is wanted, and whether it can be sustained. Sometimes the answer is to wait until help is asked for. Sometimes it is to offer once, plainly, and accept the answer.

If it is yours to refer, point the person to the doctor, counsellor, pastor or service they actually need, and help them get there if that is appropriate; then let the professional be the professional.

If it is yours to release, do so deliberately. Say the person’s name. Say what you have been carrying for them. Say that it is theirs and God’s. Then, and this is the part the carrier finds nearly impossible, do not pick it back up when the worry returns an hour later. The worry will return. Release is not a feeling that arrives; it is a direction you keep choosing, and each time the weight returns you may set it down again without treating the return as failure.

A word about the sentence “if I do not, no one will.” Sometimes it is true, and in those cases the person may genuinely be yours to share, and you may need to seek help so that you are not carrying alone. But do not withdraw abruptly from an established responsibility to test it. Instead, identify what your role actually requires, communicate clearly with the people involved, seek shared support, and reduce only those actions that are unnecessary, unrequested or beyond your proper responsibility. It is those actions—the anxious message, the unasked advice, the mental planning of a capable adult’s decisions—that can be set down safely.

Finally, bring the people to God, by name. Prayer for someone is not the same as carrying them, though the carrier often confuses the two. Prayer entrusts the person to God and asks what faithful love requires from us. We may not immediately feel lighter, but we can refuse to treat continued worry as proof that we must assume control. We will look more closely at prayer that does not become more worry in Lesson 16.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that fit your situation and leave aside any that do not.*

Father, you care for the people I love more than I do. I confess that I have picked up burdens that were not mine, and that I have called it love when some of it was fear, and some of it was a need to be needed.

I bring you the people I have been carrying, by name. (Say them.) What is mine to do for them, show me, and let me do it today. What is too heavy to do alone, help me to share and to ask for help. What they need from someone else, help me to point them towards. What is theirs, help me to leave with them, and to love them without holding them up.

And if the weight returns to my mind, remind me that its return is not a command to take control, that I have already entrusted it to you, and that I may set it down again.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
