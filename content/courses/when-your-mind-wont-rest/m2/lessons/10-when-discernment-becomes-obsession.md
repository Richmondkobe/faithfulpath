# Lesson 10: When Discernment Becomes Obsession

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to tell discernment from confirmation-seeking, weigh the ordinary means God has given, and answer honestly whether you need more information or more courage.

## Before you begin

If you find every decision, large and small, nearly impossible, and the difficulty is persistent and affects daily life, this can occur with anxiety conditions or other forms of distress; a qualified mental-health professional can assess what is happening and discuss appropriate support. If you need to be certain a decision is God's will before you can act, feel that a wrong choice would be a sin, and cannot rest even after deciding, this can occur with scrupulosity or other obsessive-compulsive symptoms; consider seeking a qualified mental-health professional who understands obsessive-compulsive difficulties and can work respectfully with your Christian faith, and Finding Help Where You Live shows where to look. Not every decision belongs in this lesson: medical, legal, financial and safety decisions require qualified information, and gathering it is not obsession. The courage this lesson commends is not recklessness; a major or difficult-to-reverse decision deserves the ordinary means used carefully. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for one decision, and not in the middle of the night. Where possible, do not make a major decision during acute exhaustion, severe illness or a crisis: maintain safety, seek appropriate help and return to the decision when you have sufficient capacity. If a deadline makes delay impossible, obtain relevant qualified advice.

## Key Scripture: James 1:5–8 (ESV)

> If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him. But let him ask in faith, with no doubting, for the one who doubts is like a wave of the sea that is driven and tossed by the wind. For that person must not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord; he is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.

James promises God's generous provision of wisdom to believers under trial. He does not promise a sign or an unmistakable answer to every decision, and in context his description of the "double-minded" person appears to concern divided allegiance, not simply uncertainty about which option to choose.

## One truth to carry with you

God gives wisdom generously and is not a cruel examiner. He is not waiting to see how long you will keep asking before he answers. James promises wisdom, not a sign; God may give you wisdom without naming the city. Your future does not hang on the thread of one correctly guessed choice. It hangs on the faithfulness of God, which is a great deal stronger, and Scripture is full of people who chose without a clear word and whom he nonetheless led, corrected and used. Many decisions are between permissible options, where he has entrusted the choosing to you. You do not need certainty to act in faith. If a decision proves unwise, God will remain faithful, and you can ask him for the humility to recognise it, take responsibility and respond wisely. Deciding is an act of faith, not the absence of one.

## The lesson

A nurse I will call Rebecca was offered a post in a hospital in another city: better hours, a specialism she had wanted for years, a good church nearby. She had three weeks to accept. She prayed. Her pastor, her small group and her parents all said it sounded like a good opportunity. She asked two friends who had made similar moves, and then two more. A sermon mentioned Abraham leaving Ur, and she took it as a sign; on Tuesday a song about staying rooted, and she took that as a sign too. She asked God for a clear word, then a clearer one. She began to feel that the absence of an answer might itself be the answer, and an hour later that it might be a test. On the day the offer expired she had not decided, and it lapsed. She told me she felt she had failed God by not hearing him. I asked what she had wanted to do. "To go," she said, without hesitation. She had wanted to go from the first day. Rebecca did not lack the ordinary information needed for a responsible choice. She lacked a certainty no adviser, sign or process could provide, and she had spent three weeks calling the search for it discernment.

James 1:5 is a great comfort and, misread, a great burden. The comfort: God gives wisdom generously, to all who ask, without reproach. Notice what is promised: wisdom, not a specific sign or an unmistakable answer. The burden comes when "with no doubting" and "double-minded" are read as condemning ordinary uncertainty. In context James's concern is deeper: a person divided in allegiance, asking for wisdom while not really trusting God or intending to go their own way regardless. The passage should not be turned into a condemnation of every anxious question; if you have read it that way, please set that reading down. Scripture commends persistence in prayer; the pastoral concern arises when prayer becomes a compulsive attempt to obtain perfect certainty rather than an expression of dependence.

Discernment is a real discipline and this lesson is not against it. It uses the ordinary means: Does Scripture forbid or command this? What does wisdom suggest? What do trusted, mature people, and where necessary qualified professionals, advise? What do the circumstances permit? What do I actually want, and why? It is bounded: it gathers what can reasonably be gathered and then decides. Obsessive confirmation-seeking uses the same means but cannot stop. It asks the same question of another person, and another, hoping for a more certain answer. It reads coincidences as signs and the opposite coincidence as a counter-sign. It is not seeking guidance; it is seeking the removal of risk, and guidance cannot provide that. It is hard to spot because it looks devout, feels responsible, postpones the cost of choosing (while discerning, Rebecca did not yet have to give anything up), and can quietly shift responsibility: if God tells me clearly, the decision is his, and if it goes badly the fault is not mine.

"God has one right choice for me, and if I miss it I will be outside his will." Christians hold different views of how God guides, and I will not settle that here. Whatever your view, three things can be said with confidence. God is not a cruel examiner who condemns sincere believers for failing to decode a hidden message. God's providence is larger than our decisions; Scripture is full of people who chose badly, or without a clear word, and whom he led, corrected and used. And many decisions are between morally permissible options, where Scripture may leave real freedom to choose wisely, though options still carry different consequences. Rebecca was choosing between two permissible cities. A word on signs, because traditions differ: an impression, sermon, dream or coincidence may invite prayerful attention, but it should not automatically be treated as an instruction. A claimed word, from yourself or anyone else, is weighed, not obeyed automatically, and no leader may use "God told me" to control your decision. If you find yourself collecting signs in both directions, that is a reliable indication that the signs are not deciding the matter.

When your mind will not settle over a decision, there are four faithful responses. Act: you already have what you need; the moral question is clear or absent, counsel has been given, circumstances permit, you know what you want. Wait: there is a genuine reason, information you can obtain, a circumstance about to change, a person still to consult; waiting is faithful when it has a reason and an end. Seek help: you need expertise you do not have, a doctor, a lawyer, an adviser, a mature Christian who knows you; seeking help is bounded, and one expert identifying a further question is the ordinary shape of a complex decision, not obsession. Surrender: the decision is not yours, or the outcome is beyond your control, or you have done all that can be done.

So the practice is one question, asked honestly: What information is genuinely missing, and what courage will still be required after I have it? Notice first: I am seeking confirmation. Take stock of the ordinary means, and include desire: what you want is relevant information, not divine authorisation, to be weighed alongside character, responsibility and consequences; the overthinker often discounts it entirely, which is its own error. Once a suitable person has given a careful answer, write it down and use it. Then answer which fits. I need specific information: name what is missing, where it can be found, by when, and get it. I have sufficient information and need courage: you may have reached the point at which further discernment is no longer adding relevant information, and unless circumstances materially change, what remains may be the courage to decide, which is an act of faith. I need both: get the information, and know that when you have it the courage will still be required, so you do not mistake the next piece of information for the last. Finally, bring the decision to God as something you are making with him, not waiting for him to make: Father, I have asked for wisdom and I trust that you are giving it. This is what I intend to do. If it proves unwise, you will remain faithful, and I ask for the humility to recognise it, to take responsibility for what follows, and to respond wisely.

A nurse I will call Rebecca was offered a post in a hospital in another city. It was a good post: better hours, a specialism she had wanted for years, and a church near the hospital that a friend had recommended. She had three weeks to accept.

She began, sensibly, by praying about it. Then she asked her pastor, who said it sounded like a good opportunity and that she should pray. She asked her small group, who said the same. She asked her parents, who were sad but supportive. She asked two friends who had made similar moves, and then two more. She read every passage of Scripture she could find about journeys, calling and cities. She noticed that a sermon that Sunday mentioned Abraham leaving Ur, and took it as a sign. Then she noticed, on Tuesday, a song about staying rooted, and took that as a sign too. She asked God for a clear word. She asked for a clearer one. She asked her pastor again, this time whether he had sensed anything specific. He had not. She began to feel that the absence of a clear answer might itself be the answer, and then, an hour later, that it might be a test of her faith.

On the day the offer expired she had not decided, and it lapsed. She told me, some weeks later, that she felt she had failed God by not hearing him. I asked her what she had wanted to do. She said, without hesitation, that she had wanted to go. She had wanted to go from the first day.

Rebecca did not appear to lack the ordinary information needed to make a responsible choice. She had asked several wise people and been given broadly the same answer. She had a clear desire, a good opportunity, no moral objection and no practical obstacle. What she lacked was certainty that no adviser, sign or process could provide, and she had spent three weeks calling the search for it “discernment.”

### Central Scripture: James 1:5–8

If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him. But let him ask in faith, with no doubting, for the one who doubts is like a wave of the sea that is driven and tossed by the wind. For that person must not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord; he is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways. (James 1:5–8, ESV) This passage is a great comfort and, misread, a great burden, so it is worth being careful.

The comfort is in the first verse. God gives wisdom generously, to all who ask, without reproach. James is writing to believers under trial, and he tells them that God is not stingy with wisdom and does not scold those who need it. It is important, though, to notice what is promised. James promises God’s generous provision of wisdom; he does not promise a specific sign or an unmistakable answer to every decision. God may provide wisdom without identifying one uniquely required option. Rebecca could ask God for wisdom with confidence. She could not, from this verse, expect him to name the city.

The burden comes when “with no doubting” and “double-minded” are read as though James were condemning the ordinary uncertainty of a person facing a decision. In context, James’s concern appears to be deeper than ordinary uncertainty about which option to choose. The “double-minded” person is divided in allegiance and unstable in relation to God: asking for wisdom while not really trusting him, or intending to go one’s own way regardless. Christians may differ over the precise nuance, but the passage should not be turned into a condemnation of every anxious question or moment of uncertainty. If you have been reading it as one, please set that reading down.

As a pastoral application, then, I would put it this way. James does not teach that repeated prayer is wrong; Scripture commends persistence in prayer. The pastoral concern arises when prayer becomes a compulsive attempt to obtain perfect certainty or temporary reassurance rather than an expression of dependence on God. The person who asks in faith trusts that wisdom is being given, even when it arrives through ordinary means and without a sign, and is willing to act on it.

### What Discernment Is, and What It Becomes

Discernment is a real Christian discipline, and this chapter is not against it. Scripture tells us to test everything, to seek counsel, to weigh our ways, and to ask God for wisdom. A decision worth making is worth praying and thinking about.

But discernment, for the overthinker, can become something else. It can become an endless search for certainty, dressed in spiritual clothing, that never arrives at a decision because a decision would end the search. It helps to set the two side by side.

Discernment uses the ordinary means God has given. It asks: Does Scripture forbid this or command it? What does wisdom suggest? What do trusted, mature people, and where necessary qualified professionals, advise? What do the circumstances make possible? What do I actually want, and why? It is bounded. It gathers what can reasonably be gathered and then decides.

Obsessive confirmation-seeking uses the same means, but cannot stop. It asks the same question of another person, and another, hoping for a different answer or a more certain one. It reads the same passages again. It treats coincidences as signs and then treats the opposite coincidence as a counter-sign. It asks God for a clear word, and then for a clearer one, because no word is ever quite clear enough. It is not seeking guidance. It is seeking the removal of risk, and guidance cannot provide that.

Several things make the second hard to distinguish from the first.

It looks devout. Nobody criticises a person for praying more. But prayer that has become a demand for certainty is different from prayer that asks for wisdom and trusts the answer.

It feels responsible. Surely it is better to be sure? But in most decisions certainty is not available, and the person who waits for it is not being careful; they are refusing the risk that every decision involves.

It postpones the cost of choosing. As long as you are still discerning, you have not yet had to give anything up. Rebecca, while discerning, did not have to leave her church or accept the loss of the post. Discernment let her avoid both. It did not, in the end, let her keep either.

It can shift the responsibility. If God tells me clearly, then the decision is his, and if it goes badly, the fault is not mine. This is rarely conscious, but it is often present, and it explains why a clear word is so much wanted and never quite received.

> Some people find every decision, large and small, nearly impossible: not only career and marriage but which shirt to wear or what to order. If that is you, and the difficulty is persistent and affects your daily life, it may be a feature of an anxiety condition, and it responds well to professional help. The tools here can sit alongside such help, but they are not a substitute for it.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“God has one right choice for me, and if I miss it I will be outside his will.”

This belief drives a great deal of obsessive discernment, and it deserves a careful answer, because Christians hold different views about how God guides.

Some believers expect specific individual guidance for many decisions and testify to receiving it. Others hold that God’s will for us is expressed chiefly in Scripture and in wisdom, and that within those boundaries we are free to choose between permissible options. I do not want to settle that question here, and I do not need to. Whatever your view, three things can be said with confidence.

First, God is not a cruel examiner who condemns sincere believers for failing to decode a hidden message. James says he gives wisdom generously and without reproach. At the same time, he does not promise to disclose every detail of his providential purpose. We may have enough wisdom to act faithfully without knowing everything God is doing.

Second, God’s providence is larger than our decisions. Scripture is full of people who chose badly, or chose without a clear word, and whom God nonetheless led, corrected and used. Your future does not hang on the thread of one correctly guessed choice. It hangs on the faithfulness of God, which is a great deal stronger.

Third, many decisions are between morally permissible options. In such cases, Scripture may leave genuine freedom to choose wisely, although the options may still carry different consequences and responsibilities. Rebecca was not choosing between obedience and sin. She was choosing between two permissible cities, two hospitals and two churches. To ask God which one he insisted on was, in a sense, to ask him to make a choice he may well have entrusted to her.

None of this means decisions do not matter. They do, and some are weighty. But their weight is not the weight of being right or wrong before God; it is the ordinary weight of consequences, which we bear with his help. The person who understands this can decide. The person who believes there is one hidden right answer will search for it as long as the search is allowed to continue.

### Signs, Impressions and Words

Because this chapter is written for Christians from different traditions, the question of signs needs more than a passing word.

An impression, sermon, dream or coincidence may invite prayerful attention, depending on your theological tradition, but it should not automatically be treated as an instruction from God. Hold such experiences humbly, test them within the Christian community, and never allow them to override Scripture, truth, safety or appropriate professional advice.

A few principles follow from that, and they hold whatever your tradition.

Scripture should be interpreted in context, not opened at random for coded instructions. A sermon or song may prompt reflection without becoming a command. An impression should be held humbly rather than treated as infallible. A claimed prophetic word, from yourself or from anyone else, should be weighed, not obeyed automatically, and no leader should use “God told me” to control another person’s decision. Conflicting impressions are not necessarily a puzzle to be decoded; if you find yourself collecting signs in both directions, that is a reliable indication that the signs are not deciding the matter. A major decision should not rest solely on a coincidence. And no spiritual impression overrides Scripture, wisdom, safety or a legitimate responsibility you already have.

### Four Faithful Responses

When you are facing a decision and your mind will not settle, it can help to know that there are, broadly, four faithful things you can do. Lesson 2 named them briefly. Here is what each of them involves, and we will return to them later in the course.

Act. Sometimes you already have what you need: the moral question is clear or absent, wise counsel has been given, the circumstances permit, and you know what you want. The faithful response is to decide and act, trusting God with the outcome.

Wait. Sometimes there is a genuine reason to wait: information you do not yet have and can obtain, a circumstance that is about to change, a person you have not yet consulted and should, or a decision that need not be made yet. Waiting is faithful when it has a reason and an end. It is not faithful when it is simply the avoidance of deciding.

Seek help. Sometimes what you need is expertise you do not have. A financial decision may need an adviser; a medical one, a doctor; a legal one, a lawyer; a relational one, a counsellor; a spiritual one, a mature Christian who knows you well. Seeking help is bounded: you ask the right person the right question and you use the answer. One expert may identify a further question that needs another adviser, and that is not obsession; it is the ordinary shape of a complex decision.

Surrender. Sometimes the decision is not yours, or the outcome is beyond your control, or you have done all that can be done and the rest belongs to God. Surrender is not resignation. It is the deliberate handing over of what you cannot carry.

Obsessive discernment usually pretends to be waiting or seeking help, while actually avoiding action and refusing surrender. The question that follows is designed to expose that.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is one question, asked honestly: What information is genuinely missing, and what courage will still be required after I have it?

Begin by noticing. When you find yourself asking the same question again, of God or of people, name it: I am seeking confirmation. Then ask what you are really seeking. If it is information you do not have, that is discernment, and you can go and get it. If it is certainty, that is something else, and no amount of asking will produce it.

Then take stock of the ordinary means. Does Scripture forbid or command this? What does wisdom suggest, considering the practicalities honestly? What have trusted, mature people advised? Seek a small, appropriate circle rather than canvassing people until someone gives the answer you want. Different questions may require different advisers: a pastor cannot replace a doctor, lawyer or financial professional. But once a suitable person has given a careful answer, write it down and use it; asking the same question of yet another person in the hope of a different answer is the reassurance-seeking we saw in Lesson 9. What do the circumstances actually permit? And what do you want? Desire is relevant information, not divine authorisation. Our desires can reflect gifts and healthy longing, but they can also be mixed, fearful or self-serving, and they should be considered alongside character, responsibility, wisdom and consequences. The overthinker very often discounts desire entirely, which is its own kind of error.

Then ask the question, and be honest about which of three answers fits. I need specific information. Name exactly what is missing, where it can be found, and by when; then get it. I have sufficient information and need courage. You are done discerning; what remains is to decide, and deciding is an act of faith, not the absence of one. I need both. Get the information, and recognise that when you have it, the courage will still be required, so that you do not mistake the next piece of information for the last.

Finally, bring the decision to God as something you are making with him, not something you are waiting for him to make for you. Father, I have asked for wisdom and I trust that you are giving it. This is what I intend to do. If it proves unwise, give me humility to recognise it, to take responsibility for what follows, and to respond faithfully.

> **A note on major, irreversible decisions**
>
> The courage this chapter commends is not recklessness. A large or irreversible decision deserves the ordinary means used carefully, and, as Lesson 5 said, is best not made in a period of acute exhaustion or illness. If the decision involves significant money, health, legal consequences or another person’s safety, seeking qualified help is not obsession; it is wisdom. What this chapter questions is not careful preparation but the endless repetition of preparation already done.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that fit your situation and leave aside any that do not.*

Father, you give wisdom generously and without reproach. You are not a cruel examiner, and you are not waiting to see how long I will keep asking before you answer.

I confess that I have sometimes asked for certainty when you offered wisdom, and called my fear of deciding by holier names. I have asked the same question again and again, not because I lacked an answer but because I lacked the courage to act on what I had been given.

Give me the faith to trust what you provide: the counsel of wise people, the light of your Word, the honest desires of my heart weighed with care, and the ordinary sense you have placed in me. Where I genuinely need more, show me what and where. Where I need courage, give me that instead.

And when I decide, go with me. If my decision proves unwise, give me humility to recognise it, take responsibility for its consequences and respond faithfully to what follows. I am not afraid of that, because you are faithful. In Jesus’ name. Amen.

## Closing declaration

God gives wisdom generously and is not a cruel examiner. I will use the means he has given, his Word, wise counsel, honest thought and my own desires weighed with care, and then I will decide. I do not need certainty to act in faith. If the decision proves unwise, God will remain faithful, and I can ask for the humility to recognise it, take responsibility and respond wisely. My future rests on his faithfulness, not on the precision of my choices.
