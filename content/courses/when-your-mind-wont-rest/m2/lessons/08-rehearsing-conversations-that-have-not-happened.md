# Lesson 8: Rehearsing Conversations That Have Not Happened

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to separate what a person has actually said from what you have imagined, prepare only your own part and, when a conversation is safe and appropriate, enter it intending to hear.

## Before you begin

Two things before the teaching. For some people rehearsing is not overthinking but a needed support: if you are autistic or find unscripted conversation difficult, preparing what you will say and considering likely responses is a legitimate practice, and nothing here discourages it. And if the person has been abusive, coercive or unsafe, do not use this lesson or its worksheet to prepare direct contact. Rehearsing may be part of keeping yourself safe, so the priority is not to stop rehearsing but to seek guidance first from a specialist domestic-abuse service, a safeguarding professional or another appropriately qualified professional. Involve a pastor only when that person understands safeguarding, respects the limits of pastoral involvement and is safe for you to approach. The responsible decision may be not to have the conversation at all. If you rehearse most conversations, including ordinary ones, with a dread that makes daily life difficult, this can occur with social anxiety or other forms of distress; a qualified mental-health professional can assess what is happening and discuss appropriate support.

## Key Scripture: Proverbs 18:13 (ESV)

> If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame.

In its own setting the proverb describes the interrupter, composing a reply while you are still speaking. This lesson applies it, and marks the application, to answering before the conversation has happened at all.

## One truth to carry with you

The conversations in your head are not evidence of what anyone will say. When you rehearse, you write both sides. Because you wrote the other person's lines, the scene is not reliable evidence of what they will say this time. It may draw on things you genuinely know about them, but it also contains what you fear, hope or expect. You cannot be ready for everything they might say, because you do not know what they will say. What you can be ready for is your own part: to say what you mean, to listen to what they actually reply, and to answer that rather than what you feared. Being ready to hear is a different kind of readiness from being ready to win, and it is the kind the proverb commends. Prepare what belongs to you, and leave the other person's part to them and to God.

## The lesson

A woman I will call Hannah spent most of a fortnight preparing for a family lunch. The previous visit had ended with a remark from her mother-in-law about how Hannah's children were being raised, and Hannah had been composing her reply ever since. In the shower she explained calmly why the remark had hurt. By the second week she had rehearsed versions in which her mother-in-law wept, versions in which she stormed out, and one, late at night, in which Hannah said something so precise that the older woman was left with nothing to say. She had held the conversation forty times, and won it thirty-eight. On the day, her mother-in-law met her at the door, hugged her, and said, "I've been thinking about what I said last time. I'm sorry. It wasn't my place." Hannah noticed she had been cold to her mother-in-law for the first hour, responding to remarks that had never been made. "I had a whole argument with a person who wasn't there. And then the real person turned up and I treated her as if she'd said all of it."

Proverbs 18:13 describes the interrupter, the person composing a reply while you are still speaking. By analogy, and I mark it as an analogy rather than the writer's own subject, the proverb offers wisdom for rehearsed conversations too: we can form an answer before the other person has had the opportunity to speak. Hannah's fortnight brought unnecessary distress and affected how she treated the real person who arrived. The proverb does not condemn appropriate preparation; it warns against answering before hearing.

Rehearsal comes in several forms: the confrontation, in which the imagined person grows more unreasonable while we grow more eloquent; the defence, an answer to the criticism, then to the follow-up, then to the follow-up to that; the explanation, an apology or piece of bad news rehearsed for the perfect wording that never arrives, so the conversation is postponed; and the replay with edits, which belongs with Lesson 7.

Not all thinking ahead is rehearsal of this kind. Three things look alike. Preparation concerns what belongs to you: your facts, your purpose, your opening words, your questions, your boundaries. It has a natural end point, and it leaves you ready to listen. Bounded scenario planning considers a small number of realistic responses so you can stay clear, safe and flexible, and there are conversations where that is simply responsible: an interview, a disciplinary meeting, a safeguarding conversation, a talk with someone who commonly turns things around. Rumination-style rehearsal repeatedly scripts the other person, treats the imagined version as evidence, and continues long after useful preparation has ended. The distinction is not whether you imagine a response but whether the preparation stays bounded, evidence-based and flexible. Hannah's fortnight was none of those.

It is worth being honest about what prolonged rehearsal costs. It supplies evidence that does not exist; Hannah felt she knew how her mother-in-law would respond, and had in fact invented it. It fixes your interpretation before you have heard theirs, and it makes you arrive already in the middle. Most often, and least noticed, it replaces the conversation altogether: rehearsing an explanation for the perfect wording is a way of not giving it, and rehearsing a confrontation can discharge just enough feeling that you never raise the matter. And it costs the present: the shower, the drive, the hour before sleep given to an argument with someone not in the room.

"If I do not rehearse, I will be caught out." There is real wisdom in this; Scripture commends thinking before answering, and some conversations deserve careful preparation. The fault is in what the preparation becomes. Rightly done, it clarifies your part and holds the other person's part as an open question. Gently, there is also a matter of trust. Repeated rehearsal can function as though the outcome depended entirely on the precision of your wording. Your responsibility is faithfulness and clarity, not control of another person's response; outcomes rest on truth, on the relationship, on the other person's heart, and on God.

The practice is to notice: I am rehearsing. Ask what this person has actually said to you about this; often the honest answer is "one remark, a fortnight ago," and everything since has been you. Decide whether a real conversation is needed. If it is, choose an appropriate time and method rather than postponing in search of perfect wording; the wisest method may be a private conversation, a written message, a mediator or an appropriate process. Then prepare what belongs to you: for an ordinary relational conversation, two or three opening sentences and the one thing you most want understood may be enough. Consider, if it helps, a small number of realistic responses, marked as possibilities. When your part is prepared, the preparation is finished. Go in intending to hear: let the other person finish where it is safe to do so, and, when clarification would help, ask a genuine question before forming your response. And when the rehearsal returns, remind yourself: my part is prepared; their part is not mine to write. A last word about the apology that is never quite ready: the perfect wording does not exist. A responsible apology names the harm honestly, avoids excuses, respects the other person's boundaries and makes no demand for forgiveness. That deserves careful preparation. What it does not need is forty rehearsals of how it will be received.

A woman I will call Hannah spent most of a fortnight preparing for a conversation with her mother-in-law.

The occasion was an ordinary one: a family lunch. But the previous visit had ended with a remark about how Hannah’s children were being raised, and Hannah had been composing her reply ever since. In the shower, she explained calmly why the remark had been hurtful. On the drive to work, she anticipated the reply, which was defensive, and answered that too. By the second week she had rehearsed versions in which her mother-in-law wept, versions in which she stormed out, and one version, late at night, in which Hannah said something so precise and so devastating that the older woman was left with nothing to say. She had, in effect, held the conversation forty times, and won it thirty-eight.

On the day, her mother-in-law met her at the door, hugged her, and said, “I’ve been thinking about what I said last time. I’m sorry. It wasn’t my place.”

Hannah told me afterwards that she had felt almost cheated. She had arrived braced for a fight and been handed an apology, and for a moment she did not know what to do with it. Worse, she noticed that she had been cold to her mother-in-law for the first hour of the visit, responding to remarks that had never been made, before the two weeks of rehearsal finally wore off.

“I had a whole argument,” she said, “with a person who wasn’t there. And then the real person turned up and I treated her as if she’d said all of it.”

### Central Scripture: Proverbs 18:13

If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame. (Proverbs 18:13, ESV) The proverb, in its own setting, describes a familiar fault in real conversation: the person who interrupts, who has decided what you mean before you have finished, who is composing a reply while you are still speaking. The wisdom literature returns to this theme often, contrasting the fool who is quick to speak with the wise who are slow to answer and quick to hear.

I want to apply the proverb to something the writer was probably not describing, and I will mark it as an application rather than the text’s own point. The rehearsed conversation is a way of giving an answer before we have heard. It is the same fault, moved earlier in time. Hannah answered her mother-in-law’s defensiveness before her mother-in-law had been defensive. She answered remarks that were never made. She had, in the proverb’s language, given her answer before she heard, and the result was folly (a fortnight of wasted distress) and a kind of shame (an hour of coldness towards a woman who had come to apologise).

The proverb does not condemn preparation. It condemns answering before hearing. The distinction between those two things will run through the whole of this chapter.

### What Rehearsal Is

The pattern in Hannah’s story is one of the most common forms of overthinking, and one of the least often named. It is the mental rehearsal of a conversation that has not yet happened, in which you supply both sides.

It comes in several forms.

The confrontation. You have a grievance, and you rehearse raising it. You compose what you will say. You anticipate their reply, usually an unsatisfactory one, and you answer that. In anxious rehearsal, the imagined person often becomes increasingly unreasonable while we become increasingly eloquent. The real person may respond better, worse or simply differently; rehearsal cannot tell us which.

The defence. You anticipate criticism, and you rehearse your reply to it. The meeting with your manager, the conversation with the elder, the question your father will ask: you have prepared an answer, and then an answer to the follow-up, and then an answer to the follow-up to that.

The explanation. You need to say something difficult, an apology or a piece of bad news or a request, and you rehearse it for the perfect wording: the version that will land gently, cause no hurt, and leave no room for misunderstanding. You never quite find it, so the rehearsal continues and the conversation is postponed.

The replay with edits. Sometimes the conversation has happened, and what you rehearse is the version in which you said the right thing. This overlaps with the replaying we looked at in Lesson 7, and the Repair, Learn, Continue or Release pattern from that chapter applies to it. This chapter is chiefly about conversations that lie ahead.

In every form, the mind is doing the same thing. It is running a scene in which the other person’s lines are written by you. And because you wrote them, the scene is not reliable evidence of what they will say this time. It may draw on things you genuinely know about them, but it also contains what you fear, hope or expect.

### Three Things That Look Alike

Before going further, it is important to say that not all thinking ahead about a conversation is rehearsal of this kind. Three quite different activities can look the same from the outside, and the difference between them is the heart of this chapter.

Preparation concerns what belongs to you: your facts, your purpose, your opening words, your questions, your boundaries. It clarifies what you need to say. It uses established facts rather than imagined motives. It has a natural end point, and when it is done it leaves you ready to listen.

Bounded scenario planning considers a small number of realistic responses so that you can remain clear, safe and flexible. There are conversations where this is simply responsible: a job interview, a disciplinary meeting, a negotiation, a legal or financial discussion, a safeguarding conversation, a talk with someone who commonly changes the subject or turns things around. It is limited in time. It does not pretend to know what the other person will say. It leaves room for surprise. For autistic readers and for people who find unscripted conversation hard, this kind of planning is a legitimate communication support, and nothing here is meant to discourage it.

Rumination-style rehearsal repeatedly scripts the other person, treats the imagined version as evidence, and continues long after useful preparation has ended. It prepares you to defend or win rather than to listen. It often replaces the conversation instead of leading to it.

The important distinction, then, is not simply whether you imagine a response. It is whether the preparation remains bounded, evidence-based and flexible. Hannah’s fortnight was none of those things.

### What Rehearsal Costs

Rehearsing a conversation feels productive. It is worth being honest about what prolonged, adversarial rehearsal actually produces.

It supplies evidence that does not exist. By the end of the fortnight, Hannah felt that she knew how her mother-in-law would respond. She did not know it. She had invented it, and then treated the invention as information. We will look more closely at this in Lesson 9, where the subject is reading other people’s minds; rehearsal is one of the main ways we do it.

It fixes your interpretation before you have heard theirs. Once you have answered a defensive reply thirty times, you are primed to hear defensiveness even in an apology. The real conversation is filtered through the rehearsed one.

It makes you arrive already in the middle. Hannah reached the door with two weeks of accumulated argument behind her. Her mother-in-law reached the door with an apology. They were not at the same point, and Hannah’s coldness was the residue of a conversation that had never taken place.

It can replace the conversation altogether. This is the most common cost, and the least noticed. Rehearsing an explanation for the perfect wording is a way of not giving it. Rehearsing a confrontation can discharge just enough of the feeling that you never raise the matter, which leaves the grievance intact and the relationship unchanged. Some people have been rehearsing the same conversation, with the same person, for years, and the person has no idea.

It costs the present. The shower, the drive, the hour before sleep: these were given to an argument with someone who was not in the room, while the people who were in the room went unattended.

None of this makes rehearsal a sin. It is a habit of mind, and for many people it began as a reasonable attempt to protect themselves in situations where they felt unprepared or unsafe. But prolonged, adversarial rehearsal rarely provides the certainty it promises. Bounded preparation may help; repeated imaginary dialogue usually does not.

> **When rehearsal may be doing something else**
>
> For some readers, rehearsing conversations is not overthinking but a needed support. If you are autistic, or find unscripted conversation difficult for other reasons, preparing what you will say and considering likely responses is a legitimate and helpful practice. If you are preparing for a conversation with someone who has been abusive, coercive or unsafe, rehearsing may be part of keeping yourself safe; in that case the priority is not to stop rehearsing but to involve a qualified counsellor, a domestic-abuse service, a safeguarding professional, or a pastor who understands both safeguarding and the limits of pastoral involvement, and to consider with them whether the conversation should happen at all. And if you rehearse most conversations, including ordinary ones, with a level of dread that makes daily life difficult, this may be a feature of social anxiety, which responds well to professional help. The tools here can sit alongside such help, but they are not a substitute for it.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I do not rehearse, I will be caught out. I need to be ready for whatever they say.”

There is real wisdom buried in this belief. Scripture commends the person who thinks before answering, and there are conversations that deserve careful preparation: a difficult meeting, a word of correction, a proposal, a confession. The fault is not in preparing, nor even in considering how the other person might respond. It is in what the preparation becomes.

Preparation, rightly done, clarifies your own part and holds the other person’s part as an open question. You may consider a limited number of realistic possibilities, but you hold them as possibilities, not as knowledge. Rumination-style rehearsal does the opposite. It scripts the other person’s replies, tone and motives, treats the script as evidence, and then prepares your answers to a person who exists only in your head. And because you cannot actually know what they will say, this rehearsal is never finished. There is always another reply to anticipate. That is why it fills a fortnight.

The honest truth is that you cannot be ready for everything they might say, because you do not know what they will say. What you can be ready for is your own part: to say what you mean, to listen to what they actually reply, and to answer that, not what you feared. Being ready to hear is a different kind of readiness from being ready to win, and it is the kind the proverb commends.

There is also a matter of trust here, which I offer gently. Repeated rehearsal can begin to function as though the outcome depends entirely on the precision of our wording. For some people this reflects a fear of losing control; for others it may be a learned attempt to stay safe. Either way, our responsibility is faithfulness and clarity, not control of the other person’s response. Outcomes rest on truth, on the state of the relationship, on the other person’s heart, and on God. You can prepare your part faithfully and leave the rest, as Lesson 6 asked you to leave tomorrow, in hands more capable than your own.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is to separate what has actually been said from what you have imagined, and then to prepare what belongs to you without conducting the whole relationship with an imaginary version of the other person.

The first step, as in the previous two chapters, is to notice. When you find yourself mid-argument with someone who is not present, name it: I am rehearsing. Then ask a simple question: What has this person actually said to me about this? Often the honest answer is “one remark, a fortnight ago.” Everything since has been you.

The second step is to decide whether a real conversation is needed. Sometimes it is not. The remark was minor, the relationship is sound, and the rehearsal has magnified something that could be let go. Sometimes it is. The matter is real, it affects the relationship, and it will not resolve itself. If a conversation is needed, choose an appropriate time and method rather than postponing it merely in search of perfect wording. Wise preparation is not avoidance, and speed is not always wisdom: emotions may need to settle, facts may need gathering, a third person may be needed, or safety may need planning. Depending on the circumstances, the wisest method may be a private conversation, a written message, a meeting with a mediator, or communication through an appropriate organisational or safeguarding process.

The third step, if the conversation is needed, is to prepare what belongs to you. For an ordinary relational conversation, two or three opening sentences and the one thing you most want the other person to understand may be enough. More complex conversations may require notes, relevant facts, questions, boundaries or professional guidance. Prepare what the situation genuinely requires, and, if it helps, consider a small number of realistic ways the other person might respond so that you are not thrown. But do not write an entire imaginary exchange and treat it as information. When what belongs to you is prepared, the preparation is finished.

The fourth step is to go into the conversation intending to hear. This is harder than it sounds after a period of rehearsal, because the rehearsed replies will try to play over the top of the real ones. It helps to decide in advance that you will let the other person finish, that you will ask at least one question before you answer, and that you will respond to what they actually said.

And when the rehearsal returns, as it will, you may remind yourself: my part is prepared; their part is not mine to write. Then turn your attention to whatever is in front of you.

A word about the explanation that is never quite ready. If you have been rehearsing an apology or a difficult piece of news in search of the perfect wording, the perfect wording does not exist. Do not postpone a necessary apology indefinitely in search of it. At the same time, a responsible apology should name the harm honestly, avoid excuses, respect the other person’s boundaries, and make no demand for immediate forgiveness or reconciliation. That kind of apology deserves careful preparation; what it does not need is forty rehearsals of how it will be received. We will return to perfectionism in Lesson 11.
