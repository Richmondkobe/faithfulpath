# Lesson 7: Replaying What You Cannot Change

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to tell replaying from repenting, and give a past event the response it actually needs: repair, learn, continue or release.

## Before you begin

This lesson is for regrets and mistakes, not for trauma. If the memory that keeps returning is of an accident, an assault, a death you witnessed, abuse or another overwhelming event, and it returns as vivid images, nightmares or a sense of reliving it, that may be a trauma response, best helped by a qualified mental-health professional; do not use this lesson's exercise on such a memory. If grief remains intensely distressing or affects your safety, speak with a doctor or bereavement professional. If you confess the same sin over and over, unable to accept forgiveness, or feel driven to confess things that may not be sins at all, that may be scrupulosity, an obsessive-compulsive pattern that responds well to professional help. Stop the exercise if writing produces intense distress, physical symptoms or a sense of reliving; do something grounding and ordinary, and speak with a professional before returning to the memory.

## Key Scripture: Philippians 3:13–14 (ESV)

> Brothers, I do not consider that I have made it my own. But one thing I do: forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus.

Paul has just counted his former credentials as loss for the sake of Christ. "What lies behind" is everything in the course already run, not a memory to be erased.

## One truth to carry with you

What lies behind you has been acknowledged. Paul's "forgetting" was not amnesia; he remembered persecuting the church and said so. He simply did not live there. He refused to let the past, good or bad, be the place from which he drew his sense of who he was. If you have named what happened, confessed what was sin, repaired what could be repaired and received forgiveness, then what remains at night is not repentance. Forgiveness is not earned by confessing the same words a sufficient number of times; 1 John 1:9 says God is faithful and just to forgive, not that he forgives eventually. You do not need to punish yourself to add to what Christ has done. You can remember truthfully without making the past your home.

## The lesson

Five years ago a man I will call Thomas persuaded his wife to put most of their savings into a friend's business. Eighteen months later the business failed and the money was gone. Thomas apologised for the pressure his confidence had placed on his wife; she forgave him within the year, told him so more than once, and meant it. By every visible measure the matter was closed. But most nights, between switching off the light and falling asleep, Thomas went back to the kitchen table where he had made the case. Some nights he ran the scene a dozen times, and each time it ended the same way, because it had already happened. "I have confessed it to God more times than I can count," he told me. "And I still cannot stop going back there."

It matters what Paul is "forgetting" in Philippians 3. Paul had a past worse than a wasted investment. He had persecuted the church, and he refers to it elsewhere without flinching. So whatever forgetting means, it does not mean he had no memory or pretended nothing happened. He remembered. He simply did not live there. As a pastoral inference, Paul's forgetting is a refusal to let the past, good or bad, be the place from which he draws his sense of who he is. That is the posture this lesson is after: not "pretend it did not happen," but "it happened, it has been dealt with as far as it can be, and I am not required to keep living in it."

Thomas's pattern is usually called rumination: the repetitive replaying of a past event, with regret, guilt or shame, without arriving anywhere new. What makes it hard for Christians to recognise is that it feels virtuous. It feels like taking responsibility, like humility, above all like repentance. But repentance moves toward truth and changed action: it names wrongdoing without minimising it, receives God's forgiveness, makes appropriate repair and begins living differently. Rumination returns to the scene without producing further truth, repair or change; it keeps demanding suffering after suffering has ceased to serve anyone. It is usually trying to do one of four things it never announces. To undo: by lingering just before the decisive moment, the mind reaches for a version in which the mistake was not made, and fails every time. To punish: if I cannot undo it, I can at least suffer for it, which quietly denies that the punishment for sin has been borne by Christ, once for all. To prevent: keep the memory vivid and I will never do it again, though learning does not require endless reliving. To understand: legitimate, until nothing new is found. And notice that not everything we replay involves sin. Thomas's failing was at most overconfidence; the unsuccessful outcome was not a sin, and his wife did not regard it as one. Repentance is for sin. Regret is a different thing, and it asks for a different response.

"If I stop replaying it, I am letting myself off the hook. Feeling bad is the least I can do." This belief rests on an unexamined assumption that continued suffering is a payment. Stated plainly, that implies the forgiveness you received was not sufficient and needed topping up, and that Christ's work was not enough to keep you humble without your self-inflicted misery. Rumination does not say it aloud. It simply keeps you at the kitchen table. And self-punishment does not, in my experience, make people more careful or more loving. Thomas's wife did not benefit from his sleepless nights; she had asked him to stop apologising. Letting yourself off the hook would mean pretending it did not happen or refusing repair. Accepting that it has been dealt with is taking God at his word. Two qualifications: forgiveness does not automatically restore trust or remove consequences, and follow-through may be long; and some people move on too quickly. This lesson is chiefly for those whose responsibility has been faced but whose minds keep imposing a punishment that produces no further repair.

When a past event keeps returning, it is asking for one or more of four things. Repair: is there a safe and appropriate responsibility that remains, an apology never made, restitution within your means, a conversation avoided? Where repair has been made and accepted, further attempts are not repair; they are rumination in disguise, and a burden to the other person. Repair does not always mean direct contact: never contact someone who has asked for no contact, bypass a safeguarding or legal process, or use an apology to obtain relief for yourself. Learn: what truth or changed practice should I carry forward? Write it in a sentence, so your mind can be shown the sentence instead of the scene. Continue: is there an ongoing commitment, repaying money, respecting a boundary, rebuilding trust, keeping a changed behaviour? These are not rumination; they are the long, forward-facing part of repentance, done in the present. Release: what cannot be altered or usefully carried belongs here, handed deliberately to God. For a sin, release trusts that confession has been heard. For a loss or an honest mistake, it accepts that you did not know what you could not know, and that God's providence is larger than your errors.

The practice is to notice the replay and name it: I am replaying. Ask what it is reaching for; often the answer is "to undo," and undoing is not available. Work through Repair, Learn, Continue or Release once, carefully, and keep the page. The next time the scene begins, remind yourself: repair is made, the lesson is written, my commitments are being kept, the rest is released. Then turn to the present. Do not run the exercise again, and do not assume the return of the feeling means God requires another confession. If the replay keeps intruding night after night despite this, that is not failure at release; it is information, and it may be time to talk to someone. And pray about the past as something already dealt with, not still owed: Father, you know what I did. I have confessed it. I have done what I could, and I am keeping the commitments that remain. I trust that you have forgiven me, and I ask you to help me live forward.

Five years ago, a man I will call Thomas persuaded his wife to put most of their savings into a friend’s business. He had prayed about it. He had done what he believed was due diligence. The friend was a Christian, the plan was sound on paper, and Thomas was confident. Eighteen months later the business failed, the friend moved abroad, and the money was gone.

Thomas had apologised for the pressure his confidence had placed on his wife, and they had talked honestly about how future financial decisions would be made. She forgave him within the year, told him so more than once, and meant it. Their marriage recovered. They rebuilt a modest cushion of savings. By every visible measure, the matter was closed.

But most nights, somewhere between switching off the light and falling asleep, Thomas went back to the kitchen table where he had made the case to his wife. He replayed the conversation. He heard himself say the things he had said. He watched her trust him. Then he went back further, to the coffee with the friend, and found the moment when he should have asked a harder question. Then forward again, to the kitchen table. Some nights he ran the scene a dozen times. Each time it ended the same way, because it had already happened.

“I have confessed it to God more times than I can count,” he told me. “I have apologised to her more times than she wants to hear. And I still cannot stop going back there.”

He had continued confessing the failed investment to God as though an unsuccessful decision were necessarily a sin. But Thomas was not, by that point, engaged in useful reflection or present responsibility. He had acknowledged his part, made the changes available to him and received his wife’s forgiveness. What remained was repeated self-accusation over an outcome he could not reverse, and he did not have a name for it.

### Central Scripture: Philippians 3:13–14

Brothers, I do not consider that I have made it my own. But one thing I do: forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus. (Philippians 3:13–14, ESV) It matters what Paul is “forgetting” here, because this verse is often used more loosely than its context allows.

In the preceding verses Paul has listed his credentials: his lineage, his zeal, his blameless observance of the law. These are the things he now counts as loss for the sake of Christ. In context, “what lies behind” includes Paul’s former grounds for confidence and everything in the course already run; the picture is of a runner who does not look back at the track behind him. His emphasis is not on erasing memory but on refusing to treat past status or attainment as his present foundation.

Paul also had a past that was worse than a wasted investment. He had persecuted the church. He refers to it elsewhere without flinching, calling himself the foremost of sinners. So whatever “forgetting” means, it does not mean that Paul had no memory of what he had done, or that he pretended it had not happened. He remembered. He simply did not live there.

As a pastoral inference, then, I would put it this way. Paul’s forgetting is not amnesia. It is a refusal to let the past, good or bad, be the place from which he draws his sense of who he is. His eyes are forward, on the call of God in Christ, and the past has been placed where it belongs: acknowledged, and left behind.

That is the posture this chapter is trying to help you find. It is not “pretend it did not happen.” It is “it happened, it has been dealt with as far as it can be, and I am not required to keep living in it.”

### Replaying Is Not the Same as Repenting

The pattern in Thomas’s story has a name in counselling literature. It is usually called rumination: the repetitive replaying of a past event, decision or conversation, typically accompanied by regret, guilt or shame, and typically without arriving anywhere new.

What makes rumination hard to recognise, especially for Christians, is that it can feel like something virtuous. It feels like taking responsibility. It feels like humility. It feels, above all, like repentance. But rumination and repentance are different activities, and it helps to set them side by side.

Repentance moves toward truth and changed action. It names wrongdoing without minimising it, receives God’s forgiveness, makes appropriate repair and begins living differently. A particular wrong can be named and confessed, necessary repair can be undertaken, and a changed course can begin. That does not mean growth is instantly finished; consequences may continue, trust may require time and growth may remain unfinished. But it does mean that forgiveness is not earned by confessing the same words a sufficient number of times. First John 1:9 does not say that if we confess our sins often enough he will eventually forgive us. It says that he is faithful and just to forgive.

Rumination repeatedly returns to the scene without producing further truth, repair or change. It may feel responsible, but it keeps demanding suffering after suffering has ceased to serve repentance or the person harmed. It circles back to the same sentence, the same moment before the mistake, and it does not move on because moving on is not what it is for.

Here are some of the things rumination often seems to be trying to do, though it does not usually announce them.

Undoing. By going back to the moment before the mistake, the mind seems to be reaching for a version of events in which it was not made. This is why the replay so often lingers just before the decisive point. The mind is trying to rewrite history, and every time it fails.

Punishing. If I cannot undo it, I can at least suffer for it. The replay becomes a private penance, and the discomfort feels deserved. But this quietly denies something central to the gospel: that the punishment for sin has been borne by Christ, once for all, and repeated self-punishment cannot add to his work.

Preventing. If I keep the memory vivid enough, I will never do it again. There is a grain of wisdom here, and we will honour it in the exercise. Learning does not require endless reliving. A lesson may need to be revisited and practised, but repeatedly replaying the painful scene is not the only way to remember it.

Understanding. Some replaying is a genuine attempt to make sense of what happened. Again, that is legitimate. But sense-making can reach a point where nothing new is being found. When you find yourself running the same scene for the fortieth time in search of understanding you already have, the search has become the problem.

Notice also that not everything we replay involves sin. Some of the most persistent replaying attaches to decisions made in good faith that turned out badly, to accidents, to words spoken in ignorance, to losses that no one caused. Thomas’s failing was, at most, overconfidence and a lack of caution; the unsuccessful outcome itself was not a sin, and his wife did not regard it as one. If you have been treating an honest mistake, a reasonable decision with a bad outcome, or a simple tragedy as though it were a moral failure requiring endless confession, that is worth noticing. Repentance is for sin. Regret is a different thing, and it asks for a different response.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I stop replaying it, I am letting myself off the hook. Feeling bad about it is the least I can do.”

This belief is deeply felt and rarely examined, and it usually rests on an assumption that continued suffering is a kind of payment.

Consider what that assumption implies. If your ongoing distress is a payment, then the forgiveness you received was not sufficient; it needed topping up. If your replaying is what keeps you humble, then Christ’s work was not enough to keep you humble; your self- inflicted misery was required as well. When it is stated that plainly, most Christians would reject it. But rumination does not state it plainly. It simply keeps you at the kitchen table.

There is also a practical problem. Continued self-punishment does not, in my pastoral experience, make people more careful, more loving or more useful to the people they have hurt. Thomas’s wife did not benefit from his sleepless nights. She had asked him to stop apologising. What she wanted was a husband who was present, at rest, and able to plan the future with her, and his replaying was quietly denying her that.

Letting yourself off the hook would mean pretending the thing did not happen, refusing to acknowledge it, or declining to repair what you could repair. Accepting that it has been dealt with is not letting yourself off the hook. It is taking God at his word.

Two qualifications belong here. First, forgiveness does not automatically restore trust, remove consequences or complete restitution. Repair may involve a sustained change in behaviour over a long time. What should end is not responsible follow-through but the belief that repeated self-punishment adds something necessary to the repair.

Second, there are people whose difficulty runs the other way: they move on too quickly, minimise real harm, and would benefit from sitting longer with what they did. This chapter is primarily for readers whose responsibility has already been faced but whose minds continue imposing a punishment that produces no further repair. If you recognise yourself in the first description instead, the exercise below will still serve you, because its first question is about repair.

### Four Responses to the Past

When a past event keeps returning, there are, broadly, four things it may be asking of you. Very often it asks for more than one, and the exercise at the end of this chapter will help you work out which.

Repair. Is there a safe and appropriate responsibility that remains? An apology that was never made, or was made badly? Restitution that is within your means? A conversation that was avoided? Where repair is possible and has not been attempted, replaying often eases when repair is made. Where repair has already been made and accepted, further attempts at repair are usually not repair at all; they are rumination in disguise, and they can become a burden to the other person.

Repair does not always mean direct contact. Do not contact someone who has requested no contact, bypass a safeguarding or legal process, pressure a harmed person to respond, or use an apology to obtain relief for yourself. In complicated situations, seek appropriate advice before acting. Sometimes the responsible form of repair is restitution, changed behaviour or cooperation with an established process rather than a personal message.

Learn. What truth or changed practice should I carry forward? Almost always there is something, and it is worth writing down in a sentence or two. I will not put money into any venture without independent advice. I will not speak to my son when I am angry. A lesson that has been written down can be revisited and practised without re-deriving it every night from the painful scene. If your mind insists on running the scene again “so that you remember,” you may show it the sentence you wrote and decline to run the scene.

Continue. Is there an ongoing consequence or commitment I must faithfully maintain? Some situations require more than a single act of repair: repaying money over time, respecting another person’s boundaries, participating in accountability, rebuilding trust, accepting disciplinary or legal consequences, maintaining changed behaviour. These are not rumination. They are the long, quiet, forward-facing part of repentance, and they are done in the present, not in the replayed past. Confusing the two matters in both directions: some people replay endlessly while neglecting the ongoing commitment, and some people treat their continuing responsibilities as a reason to keep punishing themselves.

Release. What cannot be altered or usefully carried any longer belongs here. Release is not indifference. It is a deliberate act of handing to God what you cannot carry to any good purpose. For a sin, release means trusting that confession has been heard and forgiveness given. For a loss or an honest mistake, it means accepting that you are a creature, that you did not know what you could not know, and that God’s providence is larger than your errors.

Releasing the emotional burden may need to be practised repeatedly, not because God did not hear the first time, but because our hands close again. Confession may also be appropriate again if new truth comes to light or the behaviour continues. But compulsively repeating the same confession solely to obtain certainty or temporary relief can become an expression of scrupulosity rather than healthy repentance.

Many people who ruminate are stuck on one of these because they have been trying to do another. They are trying to release something that still needs repair, and it will not release. Or they are trying to repair something that has already been repaired, and the other person is tired of it. Or they are carrying a continuing responsibility as though it were an unpaid debt rather than a faithful commitment. Sorting the event into these responses can clarify the next step, although healing, restored trust or changed behaviour may require longer work.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is to notice the replay, name what it is trying to do, and give the past event the response it actually needs.

When you catch yourself back at the kitchen table, name it: I am replaying. As with prediction in Lesson 6, the naming does not stop the memory, but it changes what you do with it. You are no longer a spectator to your own past. You are a person noticing a mental habit.

Then ask, briefly, what the replay is reaching for. Is it trying to undo? To punish? To prevent? To understand? The honest answer is often “to undo,” and the honest reply to that is that undoing is not available, and God has not asked it of you.

Then, if you have not already done so, work through the Repair, Learn, Continue or Release exercise for that event, once and carefully. When you have, you will have something to return to. The next time the scene begins to play, you can remind yourself: repair has been made; the lesson is written; my continuing commitments are being kept; the rest has been released. Then deliberately turn your attention to the present. Do not run the exercise again. Do not assume that the return of the feeling means God requires another confession. If nothing new has occurred or come to light, remind yourself that forgiveness does not depend on achieving a particular feeling of relief. If the replay continues to intrude night after night despite this, that is not a sign that you have failed at release; it is information, and it may be time to talk to someone.

Finally, bring the memory to God as something already dealt with, not as something still owed. There is a way of praying about the past that is simply rumination addressed to God, and it leaves the person praying exactly where it found them. The better prayer is shorter: Father, you know what I did. I have confessed it. I have done what I could, and I am keeping the commitments that remain. I trust that you have forgiven me, and I ask you to help me live forward.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that fit your situation and leave aside any that do not.*

Father, you know the scenes I return to when the lights are off. You know them better than I do, and you knew them before they happened.

I confess that I have sometimes treated my regret as a payment and my sleeplessness as a penance, as though your forgiveness were not enough and my suffering had to make up the difference. Forgive me for doubting the sufficiency of what Christ has done.

Where there is something I can still repair, show me, and give me the courage to do it wisely and the humility to seek advice where I need it. Where there is something I should learn, help me to carry it forward. Where there is a commitment I must keep, make me faithful in it. Where there is nothing left to do, help me to open my hands.

Teach me to remember without living there. Set my eyes on what lies ahead, on the upward call of God in Christ Jesus, and let the past be what it is: acknowledged, forgiven, and behind me.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
