# Lesson 9: Trying to Read Other People's Minds

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish observation from interpretation, consider other possible explanations, and choose whether to ask, wait or let go rather than acting on a conclusion that is not supported by adequate evidence.

## Before you begin

For some readers, close attention to what others might be thinking is a skill learned for good reason. If you have lived with someone whose moods were dangerous or unpredictable, reading the room quickly may have kept you safe; this lesson is not a criticism of you, and an appropriately qualified, trauma-informed professional can help you notice when this vigilance is responding to a present danger and when it is continuing in a situation that is now reasonably safe. If you are autistic or find others' cues hard to read, clear, direct communication may sometimes be more useful than relying on ambiguous social cues, where asking is safe and appropriate. If the fear of being judged or rejected is intense, constant and affects most of your relationships, this can occur with social anxiety or other forms of distress; a qualified mental-health professional can assess what is happening and discuss appropriate support. This lesson is not a rule that other people's behaviour must always be given a kind explanation: where there is a clear pattern of unkindness, contempt, control or deception, do not use the exercise to explain it away. And complete the worksheet during the day, once for one person and one situation; do not begin it in the middle of the night, and use only the Night-Time Thought Download then.

## Key Scripture: 1 Corinthians 13:7 (ESV)

> Love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.

Read with the verse before it: love "rejoices with the truth". "Believes all things" cannot mean accepting every claim uncritically or ignoring evidence of harm; it does challenge the reflex of suspicion.

## One truth to carry with you

You cannot know another person's heart as God does. From a short reply, a delayed one, a look, a seat taken elsewhere, the mind builds a complete account of someone's inner life and then treats the construction as knowledge. Fear can be a loud interpreter. It may lean towards judgement and rejection, sometimes because the evidence points that way, but sometimes because those are the outcomes you most dread; the task is to distinguish the two. Love, Paul says, rejoices with the truth: it does not turn uncertainty into an adverse verdict without adequate evidence, and it does not explain away a real pattern of harm either. Where the evidence is incomplete, the honest position is "I do not know." That is not weakness. It is where love does its work, and it is the place from which you can ask, wait, or let it go.

## The lesson

A man I will call Mark had a close friend from his church home group, someone he had prayed with weekly for six years. One spring the friend's replies became shorter, then slower, then, for three weeks, stopped. Mark went back through their last conversations looking for the offence. He remembered that the friend had grown close to another man in the group and concluded he had been replaced. By the end of the third week he had assembled, from silence, a complete account of what his friend thought of him. He stopped messaging. Two months later he learned that his friend's father had been diagnosed with a serious illness that spring, that the friend had spent the season driving between hospital and his parents' home, and that he had told almost no one because he could not face talking about it. He had noticed Mark's withdrawal and assumed Mark had grown tired of him. Two men who loved each other spent a whole season apart, each certain he knew what the other was thinking, and each wrong.

First Corinthians 13:7 needs careful handling. Immediately before it Paul says love "does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth," so "believes all things" cannot mean accepting every claim as true, ignoring evidence of wrongdoing, remaining in danger, believing promises without changed behaviour or withholding a report of abuse. What the phrase does challenge is the reflex of suspicion. Love does not rush to convict where the evidence is incomplete. Applied to Mark, the lesson is not that he had to invent a positive story about his friend. It is that he should not have treated a painful negative story as established fact.

Mark's habit is sometimes called mind-reading: assuming you know what another person thinks, feels or intends, without adequate evidence, and responding to the assumption as fact. Several things make it hard to see. It can lean one way, towards judgement or rejection, sometimes because the evidence points that way and sometimes because those are the outcomes most dreaded. It fills silence, which is the easiest material to build with. It changes our behaviour, and this is the costly part: Mark's assumption led him to withdraw, and his withdrawal became evidence to his friend of the very thing Mark feared. A mind-reading error, acted upon, can produce the outcome it predicted. And it feels like insight. But being right sometimes is not knowing.

The way out is not to stop noticing but to be clear about what kind of thing you have noticed. Four levels. Observation is what was said, done or recorded, without an unverified motive: he has not replied to my last four messages. The moment a word like "ignored," "snubbed" or "dismissive" enters the sentence, you have moved to the next level. Interpretation is what you think the behaviour may mean: he is avoiding me. Not necessarily wrong, but not an observation, and the mind-reading habit consists precisely in forgetting the difference. Pattern is what emerges when observations repeat. A single short reply supports many interpretations; six months of short replies, missed commitments and coldness is a different kind of evidence, and a sustained pattern of contempt, deception or control is not something charity asks you to keep explaining away. Knowledge is what has been established through evidence, disclosure or reliable confirmation; only God has complete access to a heart. Beside these sits one more category: other possible explanations, the accounts that would fit the same observations equally well. He is overwhelmed by something I do not know about. She took the nearest seat. The point of generating them is not to prove the kind one true but to show yourself that the unkind one is not the only one, and therefore that you do not know. Mark had one observation, three weeks of silence, and never generated a single alternative. The true one, which was in no way about him, never occurred to him.

"I am not assuming. I am discerning. I can tell what people think of me." Perceptiveness is a real gift, but discernment and mind-reading feel identical from inside and behave very differently. Discernment notices and holds what it noticed tentatively; it tests its impression, is willing to ask, and is willing to be wrong, because it wants truth rather than confirmation. Mind-reading notices, concludes and closes; it does not test, rarely asks, and is reluctant to be wrong, because the conclusion has usually already been acted on. The test is whether you can state the evidence, remain open to correction and revise when new information appears.

The practice is to notice and name it: I am reading a mind. Sort: what have I observed, what have I interpreted, is there a pattern or a single event, what else could explain it? Generate two or three plausible alternatives, then stop; if this becomes a search for the perfectly reassuring explanation, return to "I do not know." Then choose one of three responses. Ask, where the relationship is safe and the matter is worth it: I noticed you have been quiet lately; is everything all right? One compassionate message might have interrupted Mark's whole season. Treat the answer as new evidence alongside the person's actions and any established pattern, and do not keep asking the same question solely to obtain greater certainty or temporary reassurance. Wait, when asking is not yet appropriate, behaving meanwhile with ordinary respect, neither punishing the person for an unverified interpretation nor ignoring established evidence. Let it go, when the honest conclusion is that you do not know, it does not much matter, and you have been giving a great deal of attention to a story with one observation in it. And remember the other side: this lesson is about stories built from too little evidence. Where the observations are not silent, where there is repeated unkindness, broken promises, contempt or control, the right response is not a gentler story but to take the pattern seriously, set boundaries and, where there is harm or danger, seek help. Charity concerns the gaps in the evidence. It is not a substitute for it.

A man I will call Mark had a close friend from his church home group, someone he had prayed with weekly for six years. One spring the friend’s replies to Mark’s messages became shorter. Then slower. Then, for about three weeks, they stopped altogether.

Mark did what many of us would do. He went back through the last few conversations looking for the offence. He found a joke he had made that could, if you squinted, have been taken the wrong way. He found a Sunday when he had left quickly without saying goodbye. He remembered that the friend had recently become close to another man in the group, and concluded that he had been quietly replaced. By the end of the third week he had assembled, from silence, a complete account of what his friend thought of him: that he was too intense, that the friendship had been a duty rather than a pleasure, and that the friend was relieved to let it fade. Mark stopped messaging. When they passed at church he was polite and brief, so as not to impose.

Two months later he learned, from someone else, that his friend’s father had been diagnosed with a serious illness in the spring, that the friend had spent most of that period driving between the hospital and his parents’ home, and that he had told almost no one because he could not face talking about it. He had noticed Mark’s withdrawal and assumed, in turn, that Mark had grown tired of him.

Two men who loved each other had spent a whole season apart, each certain that he knew what the other was thinking, and each wrong.

### Central Scripture: 1 Corinthians 13:7

Love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. (1 Corinthians 13:7, ESV) This verse needs careful handling, because it has been used to teach things Paul did not mean.

First Corinthians 13 stands within Paul’s correction of a divided, status-conscious church and, more immediately, within his teaching about spiritual gifts. Love is the indispensable way in which Christian gifts and relationships must operate. Immediately before this verse he has said that love “is not irritable or resentful; it does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth.” That last phrase matters. “Love believes all things” cannot mean that Christian love accepts every statement uncritically. Since love rejoices with the truth rather than with wrongdoing, Paul cannot be requiring believers to deny evidence, conceal abuse or remain vulnerable to repeated deception.

In context, then, “believes all things” cannot mean accepting every claim as true or ignoring evidence of wrongdoing. It may describe love’s enduring readiness to trust, its refusal to become reflexively cynical, or its willingness not to rush toward the worst interpretation where the truth remains unknown. The phrase does, however, challenge the reflex of suspicion. Love does not rush to convict another person where the evidence is incomplete. It remains open to truth, including unwelcome truth, and refuses both cynicism and naïveté.

What it does not mean is equally important, and I want to state it plainly because this chapter is about interpreting other people.

“Love believes all things” does not require blind trust. It does not require you to ignore evidence, to remain in danger, to believe repeated promises without changed behaviour, to withhold a report of abuse, or to assume good intentions from someone who has demonstrated harm. Jesus, who loved perfectly, “did not entrust himself” to people whose hearts he knew, and he told his disciples to be as wise as serpents.

For the purposes of this chapter, the relevant pastoral application is modest: love does not turn uncertainty into an adverse verdict without adequate evidence. Applied to Mark, the lesson is not that he had to invent a positive story about his friend. It is that he should not have treated a painful negative story as established fact.

### What Mind-Reading Is

The pattern in Mark’s story is sometimes called mind-reading: the habit of assuming you know what another person is thinking, feeling or intending, without adequate evidence, and then responding to the assumption as though it were fact.

It is closely related to the rehearsal we looked at in Lesson 8. There, we scripted what people would say; here, we script what they think. And like rehearsal, it usually works from very little. A short reply. A delayed one. A look. A tone. A seat taken elsewhere. Someone not laughing at the joke. From material of this kind the mind constructs an account of the other person’s inner life and then treats the construction as knowledge.

Several things make the habit hard to see.

It tends to lean one way. In anxiety-driven mind-reading, interpretations often lean toward judgement, rejection or danger, because those are the outcomes the person fears. This is not because those interpretations are more likely. It is because fear is a loud interpreter.

It fills silence. Mark’s friend said nothing, and nothing is the easiest material to build with. When people do not tell us what they think, we tell ourselves on their behalf, and the story we tell is rarely about their father being ill.

It may draw on our own inner life. Sometimes our interpretation reveals as much about our own fears, expectations or previous experiences as it does about the other person. That possibility is worth considering without assuming it explains every situation.

It changes our behaviour. This is the most costly part. Mark’s assumption that he had been replaced led him to withdraw, and his withdrawal became evidence to his friend of exactly the thing Mark had feared in himself. A mind-reading error, acted upon, can produce the very outcome it predicted.

It feels like insight. People who mind-read a great deal often think of themselves as perceptive, and they are sometimes right; we will come to that. But being right sometimes is not the same as knowing, and the confident feeling of having understood someone is not a reliable guide to having done so.

### Observation, Interpretation, Pattern and Knowledge

The way out of mind-reading is not to stop noticing. It is to be clear about what kind of thing you have noticed. It helps to distinguish four levels.

Observation is what was said, done or directly recorded. He has not replied to my last four messages. She did not sit with me on Sunday. My manager’s email was two lines long. Facts of this kind are observable events described without assigning an unverified motive. Where memory is uncertain, say so. Agreement from the other person is not required for an event to be factual; a defensive or deceptive person may dispute an accurate description. But the moment a word like “ignored,” “snubbed” or “dismissive” enters the sentence, you have moved to the next level.

Interpretation is what you think the behaviour may mean. He is avoiding me. She is upset with me. My manager is annoyed. An interpretation is not necessarily wrong. But it is not an observation, and the mind-reading habit consists precisely in forgetting the difference.

Pattern is what emerges when observations repeat. A single short reply supports many interpretations. Six months of short replies, missed commitments and coldness in person is a different kind of evidence. Patterns do not give you access to another person’s heart, but they strengthen a conclusion considerably, and a sustained pattern of contempt, deception or control is not something charity asks you to keep explaining away.

Knowledge is what has been established through evidence, disclosure or reliable confirmation. The other person may be able to clarify what they think or intend, although their answer must still be considered alongside their actions and any established pattern of behaviour. Direct communication can provide evidence; it does not guarantee complete access to another person’s heart. Only God has that.

Alongside these four levels sits one more category worth keeping in mind: other possible explanations. These are the accounts that would fit the same observations equally well. He is overwhelmed by something I do not know about. She was late and took the nearest seat. My manager was writing from a phone between meetings. The point of generating them is not to prove that a kind explanation is true. It is to demonstrate to yourself that the unkind one is not the only one, and therefore that you do not, in fact, know.

Mark had one observation, three weeks of silence, and a great many interpretations. He never generated a single alternative explanation, and the true one, which was in no way about him, never occurred to him.

Sorting in this way does not tell you what the other person thinks. What it does is return you to honest uncertainty where the evidence is incomplete, and to honest recognition where a pattern is clear. Both are where love, as Paul describes it, does its work.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“I am not assuming. I am discerning. I can tell what people think of me.”

Some people are unusually perceptive, and perceptiveness is a real gift. But it helps to be clear about the difference between discernment and mind-reading, because they feel identical from the inside and behave very differently.

Discernment notices, and then holds what it noticed tentatively. It says: something seems off; I wonder what it is. It tests its impression against further evidence. It is willing to ask. And it is willing to be wrong, because its aim is truth rather than confirmation.

Mind-reading notices, concludes, and closes. It says: something is off; I know what it is. It does not test, because it feels that it does not need to. It rarely asks, because asking would risk the conclusion. And it is very reluctant to be wrong, because the conclusion has usually already been acted upon.

The test is not how often you believe you are right. Ask instead whether you can state the evidence, distinguish observation from interpretation, remain open to correction, and revise your conclusion when new information appears. Discernment can do all four. Mind-reading usually cannot.

We will look more closely at discernment, and at what happens when it becomes its own kind of obsession, in Lesson 10.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is to sort, to ask where asking is wise, and to hold the rest in honest uncertainty.

The first step, as before, is to notice. When you find yourself certain of what someone thinks of you, name it: I am reading a mind. The naming is not a rebuke. It is a reminder that you are doing something you cannot actually do.

The second step is to sort. What have I observed? What have I interpreted? Is there a pattern, or a single event? What else could explain the same observations? Generate two or three plausible alternatives, then stop. They do not need to cover every possibility. If this becomes a search for the perfectly reassuring explanation, return to “I do not know” and move to the next step.

The third step is to decide what to do with the uncertainty, and here there are broadly three options.

Ask. Where the relationship is safe and the matter is worth it, the most direct way out of mind-reading is a plain question. I noticed you have been quiet lately; is everything all right? I wondered whether I said something last week that landed badly. A brief, non- accusatory question is a healthy request for clarification. Repeatedly asking after an answer has been given, however, may become reassurance-seeking and place pressure on the relationship. In Mark’s case, one compassionate message might have interrupted the cycle, or at least prevented him from treating his assumption as settled fact.

Wait. Sometimes asking is not yet appropriate, or the person is not available, or the matter is minor and time will show. While waiting, behave with ordinary respect, without either punishing the person for an unverified interpretation or ignoring established evidence. Charity does not require additional closeness, disclosure or trust; it requires only that you not act on a verdict you have not reached.

Let it go. Sometimes the honest conclusion is that you do not know, that it does not much matter, and that you have been giving a great deal of attention to a story with one observation in it.

And when the mind-reading returns, as it will, remind yourself: I have one observation and several possible explanations, and I have not been told which is true. Then turn your attention to what is in front of you.

> **When the evidence does point somewhere**
>
> This chapter is about the stories we build from too little evidence. It is not a rule that other people’s behaviour must always be given a kind explanation. If there is a clear pattern— repeated unkindness, broken promises, contempt, control, deception—then the observations are not silent, and love that rejoices in the truth does not pretend otherwise. In that case the right response is not to generate a gentler story but to take the pattern seriously, set appropriate boundaries and, where there is harm or danger, seek help from someone qualified. Charity concerns the gaps in the evidence. It is not a substitute for it.
