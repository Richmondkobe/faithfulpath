# Lesson 6: When Your Mind Predicts Disaster

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to name a prediction as a prediction, separate possibility, probability and present reality, and do what today actually requires.

## Before you begin

This lesson is for the chain of imagined consequences that runs from a single uncertain trigger. Two cautions come first. If you experience vivid, unwanted images of disaster that arrive suddenly, feel intensely real and lead you to check, avoid or seek reassurance repeatedly, these experiences can occur with anxiety conditions, obsessive-compulsive symptoms or the effects of trauma. A qualified mental-health professional can assess what is happening and advise you about appropriate support; this lesson cannot determine the cause. And if there is evidence of abuse, violence, stalking, coercion, a medical emergency or another immediate danger, do not use this lesson to persuade yourself the danger is probably imaginary; move to safety and contact appropriate help. Testing predictions is meant to improve judgement, not to silence legitimate warning signs. If your predictions include thoughts of ending your life or harming yourself, open Finding Help Where You Live and contact appropriate support today. If you may act on those thoughts, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact an emergency or crisis service now and involve a trusted person if you can. Opening the support page does not alert Faithful Path or contact help for you.

## Key Scripture: Matthew 6:34 (ESV)

> Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble.

The closing sentence of Matthew 6:25–34 in the Sermon on the Mount, which Lesson 17 returns to in full. Its concern is not thought management but the Father's care and the call to seek his kingdom rather than organise life around fear.

## One truth to carry with you

My mind can imagine many tomorrows, but I have been given only today. Jesus does not promise that tomorrow will be trouble-free; he says the opposite, and then he tells us that today has enough in it. What is possible is not the same as what the evidence supports, and what the evidence supports is not the same as what is happening now. You can acknowledge uncertainty without treating the worst outcome as established fact. At this moment, the only thing established may be that a meeting has been requested, or a result has not come back. That is where God's help can be received and faithful action taken. The rest of the chain has not been established by the information you currently have, and you are allowed to set it down.

## The lesson

A teacher I will call Esther received a short email from the head of her school at half past four on a Thursday: "Could you come and see me in my office at eight tomorrow morning? Thanks." By five she had decided she was being dismissed. By six a parent must have complained. By eight she had calculated how long her savings would last and which subjects she might tutor. She slept for about three hours. At eight the next morning the head asked whether she would take on an extra Year 10 class, because a colleague was going on maternity leave. The meeting lasted six minutes. "The worst part," Esther said, "is that I knew while I was doing it. I knew I had no evidence. I did it anyway." If you recognise that sentence, this lesson is for you.

Notice three things in Matthew 6:34. Jesus does not say that tomorrow will be trouble-free; he says tomorrow will have its own trouble, and so will today. "Sufficient for the day is its own trouble" is an honest description of life in a fallen world, spoken by the one person who knew exactly what tomorrow held for him. Second, as a pastoral application, rehearsing tomorrow's possible trouble makes us carry its imagined weight alongside the real responsibilities of today. Esther's Thursday contained no dismissal, no complaint and no house move; it contained only the imagined weight of all three. Third, Scripture repeatedly teaches daily dependence, manna gathered by the day and daily bread asked for. Trying to experience every possible future problem in advance leaves us depleted in the present.

Esther's pattern is often called catastrophising. It is not simply noticing that something bad could happen; wisdom includes recognising danger and preparing for foreseeable difficulty. Catastrophising is a movement: a trigger (an email, a symptom, a delayed reply), a leap to the most threatening interpretation within seconds, a chain in which each imagined consequence becomes the basis for the next, and a response in which the imagined chain begins producing real distress in the body. It is hard to see from inside because it is fast, so it feels like something you are seeing rather than doing, and because it is plausible: every link in Esther's chain was possible, and that is exactly what made it convincing.

The way out is to separate the questions that dread has collapsed into one. Ask four, in order. What am I predicting? Say it as a prediction, not a fact: I am predicting that I will be dismissed. Naming it lets it be examined rather than obeyed. What evidence supports or challenges it? Not what you imagine; what you actually know. A brief email tells you very little either way. For health, legal, financial or safety concerns, seek qualified advice rather than relying on this lesson. Sometimes the evidence does point towards the feared outcome. That is a legitimate result, and the fourth question will tell you what to do with it. What is actually happening right now? This is the question catastrophising most wants you to skip. For Esther on Thursday evening: I have received an email asking for a meeting. I do not know what it is about. That is all. What does the present require of me? Sometimes something: reply, book the appointment, ask a clarifying question. Often nothing. Esther's present required her to eat dinner, mark some books and go to the meeting. And when the evidence does point the bad way, this question converts dread into preparation, which is specific, bounded and can be completed. Dread is none of those things.

"If I imagine the worst, at least I will be ready for it." Many overthinkers half believe this. Three things in reply. In my pastoral experience it does not seem to work: people who rehearse disasters all night do not cope better when trouble comes; they arrive at it already exhausted. It is not preparation: genuine preparation is specific, bounded and completed, whereas catastrophising revisits the same scene without producing anything you could act on. If you have "prepared" for the same disaster forty times, you have not been preparing; you have been suffering. And it can lead us to live as though constant vigilance were our only protection. The invitation is not to condemn ourselves but to practise entrusting what we cannot control to the Father who knows what we need. Very often the motive is love. But love that has become dread is not serving anyone, least of all the people you are afraid for.

The practice is to slow the chain down enough to see it. Notice, and name it: I am predicting. Write the chain down, link by link, exactly as your mind produced it; written out, Esther could see that five of her six links rested on nothing she knew. Then ask the four questions of the first link only. If the first link does not hold, the rest of the chain has nothing to hang from. Ask them once, carefully, per prediction, and then deliberately turn to the meal, the task, the person in front of you. If the prediction returns an hour later, remind yourself briefly of your answers; do not run the whole exercise again. Running it repeatedly turns a tool for clarity into a ritual for reassurance. Finally, bring the present reality, not the imagined future, to God. A teacher I will call Esther received a short email from the head of her school at half past four on a Thursday afternoon. It read, in its entirety: “Esther, could you come and see me in my office at eight tomorrow morning? Thanks.”

By five o’clock she had decided she was being dismissed. By six she had worked out that a parent must have complained, and had narrowed the list of possible parents to two. By eight she had calculated how many months her savings would last, considered which subjects she might tutor privately, and wondered whether her husband’s salary could cover the school fees for their own children. Somewhere after ten she began to think about whether they would need to move house. She slept, by her estimate, for about three hours.

At eight the next morning, the head asked her whether she would be willing to take on an extra Year 10 class in the second term, because a colleague was going on maternity leave. The meeting lasted six minutes.

Esther told me this story a few weeks later, half laughing and half ashamed. “The worst part,” she said, “is that I knew while I was doing it. I knew I had no evidence. I did it anyway.”

If you recognise that sentence, this chapter is for you.

### Central Scripture: Matthew 6:34

Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble. (Matthew 6:34, ESV) This verse closes a longer passage in the Sermon on the Mount, Matthew 6:25–34, which we will return to in full in Lesson 17. The passage’s central concern is not merely efficient thought management. Jesus is directing his hearers toward their Father’s care and calling them to seek God’s kingdom rather than organise their lives around fear. With that larger purpose in view, I want to draw attention to three things in this single sentence.

First, notice what Jesus does not say. He does not say that tomorrow will be trouble-free. He says the opposite: tomorrow will have its own trouble, and so will today. “Sufficient for the day is its own trouble” is not a promise that nothing bad will happen. It is an honest description of life in a fallen world, spoken by the one person who knew exactly what tomorrow held for him.

Second, notice the shape of the instruction. As a pastoral application, we might say that rehearsing tomorrow’s possible trouble can make us carry its imagined weight alongside the real responsibilities of today. Esther’s Thursday evening contained no dismissal, no complaint and no house move. It contained only the imagined weight of all three, carried in advance, on a night that had troubles enough of its own.

Third, Scripture repeatedly teaches daily dependence: Israel gathered manna according to God’s instructions, and Jesus taught his disciples to ask for daily bread. As a pastoral inference, attempting to experience every possible future problem in advance can leave us depleted in the present. God calls us to trust him with a future we cannot yet see.

It is worth adding, as we said in Lesson 3, that “do not be anxious” is a genuine instruction, but it is not a condemnation of the involuntary lurch in the stomach when an unexpected email arrives. Esther’s first reaction came before she could evaluate it. The chain that followed was also difficult to interrupt, but with practice and support she could learn to notice it earlier, question its conclusions and redirect her attention.

### What Catastrophising Is

The pattern in Esther’s story has a name. Counsellors and psychologists generally call it catastrophising.

Catastrophising does not consist simply of noticing that something bad could happen. Wisdom includes recognising genuine danger and preparing for foreseeable difficulty. Catastrophising occurs when the mind moves rapidly from uncertainty to a threatening conclusion, builds additional consequences on that conclusion and begins responding as though the entire chain has already been established.

The goal of this chapter is not to convince you that everything will be fine. It is to give each conclusion no more confidence than the evidence allows, and then to respond faithfully to what is actually known.

Notice that catastrophising is a movement, not a single thought. It typically runs like this:

A trigger. Something uncertain arrives: an email, a symptom, a delayed reply, a change in someone’s tone, a bill, a news story.

A leap. The mind supplies an interpretation, usually the most threatening one available, and usually within seconds.

A chain. Each imagined consequence becomes the basis for the next. Dismissal leads to lost income, which leads to unpaid fees, which leads to moving house. By the fourth link the mind is several steps from anything that has happened, but it does not feel that way.

A response. The body reacts to the chain as if it were real. The heart rate rises, sleep departs, and the person begins making plans, or pleading with God, about a situation that does not exist.

Two things make this pattern especially hard to see from the inside. The first is speed. The whole chain can run in less time than it takes to read this paragraph, so it does not feel like a process you are doing; it feels like something you are seeing. The second is plausibility. Catastrophising does not usually imagine the impossible. Teachers are sometimes dismissed. Parents do sometimes complain. Every link in Esther’s chain was possible. That is exactly what made it convincing.

### Four Questions, Not One

The difficulty with catastrophising is that it collapses several quite different questions into a single feeling of dread. The way out is to separate them again.

When your mind is predicting disaster, there are four distinct questions worth asking, in order.

1. What am I predicting?

State it plainly, as a prediction rather than a fact. I am predicting that I will be dismissed. Almost any feared outcome is possible, which is why “Could this happen?” on its own tells you very little. The useful first step is simply to name the prediction as a prediction, so that it can be examined rather than obeyed.

2. What evidence supports or challenges it?

This is the question that does most of the work. Given what you actually know—not what you imagine—what genuinely points towards the feared outcome, and what points away from it?

Probability should be judged from relevant evidence, not from communication style alone. A polite email can still contain serious news, and a brief message may tell you very little either way. Ask what established facts, prior events or qualified advice genuinely indicate. You are not being asked to calculate a precise percentage. A frightened mind will readily call a remote outcome “almost certain,” which is why the question is about evidence rather than feeling. Ask what supports the prediction, what does not support it, and whether someone with relevant expertise could help you judge the situation. For health, legal, financial or safety concerns, seek qualified advice rather than relying only on this chapter.

I want to be careful here. Sometimes the evidence does point towards the bad outcome. This tool does not exist to talk you out of every fear; it exists to help you see which fears are proportionate to the evidence and which are not. If you work through it honestly and conclude that the feared outcome is likely, that is a legitimate result, and the fourth question will tell you what to do with it.

3. What is actually happening right now?

This is the question catastrophising most wants you to skip. Not “what might be happening” or “what will be happening tomorrow at eight,” but what is true at this moment, in this room. For Esther, on Thursday evening, the present reality was: I have received an email asking for a meeting. I do not know what it is about. That is all. Everything else was construction.

Present reality is often more specific than the imagined chain. Sometimes it is less serious; sometimes it is genuinely dangerous. Either way, identifying what is actually happening helps you choose an appropriate response. The present is the place where you can receive God’s help and take faithful action now. The future also belongs to him, but you do not yet know what it will require.

4. What does the present require of me?

The final question turns from analysis to responsibility. Given what is actually true, is there something I should do now? Sometimes there is: reply to the email, book the appointment, ask a clarifying question, make a sensible contingency. Often there is not. Esther’s present required her to eat dinner, mark some books, and go to the meeting in the morning. It did not require a tutoring plan.

When the honest answer to the second question was “the evidence does point this way,” this fourth question becomes the most important of all, because it converts dread into preparation. Preparation is specific, bounded and can be completed. Dread is none of those things. We will look at this distinction more closely in Lesson 17.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I imagine the worst, at least I will be ready for it. Expecting disaster is a kind of wisdom.”

Many overthinkers half believe this. Predicting catastrophe feels like prudence. If I have already lived through the dismissal in my mind, the reasoning goes, then the real thing will hurt less, and I will have a plan.

Three things are worth saying in reply.

First, it does not seem to work. People who spend the night rehearsing disasters do not, in my pastoral experience, cope better when trouble actually comes. They tend to arrive at it already exhausted.

Second, it is not preparation. Genuine preparation is specific, bounded and completed: you make the contingency plan, you write it down, you stop. Catastrophising is unbounded; it revisits the same imagined scene again and again without ever producing anything you could act on. If you have “prepared” for the same disaster forty times, you have not been preparing. You have been suffering.

Third, catastrophising can lead us to live functionally as though constant vigilance were our only protection. That does not necessarily reveal a deliberate rejection of God’s care; it may be a learned response, a symptom of distress or an attempt to protect what we love. The invitation is not to condemn ourselves but to practise entrusting what we cannot control to God, who has told us that our Father knows what we need.

None of this is said to shame you. For some people, catastrophising developed after painful or unpredictable experiences. For others, it appears without a single identifiable origin. You do not need to discover an origin story before learning a healthier response. And its motive is often love. But love that has become dread is not serving anyone, least of all the people you are afraid for.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is to slow the chain down enough to see it, and then to ask the four questions in order.

The first step is simply to notice. When you feel the lurch of dread and find your mind several links along a chain of consequences, name it: I am predicting. Not “I am seeing the future,” which is what it feels like, but “I am predicting,” which is what your mind is doing. This small act of naming does not stop the fear, but it changes your relationship to it. You are beginning to recognise a mental process rather than treating its conclusion as established fact. You may not be able to stop the thought immediately, but you can learn not to give every prediction further attention.

The second step is to write the chain down, link by link, exactly as your mind produced it. This feels foolish and is enormously useful, because writing the chain down often reveals how many steps depend on assumptions rather than evidence. Email → dismissal → parent complaint → savings gone → school fees → move house. Written out, Esther could see that five of the six links rested on nothing she actually knew.

The third step is to ask the four questions of the first link only. Not of the whole chain—of the first link. What am I predicting? Dismissal. What evidence supports or challenges it? A meeting has been requested; nothing has been said about its subject; there have been no warnings, complaints or prior concerns. What is actually true? A meeting has been requested. What does that require of me? Attend it.

You will notice that if the first link does not hold, the rest of the chain has nothing to hang from.

A word about how often to do this. Ask the four questions once, carefully, for each prediction, and then deliberately stop working on it and turn your attention elsewhere—to the meal, the task, the person in front of you. If the prediction returns an hour later, you may briefly remind yourself of your answers, but do not run the whole exercise again. Running it repeatedly turns a tool for clarity into a ritual for reassurance, and reassurance-seeking feeds the very cycle you are trying to interrupt. The aim is not to make the fear disappear but to stop feeding it.

Finally, bring the present reality, not the imagined future, to God. There is a kind of prayer that is really just catastrophising with “Lord” attached to the front of every sentence. We will look at that in Lesson 16. For now: tell him what is actually true, ask him for what today requires, and leave tomorrow where he has put it.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that fit your situation and leave aside any that do not.*

Father, you know what tomorrow holds, and I do not. You have told me that today has enough in it, and that you know what I need before I ask.

I confess that I have lived whole disasters that never happened. I have spent nights in houses I never had to leave, grieving losses that never came, and I have called it prudence.

Teach me to ask what is actually true before I ask what might be. Where a real trouble is coming, show me what today requires of me, and give me the courage to do it and the wisdom to stop there. Where the trouble is only imagined, help me to set it down. And where my mind runs ahead of me faster than I can catch it, be patient with me, and lead me to the help I need.

I do not ask you to promise me an easy tomorrow. I ask you to meet me in this day, and to hold the days I cannot yet see.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
