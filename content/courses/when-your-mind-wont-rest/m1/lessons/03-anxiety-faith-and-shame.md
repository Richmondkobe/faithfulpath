# Lesson 3: Anxiety, Faith and Shame

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish a feeling, a bodily reaction and an unwanted thought from a chosen response, and identify the sentences that may have turned anxiety into shame.

## Before you begin

In this lesson, anxiety is used broadly to describe experiences of worry, apprehension, fear and bodily alarm. A temporary feeling of anxiety is not the same as an anxiety disorder, and only an appropriately qualified professional can assess or diagnose a mental-health condition. The principles here may support someone receiving treatment; they do not replace it. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for one set of shaming beliefs; do not begin it in the middle of the night. If you return to it to check whether you understood your faith correctly, stop and use the support guidance rather than completing it again. Your standing with God does not depend on completing a worksheet correctly.

## Key Scripture: Psalm 56:3–4 (ESV)

> When I am afraid, I put my trust in you. In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust; I shall not be afraid. What can flesh do to me?

The superscription associates the psalm with the occasion when the Philistines seized David in Gath. If that is the setting, David's fear arose in the presence of genuine danger rather than from an imagined threat.

## One truth to carry with you

Fear is an experience. Trust is a direction. Psalm 56 does not say, "Because I trust you, I am never afraid." It says, "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." Fear comes first; trust is what David does with it, not proof that it has gone. You can feel afraid and still turn toward God in trust, and that tension runs all through the Psalms. Your feelings do not, by themselves, define your faith, and your body's alarm does not prove that God has rejected you. Christian conviction brings wrongdoing into the truth and calls us towards repentance, repair and God's mercy. Shaming self-condemnation turns struggle into a verdict on identity and drives us towards hiding. Romans 8:1 speaks first about the believer's standing before God: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (ESV).

## The lesson

A woman I will call Ruth waited until the end of a church meeting, when almost everyone had gone home, before she approached me. She had been a member for eleven years and led a home group. "Pastor, I need to ask you something, and I need you not to think less of me. I have been anxious for months. I know all the verses. I have cast my cares on him, over and over, and they keep coming back. So either the verses don't work, or I don't have real faith. And I don't know which is worse." Ruth had been carrying two burdens: anxiety, and shame about the anxiety. The shame had become a second burden, and it was isolating her from the very people and support that might have helped her carry the first. This lesson is for the second burden.

Read Psalm 56:3–4 again and notice the order. Verse 3 does not say, "Because I trust you, I am never afraid." It says, "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." Fear comes first. Trust is what David does with the fear, not proof that the fear has gone. Then verse 4 says, "I shall not be afraid." Is he contradicting himself? I do not think so. He is describing a movement: he begins afraid, turns toward God and arrives, for now, at a place where fear no longer has the last word. Then, if the rest of the psalm is any guide, he does it again, because the enemies have not gone anywhere. This psalm was received into Israel's songbook not as a confession of weak faith but as a model of what faith does when it is frightened. Fear and trust can be present in the same person at the same moment. That does not make fear spiritually unimportant; Scripture repeatedly calls God's people away from being ruled by fear. But that call should lead us toward God, not into shame. The question is not merely, "Do I feel afraid?" A more helpful question is, "What am I doing with the fear I feel?"

Over the years I have heard the same handful of sentences from anxious Christians. "If I trusted God, I would not feel this way." "A real Christian should not be anxious." "I should be able to pray this away." "Other people at church seem to have peace; what is wrong with me?" "The Bible says do not be anxious, so my anxiety is disobedience." Each sounds biblical, and some contain actual words of Scripture. But each draws a conclusion Scripture does not permit us to apply automatically to every experience of fear, and they share a common root: a confusion between four things that are not the same.

A feeling. Fear, dread and worry are emotional responses. They arise; you do not summon them, and you usually cannot dismiss them by deciding to. A physical reaction. A racing heart, tight chest, shallow breathing, sleeplessness. These are bodily events, triggered by a real threat, an imagined one, exhaustion, illness or, for some people, conditions that are medical in nature. Your body's response to stress is not a moral act. An unwanted thought. In the middle of fear, certain thoughts present themselves: God has abandoned you; nothing you do will matter. The arrival of a thought is not the same as agreement with it; Jesus was genuinely tempted, yet without sin. An unwanted thought does not, merely by appearing, reveal your character or your faith. A chosen response. Whether we seek truth, accept support, take a faithful step or deliberately surrender to a false conclusion. This is where trust and obedience become more directly relevant, though even here our freedom can be affected by exhaustion, trauma, illness or compulsive patterns, and God sees the whole person with a clarity and mercy we do not possess. When Ruth said, "Either the verses don't work or I don't have real faith," she was judging her faith by her pulse. But her faith did not live in her pulse. It lived in the fact that she was standing in front of her pastor, frightened and ashamed, still asking for help. That is what trust looks like in the middle of fear. It looks like David in Gath.

The sentence that troubles anxious Christians most is Paul's: "Do not be anxious about anything." Notice what he does immediately afterwards. He does not say, "So stop." He says, "But in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God." He assumes there are real needs, and he gives anxious people something to do with them. Paul's words are genuine instruction, but instruction is not condemnation. Scripture also says, "Rejoice always," and nobody concludes that a grieving Christian has sinned by feeling sorrow. These commands point us in a direction; they do not pronounce judgement on every emotion that has not yet followed. I am not saying anxiety is never connected to sin. Sometimes worry grows from a refusal to trust or an idol we are protecting. But that is something to discover honestly, in prayer and with wise counsel, not something to assume every time your heart races.

Where does the shame come from, if Scripture is more generous than it? From half-heard teaching that equated strong faith with calm. From comparison, in which your inside is measured against everyone else's outside and always loses. From the fear of being a burden. And, deepest of all, from treating anxiety as a verdict on the self, so that "I am feeling fear" becomes "I am the kind of person who fails at faith."

Some readers will resist this lesson because they fear that removing the shame will remove the motivation to grow. But shame is an unreliable and often harmful engine for growth. It can drive hiding, comparison and repeated self-accusation, and may intensify overthinking. Reducing shame does not remove the invitation to grow; it may make honest reflection, support and change more possible. The work of this lesson is not to stop feeling anxious. It is to stop adding a second layer of suffering on top of the first: to hear the shaming sentences as sentences again, ask where they came from, and test them against what God has actually said. "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." "Cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7, ESV). "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV).

A woman I will call Ruth waited until the end of a church meeting, when almost everyone had gone home, before she approached me. She had been a member for eleven years. She led a home group. She was, by any outward measure, a mature believer.

“Pastor, I need to ask you something, and I need you not to think less of me.”

I have cast them, over and over, and they keep coming back.” She stopped and looked at the floor. “So either the verses don’t work, or I don’t have real faith. And I don’t know which is worse.”

Ruth had been carrying two burdens. The first was anxiety. The second was shame about the anxiety. And the second burden was heavier than the first, because it had convinced her that she could not bring the first one to anyone—not to her group, not to her pastor, and, she suspected, not really to God.

This chapter is for the second burden.

### The Sentences That Create the Shame

Over the years I have heard the same handful of sentences from anxious Christians, in different words and different accents. You may recognise some of them.

“If I trusted God, I would not feel this way.”

“A real Christian should not be anxious.”

“I must be disappointing God.”

“I should be able to pray this away.”

“Other people at church seem to have peace. What is wrong with me?”

“The Bible says do not be anxious, so my anxiety is disobedience.”

Each sentence contains something that may sound biblical, and some contain actual words from Scripture. But each draws a conclusion that Scripture does not permit us to apply automatically to every experience of fear or anxiety.

Let me deal with them together, because they share a common root: a confusion between what you feel, what your body does, what presents itself to you, and how you respond.

### Four Things That Are Not the Same

Much of the shame Christians carry about anxiety comes from collapsing four different things into one.

A feeling. Fear, dread, unease and worry are emotional responses. They arise. You do not summon them, and you usually cannot dismiss them by deciding to. Scripture is full of people who felt afraid, including people God commended.

A physical reaction. A racing heart, tight chest, shallow breathing, nausea, restlessness, sleeplessness. These are bodily events. They can be triggered by a real threat, an imagined one, exhaustion, illness or, for some people, by conditions that are medical in nature. Your body’s response to stress is not a moral act.

An unwanted thought. In the middle of fear, certain thoughts present themselves: God has abandoned you. You are on your own. Nothing you do will matter. The arrival of a thought is not the same as agreement with it. Scripture distinguishes temptation from consent: Jesus was genuinely tempted, yet without sin. This does not mean every intrusive thought should be classified as spiritual temptation. It means that an unwanted thought does not, merely by appearing, reveal your character or your faith.

A chosen response. Feelings and bodily reactions are not, by themselves, moral decisions. Our response matters: whether we seek truth, accept support, take a faithful step or deliberately surrender ourselves to a false conclusion. Even here, our freedom can be affected by exhaustion, trauma, illness or compulsive patterns. God sees the whole person with a clarity and mercy that we do not possess. This is where questions of trust and obedience become more directly relevant, although even our responses must be understood with wisdom, compassion and attention to the person’s circumstances.

When Ruth said, “Either the verses don’t work or I don’t have real faith,” she was treating the feeling and the physical reaction as though they were a chosen response. She was judging her faith by her pulse. But her pulse was not where her faith lived. Her faith lived in the fact that she was standing in front of her pastor, frightened and ashamed, still asking for help, still refusing to give up on God. That is what trust looks like in the middle of fear. It looks like David in Gath.

### What “Do Not Be Anxious” Does and Does Not Mean

The sentence that troubles anxious Christians most is Paul’s: “Do not be anxious about anything” (Philippians 4:6). We will return to that passage in Lesson 15. But I want to say something now, because too many readers hear it as a verdict.

Notice, first, what Paul does immediately after saying it. He does not say, “So stop.” He says, “But in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God.” He assumes there are things to be anxious about—real requests, real needs—and he gives anxious people something to do with them.

Notice, second, who is writing. Paul himself described overwhelming pressure, despair and deep concern for the churches. We should not diagnose him from those statements, but they show that “Do not be anxious” was not written by someone unfamiliar with severe emotional and pastoral pressure. He was a man who had learnt where to take it.

Notice, third, what kind of instruction it is. Paul’s words are genuine instruction, but instruction is not the same as condemnation. He calls believers away from being governed by anxiety and immediately directs them toward prayer, petition and thanksgiving.

The command gives anxious people a path to follow; it does not invite them to deny what they feel or condemn themselves for bodily symptoms they did not choose. Scripture also says, “Do not fear,” “Let not your hearts be troubled,” and “Rejoice always.” Nobody reads “Rejoice always” and concludes that a grieving Christian has sinned by feeling sorrow. These commands point us in a direction; they do not pronounce judgement on every emotion that has not yet followed.

I am not saying anxiety is never connected to sin. Sometimes worry does grow from a refusal to trust, or from an idol we are protecting. But that is something to discover honestly, in prayer and with wise counsel, not something to assume every time your heart races.

### Where the Shame Comes From

If Scripture is more generous than the shame, where does the shame come from? From several places, usually.

From half-heard teaching. Many believers have absorbed, from a sermon, a book or a well-meaning relative, that peace is the automatic result of faith and that its absence must therefore mean faith is absent. Nobody sat them down and said, “David was afraid in Gath.” They simply picked up the equation: strong faith equals calm.

From comparison. Church can be a place where everyone appears fine. You see people worshipping freely and assume their inner life matches their raised hands. You do not see them at two in the morning. Your inside is being compared with everyone else’s outside, and your inside will always lose.

From the fear of being a burden. Anxious Christians often believe that if they were honest about their state, they would exhaust the people around them. So they hide it, and the hiding confirms the shame.

From treating anxiety as a verdict on the self. This is the deepest source. The anxious believer does not merely think, “I am feeling fear.” They think, “I am the kind of person who fails at faith.” The feeling becomes an identity, and identities are much harder to bring into the light.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I admit that anxiety is not always sin, I am making excuses for not trusting God.”

Some readers will resist this chapter, not because they want to be ashamed, but because they are afraid that removing the shame will remove their motivation to grow. If anxiety is not my fault, why would I do anything about it?

But shame has never been a good engine for growth. Shame hides. Shame compares. Shame lies awake rehearsing its failures. Shame is, in fact, one of the primary fuels of the overthinking this course exists to address. Remove the shame, and you have not removed the reason to grow. You have removed the thing that was preventing it.

There is a difference between conviction and condemnation. Conviction says, “There is something here that God wants to address, and he is with you in it.” Condemnation says, “You are a failure, and you should keep this to yourself.” One draws you toward God. The other drives you into hiding. Paul is clear about which one belongs to those who are in Christ: “There is therefore now no condemnation” (Romans 8:1).

You can take your anxiety seriously without taking shame as your companion. In fact, you will take it more seriously once shame is out of the room, because you will finally be able to look at it directly.

### Practical Application

The work of this chapter is not to stop feeling anxious. It is to stop adding a second layer of suffering on top of the first.

That begins with catching the sentences. Every anxious Christian has a small set of shaming sentences that run almost automatically: I should be past this. Real believers don’t struggle like this. God must be tired of me. These sentences are usually so familiar that they no longer sound like sentences. They sound like facts.

The practice is to hear them as sentences again, ask where they came from, and ask whether the sentence accurately represents what God has said and how Scripture applies it. The shaming verdicts do not.

What God has said is this: “When I am afraid, I put my trust in you.” “Cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you.” “There is therefore now no condemnation.”

You can acknowledge that you are frightened and still turn toward God in trust. That is not a contradiction. That tension appears throughout the Psalms.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that are true for you, adapt them or remain silent.*

Father, you know that I have been ashamed. I have believed that my fear disappointed you, that a stronger Christian would not feel what I feel, that I should be able to pray this away by now.

I have carried that shame in silence, and it has made everything heavier.

Today I bring you both burdens—the fear and the shame about the fear. I choose to believe what your word says rather than what my shame says: that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ, and that when I am afraid, I can put my trust in you.

I do not have to be calm to come to you. I only have to come.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
