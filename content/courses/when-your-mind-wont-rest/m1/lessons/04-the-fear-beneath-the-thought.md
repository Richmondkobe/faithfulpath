# Lesson 4: The Fear Beneath the Thought

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to explore gently whether a repeated thought may be connected to a deeper fear, while still giving practical problems the practical attention they require.

## Before you begin

This lesson explores a possibility, not a hidden law of the mind. Sometimes a budget is simply a budget. Treat whatever comes to mind as something to consider, not as a message from God, a recovered memory or a final explanation of yourself. The exercise is optional. Stop it if you become overwhelmed, disconnected from your surroundings, panicked or unable to return your attention to the present: put the page aside, notice where you are, contact someone safe and seek appropriate professional support. If the fear you find is attached to trauma, abuse, bereavement or a loss that still overwhelms you, do not keep digging alone; that is exactly the point at which seeking help is the faithful next step. Before looking for a deeper fear, confirm that any practical, medical, legal, financial or safety issue has received the appropriate attention; a deeper emotional meaning does not make the practical issue unreal. Complete this exercise only during the day or your normal waking period: do not begin it in the middle of the night, and use only the Night-Time Thought Download then. You do not need to uncover the deepest explanation today.

## Key Scripture: Psalm 139:23–24 (ESV)

> Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!

The close of a psalm in which the psalmist marvels that God already knows him completely, and then asks to be searched anyway.

## One truth to carry with you

When a thought keeps returning after the facts have been considered and reasonable action has been taken, the surface subject may not be the whole issue. Sometimes the mind is also responding to what the situation seems to mean about your worth, your safety, your future or your trust in God. The possible fear may already be contributing to the pressure you feel. Naming it does not prove that it is the hidden cause, and it may initially increase discomfort. Treat it as something to consider, not as a discovery you must accept. And a fear that has been named can be brought to God as a request rather than hidden from him as a problem. Search me. Show me. Lead me. He has already searched you and knows you, and he does not turn away from what he finds.

## The lesson

A man I will call Samuel had been thinking about his church's annual budget for three weeks. He was the treasurer, careful and capable, and the numbers were not the problem; giving was slightly down, the shortfall was manageable and he had already identified two sensible adjustments. Yet he could not stop. He recalculated figures he had already confirmed and rehearsed his presentation to the elders again and again. I asked him a question I have learnt to ask. "Suppose the elders looked at the numbers and were unhappy. What would that mean to you?" "That I'd let them down." "And if you had?" "That I'm not as capable as they think I am." "And if that were true?" A long pause. "That they wouldn't want me." Samuel had not only been thinking about a budget. He had been guarding a door, and behind it was a fear of being found inadequate and set aside. The spreadsheet was real and still needed his attention. But it had become the place the fear had chosen to live.

Psalm 139 marvels that God knows the psalmist completely, and then, at the very end, asks God to search him. Why, if God already knows everything? Because there is a difference between being known and knowing that you are known. God already sees the fear beneath Samuel's spreadsheet; Samuel does not. Notice, too, that he asks God to know his thoughts, and the Hebrew term can carry the sense of anxious, disquieting thoughts, closely related to the language of Psalm 94:19. The prayer includes moral and spiritual examination, but it also brings the psalmist's inner thoughts before the God who already knows him completely. It is a prayer overthinkers can learn by heart: not only "Lord, make the thoughts stop," but "Lord, give me honesty and wisdom to see what these thoughts may be about."

Do this prayerfully but not credulously: treat what comes to mind as something to consider, not automatically as a message from God, a recovered memory or a final explanation. If it feels safe and helpful, you may discuss what you notice with a trustworthy person; you are never required to share the worksheet or name the fear to a group, spouse, pastor or course leader. If the exercise causes significant distress, disconnection, panic, a sense of reliving or difficulty returning to the present, stop and seek appropriate professional support. You do not need to decide what the reaction means.

A man I will call Samuel had been thinking about his church’s annual budget for three weeks. He was the treasurer, a careful and capable man, and the numbers were not the problem. Giving was slightly down. The shortfall was manageable. He had already identified two sensible adjustments.

And yet he could not stop. He checked the spreadsheet in the evenings. He recalculated figures he had already confirmed. He rehearsed the presentation he would give to the elders, then revised it, then rehearsed it again.

When he told me about it, I asked him a question I have learnt to ask.

“Suppose the worst happened. Suppose the elders looked at the numbers and were unhappy. What would that mean to you?”

He answered quickly. “That I’d let them down.”

“And if you had let them down?”

A longer pause. “That I’m not as capable as they think I am.”

“And if that were true?”

He did not answer for a while. Then he said, quietly, “That they wouldn’t want me.”

Samuel had not only been thinking about a budget for three weeks. He had been guarding a door, and behind the door was a fear of being found inadequate and set aside. The spreadsheet was real, and it still needed his attention. But it had also become the place the fear had chosen to live.

### Central Scripture: Psalm 139:23–24

Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting! (Psalm 139:23–24, ESV) These verses close one of the most intimate psalms in the Bible. Throughout the psalm, the psalmist marvels that God knows him completely: his sitting down and rising up, his words before they are spoken, his frame before it was formed. Nothing is hidden.

Then, at the very end, he does something remarkable. He asks God to search him. Having spent the whole psalm declaring that God already knows everything, he still says, “Search me.” Why?

I think because there is a difference between being known and knowing that you are known. God already sees the fear beneath Samuel’s spreadsheet. But Samuel does not, and the psalmist’s prayer is a request that the knowledge God already has might become knowledge he shares. It is a prayer to be shown what is really there.

Notice also that he asks God to know his thoughts. The Hebrew term translated “thoughts” can carry the sense of anxious or disquieting thoughts and is closely related to the language used in Psalm 94:19. The psalmist is not asking God to inspect his theology. He is asking God to look beneath the churning.

This is a prayer overthinkers can learn by heart. Not only “Lord, make the thoughts stop,” but “Lord, give me honesty and wisdom to see what these thoughts may be about.”

### When the Surface Thought Is Not the Whole Story

Here is the central idea of this chapter, stated carefully.

When a thought keeps returning after the available facts have been considered and reasonable action has been taken, the surface subject may not be the whole issue. Sometimes the mind is also responding to what the situation appears to mean about your worth, safety, future, relationships or trust in God.

Grace, in Lesson 1, had a real question about her manager. Daniel, in Lesson 2, had a real decision about a job. Samuel had a real budget. In each example, the practical situation had become connected to a deeper fear. That fear may be old, or it may have arisen from the present circumstances. Either way, the practical problem and the fear had become tangled together, so that solving one no longer settled the other.

This is why answering the surface thought alone does not always bring rest. You can show Grace that her manager was not upset. Tomorrow her mind may find a different colleague to be uncertain about, because the fear of being found inadequate has not been touched. You can help Daniel decide about the job. Next year he may be stuck on a different decision, because the fear of getting it wrong has not been named.

When overthinking continues after the facts have been gathered and reasonable action has been identified, it can be helpful to ask whether the situation carries a deeper meaning. A budget may also represent a fear of failure. An unanswered message may awaken a fear of rejection. A difficult decision may become connected to a fear of permanent regret.

This is a possibility to explore, not a hidden law of the mind. ### The Fears That Often Sit Beneath

In twenty years of these conversations, a small number of fears have appeared again and again. I list them not so you can diagnose yourself from a list, but so that when you look beneath your own thoughts, you may recognise what you find.

Fear of rejection. If they really knew me, they would not want me. This fear can sit beneath overthinking about relationships, conversations, silences and social situations.

Fear of failure. If this goes wrong, it will prove I am not good enough. This fear can sit beneath perfectionism, delay and endless preparation.

Fear of financial loss. If the money runs out, I will not be able to protect the people I love. This fear can sit beneath compulsive checking, calculating and planning.

Fear of disappointing people. If I let them down, I will lose their respect or their love. This fear can sit beneath people-pleasing and the inability to say no.

Fear of making the wrong decision. If I choose badly, the consequences will be permanent and I will have only myself to blame. This fear can sit beneath decision paralysis and the endless search for confirmation.

Fear of losing control. If I stop managing everything, something terrible will happen. This fear can sit beneath overplanning, hypervigilance and the inability to rest.

Fear that God will not come through. I say I trust him, but I am not sure he will actually act. This fear can sit beneath much religious overthinking and is often the hardest to admit.

Fear of repeating the past. What happened before will happen again, and I will not survive it twice. This fear can sit beneath overthinking that seems out of proportion to the present situation.

You may recognise one fear or several. Do not force yourself to choose a dominant fear if none is immediately clear.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I find the fear underneath, I will have to feel it, and I cannot handle that.”

This is one reason many people keep overthinking. The surface thought is uncomfortable, but it is manageable. The fear underneath feels as though it might swallow them. So the mind keeps circling the spreadsheet, because circling the spreadsheet is safer than facing the thought, They wouldn’t want me.

I understand this. But I want to offer two things in reply.

First, you are already feeling the fear. You have been feeling it for weeks, in the form of a mind that will not rest. Naming it does not create it. Naming it changes it from a nameless pressure into something you can look at, pray about and bring to others.

Second, naming a fear can sometimes make it more specific and therefore more workable. When Samuel finally said, “They wouldn’t want me,” he heard the sentence out loud and recognised that it did not match what he actually knew. His elders had shown him nothing but confidence for years. The fear had been enormous in the dark and became something he could examine in the light.

But this is not always immediate or comfortable. Some fears are connected to experiences that require time, safety and skilled support before they can be explored responsibly. If the fear you find is attached to trauma, abuse, bereavement or a loss that still overwhelms you, the wise response is not to keep digging alone. That is exactly the point at which seeking help becomes the faithful next step.

### Two Kinds of Attention

Finding the fear is not the end of the work, but it changes what the work is.

A practical problem requires practical action. A deeper fear requires a different response. Sometimes both are present.

Once Samuel knew that beneath the budget was a fear of rejection, he still completed the practical work that belonged to him. He finished the spreadsheet, made his two adjustments and gave his presentation. What changed was that he stopped expecting the spreadsheet to resolve the fear of rejection. The budget and the fear now received different kinds of attention.

For the fear, he began asking a different set of questions. Is this fear telling the truth? What does God say about it? Who has actually rejected me, and who has not? What would I do differently if I believed I was secure?

These are questions Module 3 will help you answer in detail. For now, notice that they are answerable in a way that “Will the elders be happy with the numbers?” is not. That question cannot be settled in advance, and so the mind circles it. “Am I secure in Christ whether or not the elders are happy?” can be answered, and Scripture answers it.

Security in Christ does not make human rejection painless or practical consequences unimportant. It means that another person’s response is not the final measure of your worth or the final verdict over your life. Fear that has been named can be brought to God as a request rather than hidden from him as a problem. That is the movement at the end of Psalm 139: Search me. Show me. Lead me. It is the difference between praying only about the spreadsheet and also praying about the fear.

### Practical Application

The tool this chapter offers is a question, asked a few times, in a downward direction.

If this happened, what would it mean to me?

You start with the surface thought and ask what it would mean if the feared outcome occurred. Then you take the answer and ask again. You keep going, briefly, until you reach a sentence that is no longer about the situation and is instead about you, your worth, your safety, your future or your God.

Three cautions.

Do this gently and not endlessly. The aim is to notice a possible fear, not to interrogate yourself. Three to five layers is enough. If you find yourself digging repeatedly on the same thought, looking for a deeper or more accurate answer, stop. That is the overthinking reappearing in a new form.

Do this prayerfully but not credulously. Pray for wisdom as you explore what the thought may mean to you. Treat what comes to mind as something to consider—not automatically as a message from God, a recovered memory or a final explanation of your behaviour.

Do this with others where you can. Some fears are difficult to see in yourself and obvious to a trusted friend, spouse, pastor or counsellor. And if what you find feels too heavy to carry with a friend, that is a sign to speak with a pastor or a qualified professional.
