# Lesson 5: Your Body Is Part of the Story

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to check your body before you interpret your mind, recognise the physical factors that commonly intensify restless thinking, and know when a pattern belongs to a doctor rather than a journal.

## Before you begin

Elijah asked God to take his life. If you have had thoughts of not wanting to be alive, or of harming yourself, please do not treat this as a matter of tiredness alone. Speak today with a doctor, a mental-health professional or a crisis service in your country, and tell someone safe who is near you. If you may act on these thoughts, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact your local emergency services or an urgent crisis service now, or go to the nearest emergency department. Do not remain alone. This lesson can wait. You cannot. The Finding Help Where You Live page explains how to find support in your country. Urgent physical symptoms: do not assume that chest pain, severe difficulty breathing, fainting, sudden confusion or another new or severe physical symptom is anxiety. Seek urgent medical assessment or contact your local emergency services. The Body and Mind Check-In is not a symptom checker and must not delay medical care. And a rule for everything that follows: never start, stop or change medication because of anything in this course. If symptoms begin or worsen after a medication change, contact the prescriber or a pharmacist; do not alter the medication yourself.

## Key Scripture: 1 Kings 19:1–18 (ESV)

> And he lay down and slept under a broom tree. And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, "Arise and eat."

Elijah, straight from Mount Carmel, fleeing for his life and asking God to let him die. Read the whole passage before continuing, because the sequence matters.

## One truth to carry with you

You are not only a mind. God made your body and called it good, and Scripture is unembarrassed by the fact that people who are hungry, exhausted or ill think and feel differently from people who are fed and rested. When Elijah sat under the broom tree and asked to die, God did not rebuke his despair or correct his theology. Through the angel he provided touch, food, water and sleep, and only later, at Horeb, did the conversation about Elijah's fear and future begin. The lesson is not that every spiritual crisis is physical, and food and sleep do not cure anxiety disorders. It is that the body should not be ignored when we are trying to understand a spiritual or emotional crisis. So be slow to draw conclusions about your faith or your future from an exhausted, hungry or unwell body. Ask first: what is my body telling me?

## The lesson

A pastor I will call Joseph phoned me late one evening in what he called spiritual crisis. He had been questioning his calling for a week, felt distant from God and had begun to wonder whether the last fifteen years had been a mistake. I listened for a long time. Then I asked questions that had nothing to do with his calling. How much had he slept? Four or five hours a night, maybe less. When had he last taken a day off? He could not remember. Had he eaten properly? Something, at some point. He had also been fighting a chest infection for a fortnight and drinking a great deal of coffee to push through it. I did not tell him his crisis was imaginary, or that sleep would answer his questions. I told him I did not think either of us could hear those questions clearly until he had slept, eaten and let his body recover, and because the infection had gone on for two weeks, I encouraged him to see a doctor. He slept eleven hours and saw his doctor the next day. The questions about his calling did not disappear, but when we spoke the following week they had become smaller and more specific, and he could think about them without despair. Joseph's body had been part of the story all along. He had simply not been reading that part.

Elijah has just come from Carmel, where fire fell and the drought ended. Then Jezebel threatens his life, and he runs into the wilderness, sits under a broom tree and asks God to let him die. Only after this, and forty days of travel, does he arrive at Horeb, where God asks, "What are you doing here?", and only then does the conversation about his fear, his isolation and his future begin. First Kings 19 does not allow us to diagnose Elijah, and it does not teach that food and sleep cure despair; his danger and his questions still had to be addressed, and they were, at Horeb. Yet the sequence is pastorally significant. God did not treat his prophet as a disembodied soul. If God treated his exhausted servant that way, we should not treat ourselves, or each other, as though the body were irrelevant to the mind.

A pastor I will call Joseph phoned me late one evening. He was, he said, in spiritual crisis. He had been questioning his calling for a week. He felt distant from God. ### Why the Body Shapes the Mind

Many Christians have absorbed, without ever hearing it stated, a picture in which the spirit is what matters and the body is a container. On that picture, a restless mind is a spiritual problem with spiritual causes and spiritual solutions, and physical factors are beside the point.

Scripture does not share that picture. God made human beings as embodied creatures and called that good. Jesus took on a body, grew tired, slept in boats and asked for water. Paul told Timothy to take a little wine for his stomach. The Bible is unembarrassed by the fact that people who are hungry, exhausted or ill think and feel differently from people who are fed and rested.

So, without pretending to be a doctor, let me name some of the physical factors that commonly intensify anxious and repetitive thinking.

Sleep deprivation. Insufficient or poor-quality sleep can affect emotional regulation, concentration, decision-making and the ability to keep difficulties in perspective. Problems that look manageable at noon can look overwhelming at three in the morning, and this is not simply because the problems have changed.

Prolonged stress. A body that has been under pressure for weeks or months does not switch off when the pressure eases. It stays braced, and a braced body tends to produce a scanning, vigilant mind.

Hunger and poor nutrition. Irregular meals can contribute to physical discomfort, fatigue or difficulty concentrating. These sensations may complicate an already anxious day, although food patterns do not explain every experience of anxiety.

Illness and pain. Being unwell, even with something minor, drains the reserves that would normally help you keep worry in proportion. Chronic pain and chronic conditions do this over long periods.

Caffeine and other stimulants. Caffeine in coffee, energy drinks, some teas, soft drinks and certain products can contribute to restlessness, increased heart rate, anxiety or disrupted sleep in some people. Sensitivity varies, and someone who consumes caffeine regularly may need to reduce it gradually rather than stopping suddenly. Many people who feel anxious in the evenings have not considered what they drank in the afternoon.

Medication. Some medicines can affect mood, alertness, sleep or anxiety, particularly when started, stopped or adjusted. Never start, stop or change medication because of anything in this course. Discuss concerns with the prescribing professional or a pharmacist, especially if a change in your thinking coincides with a change in what you take.

Exhaustion. Not merely a lack of sleep, but the accumulated tiredness of long seasons without rest. Joseph was exhausted in this sense, and no single night’s sleep would have fully addressed it.

You will notice that none of these is sinful. Nobody has failed God by being tired or unwell. But each of them can make a restless mind more restless, and most overthinkers have never asked whether their body is contributing to the noise.

### What This Chapter Does Not Claim

I want to be as clear here as anywhere in this course, because this is a place where well-meaning teaching can do harm.

Not every spiritual struggle has a physical cause. Some anxious thinking arises from genuine spiritual questions, from real sin that needs repentance, from relational wounds, from grief, or from fears of the kind we explored in Lesson 4. Attending to the body does not replace attending to those things. Elijah still needed Horeb.

Food and sleep do not cure anxiety disorders. If you live with an anxiety condition, depression, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, trauma-related symptoms or another mental-health condition, better sleep and regular meals may help you cope, but they are not treatment. Treatment is what qualified professionals provide, and it may include therapy, medication or both. Please do not let anyone, including this chapter, persuade you that you have simply not been resting enough.

Physical causes and spiritual realities are not rivals. A person can be sleep-deprived and also carrying a real fear of failure. A person can have a diagnosed condition and also need to hear that they are secure in Christ. The body is part of the story, not the whole of it.

The aim of this chapter is modest. It is to add one question to the ones you already ask when your mind will not rest: What is my body telling me?

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I attend to my body, I am being unspiritual. Real faith pushes through.”

This belief is common among conscientious Christians, and especially among those in ministry. Rest feels like self-indulgence. Eating properly feels like a distraction from the work. Admitting tiredness feels like admitting weakness.

But Jesus, who had more to do than any of us, withdrew to quiet places and slept through storms. The biblical pattern of Sabbath reminds us that human beings were not made for uninterrupted production. Christians understand the continuing application of the Sabbath command in different ways, but Scripture consistently treats rest, worship and creaturely limits as spiritually significant. The angel did not tell Elijah to push through. The angel told him to eat.

Pushing through is sometimes necessary. There are seasons—a newborn, a crisis, a deadline—when rest is not available and God gives grace for it. But pushing through may also become a way of avoiding limits, proving our worth or believing that everything depends on us. At other times, people push through because their circumstances leave them few choices: caregivers, parents of young children, those working long hours to keep a household afloat. The purpose here is not to condemn exhausted people but to question the belief that ignoring every limit is necessarily an expression of faith.

Caring for your body, where you are able, is not a departure from the spiritual life. It is part of loving God with your whole self, which includes the self that needs to eat and sleep.

### Practical Application

The practice this chapter offers is a habit of checking your body before you interpret your mind.

When you notice that your thoughts are racing, before you conclude anything about your faith, your calling, your relationships or your future, pause and ask a few simple questions. When did I last sleep well? When did I last eat? What have I been drinking? Am I unwell? How long has this season of pressure lasted?

The answers will not explain everything. But they will often explain something, and that something may change how seriously you take the conclusions your tired mind is offering you.

A useful principle for overthinkers: when reasonably possible, avoid making a major, irreversible decision during a period of acute exhaustion, significant sleep loss or untreated illness. If a decision cannot wait, involve a trusted and appropriately qualified person who can help you review the facts. Joseph did not decide about his calling at midnight after a week of poor sleep. He waited until he could see clearly. That was not avoidance. It was wisdom.

If the check-in reveals a pattern—weeks of poor sleep, a long stretch without rest, symptoms that are not improving—that is information for your doctor, not merely for your journal.

Teach me to rest without guilt where rest is possible, and to trust you for grace where it is not. Teach me to eat, sleep and recover as acts of trust rather than acts of weakness. And where my body needs help I cannot give it, give me humility to seek that help.

I bring you my whole self—mind, body and spirit—and I ask you to restore what is worn.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
