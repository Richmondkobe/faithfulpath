# Lesson 2: Thinking, Worrying or Overthinking?

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish useful thinking from mental circling using five questions and identify whether the next response is to act, wait, seek help or surrender.

## Before you begin

The seven kinds of thinking in this lesson are pastoral descriptions, not clinical diagnoses. If thoughts feel involuntary, return with strong urges to check, confess, repeat or obtain certainty, or repeatedly centre on salvation or feared sin, consider speaking with a qualified mental-health professional who understands obsessive-compulsive symptoms. Effective treatments are available, but this course cannot assess or treat the condition; some parts of the teaching may remain useful, and you should follow the professional's guidance about whether to use the worksheet. Keep the five questions somewhere you can use them during the day. If the thought returns in the middle of the night, use only the Night-Time Thought Download and return to these questions during your next waking period.

## Key Scripture: Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 (ESV)

> For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven: a time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up what is planted; a time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time to build up; a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance; a time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing; a time to seek, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to cast away; a time to tear, and a time to sew; a time to keep silence, and a time to speak; a time to love, and a time to hate; a time for war, and a time for peace.

Fourteen contrasting pairs. The Teacher's claim is not only that things happen but that wisdom involves recognising the right time for them.

## One truth to carry with you

There is a time to think and a time to stop thinking. Carefulness has a shape: it gathers what is needed, weighs it honestly, seeks counsel, prays, and then acts on the best available wisdom, accepting that certainty is not on offer. Endless thinking is not a more intense form of carefulness. It is a different thing wearing carefulness as a disguise. You do not have to keep circling to prove that you care. When the honest answer to "Am I learning anything new?" is no, the season for deliberately working on this question has ended, at least for now, and what comes next is not more thought. It is one of four responses: act, wait, seek help, or surrender. And stopping, when the thinking has done its work, is not carelessness. It is trust.

## The lesson

A man I will call Daniel came to see me because he could not decide whether to accept a job in another city. The offer had come six weeks earlier; the company had extended the deadline twice. He had prayed, made lists, asked his wife, his pastor, his father and two friends, and read every verse on guidance he could find. I asked him what he had learnt in the last two weeks that he did not know before. After a long pause: "Nothing, really." "Then what have you been doing for two weeks?" "Thinking about it." Daniel believed he was being careful, and he was partly right. But somewhere in the fourth or fifth week, careful thought had quietly become something else, and he could not see the moment it changed. This lesson is about learning to see that moment.

Ecclesiastes 3 is often read as a poem about the rhythms of life, and it is. But notice its underlying claim: wisdom is not only doing the right thing; it involves recognising the right time to do it. The passage does not mention thinking, and I do not want to claim the Teacher had our thought lives in view. But the principle applies with unusual force. There is a time to consider and a time to stop considering. Applying that principle, some overthinking can be understood as a failure to recognise when useful thinking has completed its work. This does not explain every form of repetitive thinking; some patterns are influenced by trauma, anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive symptoms or other conditions that cannot be resolved merely by deciding that the time for thinking has ended. Still, recognising when thought has stopped being useful helps almost everyone, whatever else they may also need.

Most overthinkers lump all their mental activity under one word, and that is part of the problem. So let me draw some distinctions. Reflection looks back in order to understand. Planning looks forward in order to prepare, and produces a step or a list. Discernment weighs a choice in the light of God's will and wisdom; it is unhurried but not endless. Problem-solving works on something that has a solution. All four are good. Now the three forms of mental circling. Worry looks forward, like planning, but produces no steps; it imagines what could go wrong and then imagines it again with variations. Rumination looks back, like reflection, but produces no insight; it replays the same pain in a different order. Obsessive thinking returns with a force that feels almost physical, demanding an answer or a ritual before it will release you, and the relief lasts only minutes.

Purposeful thinking and mental circling are not separated merely by subject. You can reflect on a conversation or ruminate on it; plan for a meeting or worry about it. They are separated by fruit. Jesus taught that fruit reveals the nature of a tree, and, without suggesting that passage is about overthinking, its principle gives us a useful question: what is this pattern of thought producing? Worry, rumination and obsessional or compulsive thinking produce more of themselves. Daniel's decision was important, he cared deeply, and he was extraordinarily thorough. He was still stuck, because he was measuring his thinking by its seriousness rather than by its fruit.

Here is the tool this lesson is built around. When you notice your mind returning to a subject, ask five questions. Is this thought producing a clear action, something you could write down and do? Am I learning anything new, or rearranging the same pieces? Is this within my control? Have I already considered this, and reopened a conclusion without any new information? Is continued thinking helping me obey God, or preventing me from loving the people in front of me, doing the work that belongs to me and receiving necessary rest? Consider the answers together: is the thinking still producing relevant information, understanding or a proportionate action, or is it reopening the same material without anything important changing? Ask them once for the chosen subject, and do not repeat them until the answer feels certain. If the thinking is no longer producing anything new, the season for working on this question has ended, at least for now. What comes next is one of four responses. Act: a clear responsibility belongs to me now. Wait: action may be needed, but not yet. Seek help: I lack information, skill, safety or support. Surrender: the outcome does not belong to me. We will return to these four responses again and again.

"If I stop thinking about it, I am being careless." Many overthinkers are conscientious people, and they hear a call to stop as a call to become careless. But Daniel in week six had stopped gathering information in week two and stopped hearing anything new in week three. What he was doing was not care. By that stage the thinking appeared to be functioning as avoidance: continuing to analyse postponed the frightening act of choosing. Stopping when the thinking has done its work is not carelessness. It is trust.

A word about what "stopping" means. You cannot always command a thought to leave, and trying to force it out often makes it return more strongly. What you can do is stop deliberately working on the question and redirect your attention to something that belongs to the present moment. The thought may drift back. That is not failure. Each time it does, you simply decline to reopen the file and turn again toward what is in front of you. The most useful habit you can build from this lesson is a pause, just long enough to ask, "Which of the seven is this?" You will often get it wrong at first; rumination is very good at looking like reflection. But the more often you ask, the sooner you will feel the moment when useful thinking tips over into circling, and that is where change begins.

A man I will call Daniel came to see me a few years ago because he could not decide whether to accept a job in another city. He had been offered the position six weeks earlier. The company had extended the deadline twice.

Daniel believed he was being careful. He believed a serious Christian should not rush a decision like this. He was partly right. But somewhere in the fourth or fifth week, careful thought had quietly become something else, and he could not see the moment it changed.

This chapter is about learning to see that moment.

### Central Scripture: Ecclesiastes 3:1–8

For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven. (Ecclesiastes 3:1, ESV) The Teacher in Ecclesiastes goes on to list fourteen pairs: a time to be born and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to pluck up, a time to weep and a time to laugh, a time to keep silence and a time to speak. Read the whole passage slowly before you continue.

Christians often read this passage as a poem about the rhythms of life, and it is. But notice its underlying claim. Wisdom is not only about doing the right thing; it also involves recognising the right time to do it. Silence is good, but there is a time when silence becomes cowardice. Speaking is good, but there is a time when speaking becomes foolishness. The same action can be faithful in one season and harmful in another.

The passage does not mention thinking directly, and I do not want to claim that the Teacher had our thought lives in view. But the principle applies with unusual force. There is a time to consider, and a time to stop considering. There is a time to gather information, and a time to act on the information you have. There is a time to search your heart, and a time to leave the searching and go to sleep.

Applying the principle of Ecclesiastes to our thought lives, some overthinking can be understood as a failure to recognise when useful thinking has completed its work. It is not that the thinking is wrong. It is that the season for it has ended, and the mind has not noticed.

This does not explain every form of repetitive thinking. Some patterns are influenced by trauma, anxiety, depression, obsessive- compulsive symptoms or other conditions that cannot be resolved merely by deciding that the time for thinking has ended. Nevertheless, recognising when thought has stopped being useful remains an important skill, and it is one that helps almost everyone, whatever else they may also need.

### Seven Kinds of Thinking

Most overthinkers lump all their mental activity together under one word: “thinking.” That is part of the problem. When everything is called thinking, the healthy and the unhealthy become impossible to separate, and the reader either feels guilty about all of it or defends all of it.

So let me draw some distinctions. These categories are pastoral descriptions, not clinical diagnoses. Real mental activity does not always fit neatly into one category, and a pattern may move from one category to another. The purpose is not to label every thought correctly. It is to help you notice whether your thinking is moving toward understanding and faithful action or keeping you trapped in repetition.

Reflection looks back in order to understand. What happened there? Why did I react that way? What does that experience teach me? Reflection is slow, honest and usually calm. It normally moves toward understanding, even when it leaves some questions open.

Planning looks forward in order to prepare. What needs to happen before Friday? What will I say if they ask about the budget? Planning has a concrete target and normally produces a step, a list or a schedule.

Discernment weighs a choice in light of God’s will and wisdom. Is this consistent with Scripture? Does it fit who God has made me to be? What do wise people say? Discernment is unhurried but not endless. It moves toward a wise and faithful decision, which is not the same as a guaranteed one.

Problem-solving works on something that has a solution. The car will not start; what are the likely causes? Problem-solving tests possible responses and identifies what can be done next, even if the next step is handing the problem to someone better placed to solve it.

All four of these are good. Scripture commends them. God gave you a mind for exactly this work.

Now the other three.

Worry looks forward, like planning, but without producing steps. It imagines what could go wrong and then imagines it again with slight variations. Worry feels like preparation but produces nothing you can act on. It does not end; it is interrupted.

Rumination looks back, like reflection, but without producing insight. It replays a conversation, a mistake or a loss, over and over, as though one more viewing might change what happened. Rumination feels like processing but produces only the same pain in a different order.

Obsessive thinking is the most intense of the three. A thought or question returns with a force that feels almost physical, demanding an answer or a ritual before it will release you: Did I lock the door? Did I really mean that prayer? Am I truly saved? Did I offend God without knowing it? The relief that comes from answering lasts only minutes before the question returns.

I need to be clear about that last category. Some people experience intrusive, repetitive thoughts that go well beyond ordinary overthinking, and some of these patterns have clinical names and effective treatments. If your thoughts feel involuntary, come with strong urges to check, confess or repeat something, or centre persistently on doubts about your salvation or fears of having committed some terrible sin, this course can still help, but please also speak with a qualified mental-health professional. That is not a spiritual failure. It is wisdom about timing—the very thing this chapter is about.

### Why the Categories Matter

Look again at those seven and you will notice something. The healthy four and the unhealthy three are not separated by subject. You can reflect on a conversation or ruminate on it. You can plan for a meeting or worry about it. You can discern a decision or obsess over it.

They are separated by fruit.

Jesus taught that fruit reveals the nature of a tree. Without suggesting that this passage is directly about overthinking, its principle gives us a useful question: what is this pattern of thought producing? Reflection produces understanding. Planning produces steps. Discernment produces a faithful decision. Problem-solving produces a next move. Worry, rumination and obsession produce more of themselves.

This is why you cannot tell whether your thinking is healthy by asking, “Is this an important subject?” or “Do I care about this?” or “Am I being thorough?” Daniel’s job decision was important. He cared deeply. He was extraordinarily thorough. And he was still stuck, because he was measuring his thinking by its seriousness rather than by its fruit.

### The Five Questions

Here is the tool this chapter is built around. Memorise these five questions. Write them on a card. Put them where you will see them at night.

When you notice your mind returning to a subject, ask:

1. Is this thought producing a clear action? Not a feeling, not a mood, not a vague intention, but something you could write down and do. If you have returned to the subject repeatedly without gaining information, insight or a possible action, that may be a sign that useful thinking has become mental circling.

2. Am I learning anything new? Has the last pass through this subject given you a single fact, insight or possibility that you did not have before? Or are you rearranging the same pieces?

3. Is this within my control? Some things are yours to act on. Some things are partly yours. Some things belong entirely to God or to other people. Thinking hard about something outside your control does not move it inside your control. It only wears you down.

4. Have I already considered this? Be honest. Have you reached a conclusion on this before, perhaps several times, and then reopened it? New information can rightly reopen a decision. But if no important information has changed, a conclusion that repeatedly reopens may not be receiving further consideration; it may be receiving further distrust.

5. Is continued thinking helping me obey God? This is the question that makes the tool Christian rather than merely practical. Even when God’s direction about a major decision is not yet clear, many of today’s responsibilities remain visible: love the people in front of you, act truthfully, complete the work that belongs to you and receive necessary rest. Is your thinking helping you live faithfully today, or preventing you from doing so?

If the answers are mostly no, the season for deliberately working on this question has ended, at least for now. What comes next is not more thought. It is one of four responses:

Act. A clear responsibility belongs to me now.

Wait. Action may be needed, but not yet.

Seek help. I lack information, skill, safety or support.

Surrender. The outcome does not belong to me. We will return to these four responses again and again in the chapters ahead, and Module 3 will show how to do each of them well. For now, it is enough to recognise that thinking is no longer the right tool.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I stop thinking about it, I am being careless.”

Many overthinkers are, at root, conscientious people. They have learnt that thinking things through is responsible, and they are right. So when I suggest that there is a time to stop deliberately working on a question, they hear me suggesting that they become careless, and everything in them resists.

But consider Daniel. Was he being careful in week six? He had stopped gathering information in week two. He had stopped hearing anything new from his advisers in week three. What he was doing in week six was not care. It was avoidance dressed as care. The thinking had become a way of not deciding, because deciding felt frightening.

Carefulness has a shape. It gathers what is needed, weighs it honestly, seeks counsel, prays, and then acts on the best available wisdom, accepting that certainty is not on offer. Endless thinking is not a more intense form of carefulness. It is a different thing wearing carefulness as a disguise.

Stopping when the thinking has done its work is not carelessness. It is trust.

A word about what “stopping” means. You cannot always command a thought to leave, and trying to force it out often makes it return more strongly. What you can do is stop deliberately working on the question and redirect your attention to something that belongs to the present moment. The thought may drift back. That is not failure.

Each time it does, you simply decline to reopen the file and turn again toward what is in front of you.

### Practical Application

The most useful habit you can build from this chapter is a pause. Not a long one. Just enough to ask, “Which of the seven is this?”

You will not always be able to answer, and you will often get it wrong at first. Rumination is very good at looking like reflection. Worry is very good at looking like planning. But the more often you ask, the faster you will recognise the difference, and eventually you will feel the moment when useful thinking tips over into circling, the way you can feel a car beginning to skid before it has fully lost grip.

That moment of recognition is where change begins. You cannot redirect what you have not noticed.

## Worksheet: Healthy Thinking or Mental Circling?

Choose one recurring subject. Over no more than three days, complete a maximum of one brief entry per day about that subject. Do not record every recurrence. After the third entry, or earlier if the pattern is already clear, stop and keep the worksheet. Do not begin a new copy for the same subject because uncertainty returns, and do not use it in the middle of the night. Open it from the Resources box.
