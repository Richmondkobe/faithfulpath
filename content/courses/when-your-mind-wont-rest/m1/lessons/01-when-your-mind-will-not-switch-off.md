# Lesson 1: When Your Mind Will Not Switch Off

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to name what has been occupying your mind, without trying to solve it tonight, and recognise the difference between thinking that moves and thinking that circles.

## Before you begin

Occasional overthinking is common, but persistent or severe symptoms may need more than a course. If racing or repetitive thoughts regularly prevent sleep, disrupt daily life, accompany panic, involve compulsive behaviour, or leave you feeling unsafe, speak with an appropriately qualified healthcare or mental-health professional. Seeking help is not a failure of faith. The exercise in this lesson asks you to write down what your mind has been returning to; it does not ask you to fix any of it.

## Key Scripture: Psalm 94:19 (ESV)

> When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul.

A psalm spoken from a place of pressure, while the troubles described had not yet been resolved.

## One truth to carry with you

Your mind may be crowded today, but that does not mean God has withdrawn from you or that you have failed him. Psalm 94:19 holds two realities together: the cares remain many, and God's consolation is still present. You do not have to force yourself into calm before coming to him. You do not have to answer every question before you are allowed to rest. Bring God the mind you actually have: the tired mind, the circling mind, the mind with unfinished questions. His comfort is not reserved for the moment when everything has been resolved. It can meet you here, while life is still uncertain. Your thoughts may be loud, but they are not your master, and they do not place you beyond the reach of God's care.

## The lesson

It is late, and your body is asking for sleep, but your mind is holding a meeting you never agreed to attend. You replay something you said that morning. You imagine what could go wrong tomorrow. You pray about it, and a few minutes later you are thinking about it again.

If that is familiar, you are not alone, and you are not necessarily failing God. This course will not tell you to stop thinking. God gave you the ability to think, examine, remember, imagine and plan. The problem begins when your thoughts keep moving but no longer take you anywhere.

Let me describe a woman I will call Grace. She is a faithful believer, a reliable colleague and a loving mother, and nobody at church would guess what her evenings are like. She switches off the light at ten. By quarter past she has replayed a short exchange with her manager three times. By eleven she has moved from her son's exam results to a bill that is not late to a comment her sister made in 2019. At some point she remembers to pray. Then she opens her eyes and the meeting resumes where it left off. Grace is not lazy or unspiritual. She is exhausted, and she is ashamed, because she believes a stronger Christian would hand all of this to God and fall asleep. I have sat with many Graces. What they have in common is not weak faith. It is a mind that has learnt to run without a destination.

Notice two things about Psalm 94:19, which gives us the words of a psalmist with a crowded mind. The psalmist does not say, "When I had cares," but "When the cares of my heart are many." The Hebrew word can describe anxious, disquieting thoughts, the concerns that multiply until the mind feels crowded. This is not a confession of sin; it is a description of a condition. And notice where relief comes from. Not from the cares being resolved. God's consolations reach him while the cares are still many. Both are true at once.

Overthinking rarely feels like a problem from the inside; it feels like diligence. But there are marks. The same subject keeps returning, as though your earlier conclusion never happened. You are always somewhere else. Rest feels unsafe, because silence feels like negligence. You feel a foggy tiredness that sleep does not fix. Small things become large.

I do not want you to become afraid of your own mind. Thinking is a gift, and Scripture commands it: consider, ponder, examine, test. Joseph planned for famine; Jesus told his followers to count the cost. The difference between useful thinking and mental circling is not the subject and not the intensity. It is the direction. Useful thinking gathers, weighs, decides and then hands over to action or to rest. Circling revisits and re-worries, often stopping only when exhaustion, distraction or an outside interruption breaks the loop. Ask, "Where is this thought taking me?" If the honest answer is "back to where I started," you are circling.

Why does the mind keep returning? Usually because the thinking has not produced what it is reaching for. Sometimes that is missing information. But very often, after the same facts have been reviewed repeatedly, the mind is seeking not information but certainty. Give the mind a question without an answer, "Will they still like me?", and it does not know how to stop. That is why "I have already thought about this" settles nothing. And often there is a fear underneath, which we will explore in Lesson 4: Grace is not really thinking about her manager's tone. She is guarding against the fear of being found inadequate.

Many overthinkers believe that if they could think hard enough the fog would clear. After a certain point, the opposite is usually true. More thinking about an unresolvable question adds noise, not clarity. Clarity often comes from thinking differently, or from not thinking about that subject for a while.

A mind that never rests has a cost: a low-grade state of alarm, sleep and health that suffer, relationships in which you are less present, and, most painfully, prayer that becomes another venue for worry. Naming the cost is not a judgement on you. It is the first step towards taking your own peace seriously.

One misunderstanding will undermine everything if you carry it with you: "If I really trusted God, my mind would be quiet." Trusting God and having a quiet mind are related, but they are not the same thing. Many of the most trusting people in Scripture had very loud minds; David's psalms are full of them, and Jesus was "greatly distressed and troubled" in Gethsemane. A restless mind does not, by itself, prove that you do not trust God. It may show that you are tired, frightened, overwhelmed or carrying more than you were meant to carry alone; in some situations it may point to a condition that deserves professional attention. Trust is revealed in what you do with your restless mind: where you bring your fears, whom you let support you, which conclusions you refuse without evidence, and what faithful step you take next. Trust may eventually produce a greater sense of calm, but trust itself is a direction before it becomes a feeling.

### A Scene You May Recognise

Let me describe a woman I will call Grace. She is a faithful believer, a reliable colleague and a loving mother. Nobody at church would guess what her evenings are like.

At about ten o’clock she switches off the light. By quarter past, she has replayed a short exchange with her manager three times, each time hearing a different tone in his voice. By half past, she is composing an email she will not send. By eleven, she has moved on to her son’s exam results, which are not due for another month, and from there to a bill that is not late, and from there to a comment her sister made in 2019.

At some point she remembers to pray. She does. Then she opens her eyes and the meeting resumes exactly where it left off.

Grace is not lazy. She is not unspiritual. She is exhausted, and she is ashamed, because she believes that a stronger Christian would simply hand all of this to God and fall asleep.

I have sat with many Graces over the past twenty years. Some are men who would never use the word “anxious” about themselves but who lie awake calculating. Some are pastors. Some are teenagers. Some have walked with Christ for fifty years. What they have in common is not weak faith. What they have in common is a mind that has learnt to run without a destination.

### Central Scripture: Psalm 94:19

When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul. (Psalm 94:19, ESV) I want you to notice two things about this verse before we go any further.

First, the psalmist does not say, “When I had cares.” He says, “When the cares of my heart are many.” The Hebrew word translated “cares” can describe anxious or disquieting thoughts—the inner concerns that multiply until the mind feels crowded. This is a man describing a crowded mind. He is not confessing a sin. He is describing a condition.

Second, notice where relief comes from. Not from the cares being resolved. Not from the psalmist thinking his way to an answer. Relief comes from God’s consolations, the comforts God himself supplies, and they reach him while the cares are still many. The verse does not say the cares departed and then his soul was cheered. It says both were true at once.

That is the pattern of this whole course. Peace does not wait at the far end of a solved problem. God’s comfort can arrive in the middle of a mind that is still full.

### What Overthinking Feels Like

Overthinking is difficult to define because from the inside it rarely feels like a problem. It feels like diligence. It feels like caring. It feels like being responsible.

But there are some recognisable marks.

The same subject keeps returning. You reach a conclusion, and then twenty minutes later you are reconsidering it from the beginning, as though the earlier thinking never happened.

You are always somewhere else. Your body is at dinner, but your mind is at tomorrow’s meeting. Your body is at church, but your mind is at last Tuesday’s argument.

Rest feels unsafe. When your mind finally goes quiet, a small alarm sounds: What have I forgotten? What am I not preparing for? So the thinking starts again, not because there is anything new to think about, but because silence feels like negligence.

You feel tired in a particular way. Not the clean tiredness of a day’s work, but a foggy, heavy tiredness that sleep does not seem to fix, because the mind was working all night.

Small things become large. A neutral text message becomes a verdict on the friendship. A raised eyebrow becomes a rejection. A delayed reply becomes a crisis.

If you recognise yourself in three or four of these, keep reading. You are exactly who this course was written for.

### Useful Thinking and Mental Circling

I want to be careful here, because I do not want you to become afraid of your own mind.

Thinking is a gift. Scripture commands it. “Consider,” “ponder,” “meditate,” “examine,” “test” all appear on the pages of the Bible as things God’s people are told to do. Joseph planned for famine. Nehemiah surveyed the walls before rebuilding. Jesus told his followers to count the cost before building a tower. God is not against careful thought.

The difference between useful thinking and mental circling is not the subject and not the intensity. It is the direction.

Useful thinking moves. It gathers, weighs, decides and then hands over to action or to rest. It has an end.

Mental circling moves too, but in a loop. It revisits, re-examines, re- imagines and re-worries, and it ends only when exhaustion or distraction interrupts it. Nothing is decided. Nothing is released.

A simple way to tell them apart is to ask: Where is this thought taking me? If the honest answer is “back to where I started,” you are circling.

### Why the Mind Keeps Returning to the Same Issue

Why does your mind go back to something you have already considered many times?

Usually, the thinking continues because it has not produced what the mind is reaching for. Sometimes that is missing information— there are decisions that really do need one more fact before they can be settled. But very often, especially after the same facts have been reviewed repeatedly, what the mind is seeking is not more information but certainty.

The mind is a remarkable problem-solver. Give it a task with a solution, and it works until it finds one, and then it stops. But give it a task without a solution—Will they still like me? Will the job be safe? Did I make the right choice five years ago?—and it does not know how to stop. It keeps searching for the answer that would let it rest, and since that answer does not exist, it keeps searching.

This is why “I have already thought about this” does not settle anything. The mind is not returning because it lacks thought. It is returning because it lacks certainty, and it has confused the two.

There is often something else underneath as well, which we will explore in Lesson 4: a fear. The repetitive thought is frequently a guard standing in front of something the mind does not want to look at directly. Grace is not really thinking about her manager’s tone. She is guarding against the fear of being found inadequate. Until that fear is named, the thoughts will keep circling it.

### Why More Thinking Does Not Always Produce More Clarity

Many overthinkers believe that if they could just think hard enough, long enough, or carefully enough, the fog would clear.

The opposite is usually true.

After a certain point, additional thinking about an unresolvable question does not add clarity. It adds noise. Each new pass introduces new possibilities to fear, new angles to consider, new imaginary versions of other people’s reactions. The picture gets more crowded, not clearer.

Consider how you would advise a friend who told you they had spent four hours deciding whether their pastor was upset with them. You would not say, “Give it another four hours.” You would say, “You have thought enough. Either ask him or let it go.” You would recognise instantly that more thinking is not the solution. It is much harder to see this from the inside.

Clarity often comes not from thinking more but from thinking differently, and sometimes from not thinking about that subject at all for a while. Some of the wisest decisions I have made came after a night’s sleep, a walk, or an honest conversation with someone I trust, not after another hour of analysis.

### The Cost of a Mind That Never Rests

I do not raise this to frighten you, but because many Christians underestimate what a restless mind is costing them, and so they never take it seriously enough to seek change.

Emotionally, a mind that never rests lives in a low-grade state of alarm. Joy becomes difficult, because you are always braced. Gratitude becomes difficult, because you are always scanning for what could go wrong next.

Physically, chronic mental agitation affects sleep, appetite, energy, concentration and, over time, health. Repeatedly imagining a threatening situation can activate some of the same bodily stress responses that occur when a person faces an immediate threat.

Relationally, overthinking makes you less present with the people you love. It also makes you more likely to misread them, since a mind that is constantly analysing will find things to analyse.

Spiritually, the cost is perhaps the most painful. It can become difficult to attend to God when your own thoughts never stop demanding attention. Prayer becomes another venue for worry. Scripture reading becomes a hunt for reassurance rather than a meeting with God. And shame accumulates, because you know this is not the peace Jesus spoke of, and you do not know how to get there.

If any of that describes you, please hear me: naming the cost is not a judgement on you. It is the first step towards taking your own peace seriously.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I really trusted God, my mind would be quiet.”

We will spend all of Lesson 3 on this, but I want to say something briefly now, because if you carry this belief into the rest of the course it will undermine everything.

Trusting God and having a quiet mind are related, but they are not the same thing. Many of the most trusting people in Scripture had very loud minds. David’s psalms are full of them. Paul wrote of being “burdened beyond our strength” and of “fighting without and fear within.” Jesus himself was “greatly distressed and troubled” in Gethsemane.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that are true for you, adapt them or remain silent.*

Father, you already know what I have been thinking about. You know the meetings my mind holds without my permission. You know how tired I am.

I have sometimes believed that you were disappointed in me for this. Today I choose to bring my crowded mind to you instead of hiding it.

I do not ask you to make me stop thinking. I ask you to teach me to think with you, and to rest in you while the questions are still open.

When the cares of my heart are many, let your consolations cheer my soul.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
