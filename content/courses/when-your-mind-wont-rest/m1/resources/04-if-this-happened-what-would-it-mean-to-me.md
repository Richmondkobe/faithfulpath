# "If This Happened, What Would It Mean to Me?"

*Worksheet 4 in the printed toolkit · Lesson 4*

> **NOT FOR GROUP SHARING.** This exercise is optional and private. You are not required to disclose your answers to a group leader, pastor, spouse or other participant.
>
> It is not a method for recovering memories, and whatever comes to mind is not a message from God or a definitive explanation. Ask the downward question no more than three times, and stop earlier if nothing comes or discomfort rises. "No deeper fear appeared" is a complete result. Do not use it with trauma memories, do not use it in the middle of the night, and do not repeat it to find a deeper answer. Present danger requires safety action, not deeper interpretation.

> **Stop the exercise if** you become overwhelmed, disconnected from your surroundings, panicked or unable to return your attention to the present. Put the page aside, notice where you are, contact someone safe and seek appropriate professional support. You do not need to uncover the deepest explanation today.

Choose one repeat visitor—the thought that has returned most often. Complete this exercise once for that thought, and then stop. Do not repeat it on the same thought looking for a better answer.

Step 1. Begin with prayer. Read Psalm 139:23–24 and ask God for honesty, wisdom and gentleness as you consider whether the surface concern carries a deeper meaning.

Step 2. Write the surface thought. In one line, what is the thought about? (Samuel: The elders will not be happy with the budget.)

Step 3. Note the practical action. What reasonable action, if any, does the surface problem still require of you? Write it down so it is not lost. It will need its own attention.

Step 4. Ask the question and write the answer. If this happened, what would it mean to me?

Step 5. Ask it again, of your answer. And if that were true, what would it mean to me?

Step 6. Ask again, until the sentence changes. Keep going, no more than five times, until the sentence is no longer about the situation and is about you, your worth, your safety, your future, or God. If no deeper sentence appears, that is a legitimate result. The surface problem may be the whole issue.

Step 7. Name the fear, if there is one. Look at the list of common fears. Which one is this closest to? Write it in your own words: I am afraid that…

Step 8. Notice any known connection. (Optional.) Does this fear remind you of a situation or relationship you already remember? If so, note it briefly. If not, leave this step blank. You do not need to discover where a fear began before you can respond to it wisely.

Step 9. Bring it to God as it is. Write a one-line prayer that names the fear directly. Not only Help me with the budget, but Father, I am afraid of being set aside.

Step 10. Decide who else should know. Name one person who could hear this fear from you. If the fear feels too heavy to carry with a friend, that is a sign to speak with a pastor or counsellor.

Keep this page. Module 3 will return to the fear you have named and show you how to test it against the truth.

---

*Complete this once for one matter, then stop. If you find yourself redoing it to feel certain the answer is right, that is a signal to put it down, not to try again. This worksheet does not treat an anxiety disorder, depression, OCD or trauma; if a professional is supporting you, their guidance takes priority.*
