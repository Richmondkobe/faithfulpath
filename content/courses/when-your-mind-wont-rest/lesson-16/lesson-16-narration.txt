Welcome to Lesson 16. Here is today's question. Have you ever finished praying more frightened than when you began? You knelt to hand something over, and forty minutes later you rose with your heart racing. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one short prayer, so find a quiet place if you can.

Here is what you will learn today: how to notice when prayer has become rehearsal, and a six-movement shape that lets a prayer come to rest, without limiting persistent prayer. Two things first. Prayers or phrases repeated until they feel right, or a prayer you cannot stop because stopping feels dangerous, deserve help from a professional who understands both faith and obsessive-compulsive symptoms. And being unable to pray at all is a common experience in depression and grief. It is not a spiritual verdict, and persistent low mood deserves a doctor's assessment. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is Psalm 62, verse 8, in the English Standard Version. "Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us." Read the whole psalm and notice how it begins: "For God alone my soul waits in silence." Alongside the pouring out there is waiting, and the waiting is silent. Then verse 8 gives the instruction to everyone: trust, pour out, refuge. It says pour: everything, unedited. And the pouring is held between trust and refuge. The heart is poured out before someone, and that someone receives it. The psalm is not a six-step method; what follows is a pastoral practice drawn from its themes.

Let me tell you about a woman I will call Elizabeth. She prayed for her daughter every night, and every night she finished more frightened than she began. Her daughter, in her first year at university, was struggling with the course, with loneliness, and with a relationship that had ended badly. Elizabeth did what she had always done and took it to God. But her prayers went like this: she would tell God what was wrong, and in the telling remember another thing that was wrong, and imagine what might happen if that got worse, and ask him to prevent it, and wonder whether he would, and whether she had prayed enough. By the end of forty minutes she had walked through every possible disaster and risen with a heart racing faster than when she knelt. "I think I am praying," she said. "But it feels like worrying with my eyes closed." Her prayer contained real love and real intercession. What it had lost was the shape that lets a prayer come to rest.

Some patterns are worth recognising. Prayer that addresses God but talks to itself: the words go upward while the mind rehearses and predicts, and adding "Lord" to the front of each sentence does not change what the sentence is doing. Prayer that circles without lament or trust: the psalms lament, but they do not rehearse imagined futures in ever-greater detail. Prayer that seeks a guarantee: "promise me she will be all right." God gives many firm promises, but not every desired outcome, and a prayer asking for what is not on offer cannot rest. Prayer that cannot come to rest. And prayer that replaces action or help, when what a situation needs is a phone call, a conversation, or a doctor.

Repeating a request is not evidence of unbelief. Jesus told a parable so that his disciples ought always to pray and not lose heart. In Gethsemane he prayed the same request repeatedly, and Paul pleaded three times about his thorn. The question is what the repetition is doing. Persistent prayer keeps asking without losing heart, open to God's will. Lament returns honestly to unresolved pain. Intercession carries an ongoing need day after day; Elizabeth's nightly prayer was intercession, and it was right. Compulsive repetition repeats in order to neutralise fear or make the prayer feel complete, driven by the sense that stopping is dangerous. The first three are healthy expressions of faith. The fourth may need professional help. The same words can belong to any of them. What distinguishes them is what they are for.

"If I still feel anxious after praying, I did not pray properly, or God did not hear me." This turns prayer into a performance measured by a feeling. Scripture does not measure it that way. What prayer does is bring us, and what troubles us, into the presence of God, where the trouble can be seen for what it is, and where we are reminded of who he is. Sometimes that produces peace quickly. Sometimes the peace comes later, or as steadiness rather than calm. The prayer was still prayer.

So here is the shape, drawn from the themes Psalm 62 holds together. It is not a formula. It is somewhere for a prayer to go when it keeps turning into rehearsal. One: become present. Be still for a moment before you speak; you are coming before a person who already knows what you will say. Two: name the reality. State what is actually known, in a sentence or two. Not the chain of consequences; the event. Three: pour out your heart. Say honestly what you feel: fear, grief, anger, desire. Do not tidy it. God has read the psalms too. Four: make your request. Ask specifically, and remain open to God's will. You may ask again tomorrow; that is intercession. What you need not do is ask again within the same prayer to feel that the asking has taken. Five: remember and discern. Return your attention to what Scripture clearly says, and name what is yours to do today: act, wait, seek help, or surrender. Six: entrust the outcome. Place what you cannot control into God's care, deliberately and in words, and then rise and do the thing from movement five. The refuge is real whether or not you feel it yet.

The whole of this can take five minutes, or much longer if the pouring out is heavy. Length does not determine faithfulness. Notice where you tend to stay. Many who pray anxiously spend most of the prayer pouring out and asking, and rarely reach remembering and entrusting. The answer is not to pour out less. It is to let the prayer continue. What you are watching for is not repetition, but the sense that you must repeat, that stopping is dangerous, that the prayer has not worked until a feeling arrives. And use the psalms. If you cannot find words, they have found them for you.

Now it is your turn. Pray the six movements once, for the matter that has been turning your prayers into worry. Be still. Name what is actually known. Pour out your heart, untidied. Make your request, once. Remember what God has said, and name what is yours to do today. Then entrust the outcome, in words. When you have finished, do the thing from movement five before the day ends. If that thing was surrender, do something ordinary instead, and let the entrusting stand, knowing you may return to it tomorrow. Pause the audio now and pray. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. Pour out your heart. Then let the prayer continue to its rest. God is a refuge, and a refuge is somewhere you arrive, not somewhere you circle. Entrusting is not a door that closes behind you. You may come back tomorrow with the same need.

Let us close. Father, I have poured out my heart before you. You have heard it the first time. I place what I cannot control in your care, deliberately, and I rise to do what is mine today. You are my refuge, whether or not I feel it yet. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
