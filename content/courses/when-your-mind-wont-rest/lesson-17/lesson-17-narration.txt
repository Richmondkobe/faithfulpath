Welcome to Lesson 17. Here is today's question. How many evenings have you spent in a house you never had to move to? Some of us have lived whole months in the future, and none of them arrived. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to tell proportionate preparation from mentally living in an imagined future, and how to sort one concern into Today, Later, or Release for now. One thing first. If tomorrow contains a real and present hardship, money that will not stretch, housing that is not secure, a situation in which you are not safe, then the "today" list may be long, and its actions may need other people: a debt adviser, a housing service, a doctor, a solicitor, a domestic-abuse service. Seeking that help is preparation of the wisest kind. The support link is under this lesson.

Our passage is Matthew 6, verses 25 to 34, in the English Standard Version. "Look at the birds of the air: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they?" Jesus is speaking to people who were not wealthy about anxiety over provision, and he answers not with a technique but with the Father. The birds are fed, not because they are idle, but because their lives are sustained by him rather than secured by anxious mastery of the future. This is not a guarantee of uninterrupted material security; Scripture knows famine, and Jesus himself had nowhere to lay his head. Only then does verse 34 arrive: tomorrow will be anxious for itself. He assumes trouble, and declines to let tomorrow's be added to today's.

Let me tell you about a man I will call Philip. He lived through the same eleven weeks every evening for a month, and none of them had happened yet. His firm had announced a restructuring; roles like his might be merged, though nobody had said his would be. Philip did the sensible things quickly. He updated his CV, spoke to his manager, and sat down with his wife to look at what the household could manage. That took an afternoon. Then, each evening after the children were in bed, he went further: the meeting in which he was told, the months of applications, the savings running down, the smaller house, the conversation with his son about changing schools. "I keep telling myself I'm being prepared," he said. "But I've prepared for the same thing forty times, and I'm no more ready than I was the first night. I'm just more frightened."

The question is not whether to think about tomorrow, but how to tell preparing from living in it, because from the inside the two feel similar. Preparation moves towards a result: clearer understanding, a decision, a plan, a next step. Preparation has an end; living in tomorrow does not finish, because the future has no edge. Preparation works with what is established: the restructuring was established, the loss of his role was not. Preparation leaves you with a next step; the person who has lived through it forty times knows in detail what they will feel and has no clearer idea what they will do. And preparation may include emotion, but it does not consist of it.

"If I really trusted God, I would not think about tomorrow at all." This usually harms the most responsible people in a congregation: the parent with the budget, the carer. They hear "do not be anxious about tomorrow," conclude that their planning is unbelief, and add guilt to the concern they already carried. Jesus is not forbidding thought about the future. He is forbidding a particular relationship to it: taking up tomorrow's trouble as though it were already today's. Philip had the opposite version: "If I do not think it through now, I will be caught out later." So I asked him: did the fortieth rehearsal find you readier than the first? He was honest enough to say it had not.

When you notice that you have moved into tomorrow, everything you are carrying there can be placed in one of three groups. But a check comes first. Is anything here urgent? Before sorting, ask whether the tomorrow you have been living in contains credible danger or immediate need: abuse, violence, stalking, imminent homelessness, a medical symptom that should not wait, a child at risk. If it does, that item does not go into any group. It goes to the top of the page, and the response is to seek safety or qualified help now. This lesson is about worry that outruns the evidence, not about danger the evidence supports. A course must never be the reason someone treated a real threat as overthinking.

Then sort into three. Today: some of what you carry is a genuine responsibility that exists now and can be acted on today. The response is to act, or to seek help, and then to stop, because it has been done. Later: some will require action, but not yet. It waits for a date, a decision by someone else, a result. Write it down with the point at which it becomes today's business; the mind lets go more readily when it knows the thing is kept somewhere. Release for now: some is not presently actionable, because it depends on something that has not happened, or lies beyond your role altogether. Release means release from present mental work, not a declaration that the matter will never need attention. The smaller house belonged here. Every evening Philip spent in its rooms was taken from the house he was in.

Three cautions about sorting honestly. Do not use "later" to avoid what belongs to today. If a frightening phone call could be made now, it is today's, and putting it on the later list is avoidance wearing the clothes of wisdom. Do not use "release" to avoid "seek help". If the future you are living in is financial or legal or medical and you lack the knowledge to sort it, the right group is often "today", and the action is to ask a qualified person. And do not measure the sorting by how you feel afterwards. Correct sorting may not make you calmer, and continued anxiety does not prove an item was placed wrongly. In practice, notice the crossing, and name where you actually are: it is a Tuesday evening in September and I am in my own kitchen. That is not a rebuke. It is a location.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Take the tomorrow you have been living in. Run the urgency check first; if it finds danger or immediate need, stop the exercise and seek help or safety now. Otherwise, write three short columns: Today, Later, Release for now. Then take one Today item and, if it is safe and realistically possible, do it today; if it needs an appointment or another person, schedule it, and do not treat the delay as failure. If there is nothing for Today, return deliberately to one ordinary part of the day you are actually in. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. Every evening spent in tomorrow's house is taken from the one you are in. When tomorrow arrives again in your mind, name where you are, check whether anything has changed, and give it back again. It becomes briefer with practice; it does not become unnecessary. What Philip regretted was not the concern, which had been reasonable, but the thirty evenings spent in a house he never had to move to, while the children he was afraid of failing were asking for him in the actual one.

Let us pray. Father, you feed the birds, and I am worth more than they. Here is what is today's; give me strength to do it. Here is what is later; keep it for me until its time. The rest I give back to you, for now, and I return to the day you have actually given me. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
