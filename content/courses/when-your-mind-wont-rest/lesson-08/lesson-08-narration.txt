Welcome to Lesson 8. Here is today's question. Have you ever won an argument with someone who was not in the room? In the shower, on the drive, in the hour before sleep. You said the perfect thing, and they had no answer. And then the real person turned up. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to separate what a person actually said from what you imagined, how to prepare only your own part, and how to go into a conversation intending to hear. One caution before we start. If the person you are rehearsing for has been abusive, coercive or unsafe, do not use this lesson to prepare direct contact. Take that question to a domestic-abuse service, a safeguarding professional, or another qualified person first. The wise decision may be to have no conversation at all. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is Proverbs 18, verse 13, in the English Standard Version. "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame." The proverb describes the interrupter, the person composing a reply while you are still speaking. I am applying it by analogy, and I will say so plainly: rehearsing a conversation is answering before the other person has had a chance to speak. The proverb does not condemn preparation. It warns against answering before hearing.

Let me tell you about a woman I will call Hannah. She spent most of a fortnight preparing for a family lunch. The previous visit had ended with a remark from her mother-in-law about how Hannah's children were being raised, and Hannah had been composing her reply ever since. In the shower she explained calmly why it had hurt. By the second week she had rehearsed versions where her mother-in-law wept, versions where she stormed out, and one, late at night, where Hannah said something so precise that the older woman had nothing left to say. She held the conversation forty times, and won it thirty-eight. On the day, her mother-in-law met her at the door, hugged her, and said, "I've been thinking about what I said last time. I'm sorry. It wasn't my place." Hannah noticed she had been cold to her for the first hour, responding to remarks that had never been made. "I had a whole argument with a person who wasn't there," she said. "And then the real person turned up, and I treated her as if she'd said all of it."

Rehearsal comes in a few forms. The confrontation, in which the imagined person grows more unreasonable while you grow more eloquent. The defence, an answer to the criticism, then to the follow-up, then to the follow-up to that. And the explanation: an apology or a piece of bad news rehearsed for the perfect wording, which never arrives, so the conversation keeps being postponed. There is also the replay with edits, going back and improving a conversation that already happened; that one belongs with Lesson 7.

Here is the main truth about what rehearsal costs. It supplies evidence that does not exist. Hannah felt she knew how her mother-in-law would respond, and had in fact invented it. It fixes your interpretation before you have heard theirs, and it makes you arrive already in the middle of an argument. Most often, and least noticed, it replaces the conversation altogether. Rehearsing an explanation for the perfect wording is a way of not giving it. Rehearsing a confrontation can release just enough feeling that you never raise the matter at all. And it costs the present: the shower, the drive, the hour before sleep, given to someone who is not in the room.

Not all thinking ahead is this kind of rehearsal. Three things look alike. Preparation concerns what belongs to you: your facts, your purpose, your opening words, your questions, your boundaries. It has a natural end point, and it leaves you ready to listen. Bounded planning considers a small number of realistic responses so you can stay clear, safe and flexible. For some conversations that is simply responsible: an interview, a disciplinary meeting, a talk with someone who commonly turns things around. Rehearsal is different. It scripts the other person, treats the script as evidence, and continues long after useful preparation has ended. The test is not whether you imagine a response. It is whether your preparation stays bounded, based on evidence, and flexible. Hannah's fortnight was none of those.

"If I do not rehearse, I will be caught out." There is real wisdom in this. Scripture commends thinking before answering, and some conversations deserve careful preparation. The fault is in what the preparation becomes. Done rightly, it clarifies your part and holds the other person's part as an open question. And gently, there is a matter of trust here. Repeated rehearsal can act as though the outcome depended entirely on the precision of your wording. It does not. Your responsibility is faithfulness and clarity, not control of another person's response. The outcome rests on truth, on the relationship, on the other person's heart, and on God.

Here is the tool. One: notice, and name it. "I am rehearsing." Two: ask what this person has actually said to you about this. Often the honest answer is "one remark, a fortnight ago," and everything since has been you. Three: decide whether a real conversation is needed. If it is, choose a time and a method rather than postponing in search of perfect wording; the wisest method may be a private conversation, a written message, a mediator, or a proper process. Four: prepare only what belongs to you. For an ordinary relational conversation, two or three opening sentences and the one thing you most want understood are usually enough. When your part is prepared, the preparation is finished. Five: go in intending to hear. Let the other person finish, and ask a genuine question before forming your response. When the rehearsal comes back, tell yourself: my part is prepared; their part is not mine to write.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Take the conversation you have been rehearsing most. Write only what the other person has actually said to you about it, and when. Not what you imagine they think. Only their words, as best you remember them; if it was a look or a silence, write that and note you are interpreting it. Then write one line. Either, "Everything after that was mine." Or, "They have said more than one thing, and a real conversation is needed," in which case write the day you will ask for it. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. My part is prepared. Their part is not mine to write. A last word for anyone polishing an apology that is never quite ready: the perfect wording does not exist. A good apology names the harm honestly, makes no excuses, respects the other person's boundaries, and does not demand forgiveness. That deserves careful preparation. What it does not need is forty rehearsals of how it will be received.

Let us pray. Father, you know the conversations I have been holding alone, and the people I have been arguing with who were not there. Give me words for my part, and ears for theirs. Keep me from answering before I have heard. The outcome is yours, not mine. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
