# Lesson 19: Peace Before Answers

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to name the condition you have placed on rest, separate necessary watching from anxious watching, and resume one part of ordinary life before the answer comes.

## Before you begin

Waiting for a serious answer — a diagnosis, a result, a verdict, news of someone you love — is one of the hardest things a human being does, and heightened anxiety during such a wait is an ordinary response, not a spiritual failure. If anxiety or disrupted sleep is severe, persistent, worsening or interfering with daily functioning, tell your doctor or the clinical team; you do not need to wait a particular number of weeks before asking for help, and you can ask the clinical team whether support is available for patients' families or carers. If you are waiting for an answer about your own health, ask the clinician what information can responsibly be given, when results are expected and whom to contact if they are delayed; ask once and keep the answer somewhere accessible rather than repeatedly checking. If the thing you are waiting to know concerns someone's safety, or yours, that is a matter for present action and qualified help, not for rest. If what you are waiting for is a sense that God has forgiven you, or a certainty that you are truly saved, and no assurance ever seems to settle it, that may be the scrupulosity earlier lessons described; a pastor who understands it, working with a mental-health professional, can help in a way that more reassurance cannot. Nothing in this lesson asks you to give up necessary monitoring or care. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for one wait.

## Key Scripture: John 14:27 (ESV)

> Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you. Not as the world gives do I give to you. Let not your hearts be troubled, neither let them be afraid.

The night before the crucifixion, in the upper room. Jesus has told the disciples that one will betray him, that Peter will deny him, and that he is going where they cannot follow. Every question that mattered to them was open.

## One truth to carry with you

Christ gave his peace to the disciples on the night when their answers were furthest away. He did not say, "You will have peace once you understand." Every question that mattered to them was open, and about to become more frightening, and it is into that room that he says: my peace I give to you. So the peace of Christ is not certainty that the answer will be favourable, and it is not an immediate feeling of calm. It is the security of belonging to him and not being left alone while the answer remains unknown. That security can support sleep, relationship, worship and ordinary life. But difficulty doing those things is not proof that you have failed to receive it. If you belong to Christ, you are his, and he will not leave you while the answer remains open.

## The lesson

A man I will call Caleb told me he would be able to breathe again in six weeks, and then, when the six weeks had passed, that it would be another three months. His wife had finished cancer treatment, and the scan that would show whether it had worked was six weeks out. Caleb had stopped sleeping well, stopped coming to the midweek group, and stopped, he admitted, being much of a husband, because he was so busy being a sentry. "I can't rest until we know," he said. "It would be like leaving my post." The scan, when it came, was encouraging but not conclusive, and the oncologist wanted another in three months. I asked what he would do if that one was also inconclusive. He did not answer, because he could see where the question went: the rest he was postponing until he knew was rest he would never take.

It is the night before the crucifixion. Jesus has washed the disciples' feet, told them that one will betray him and that Peter will deny him, and promised the Helper. Although he had spoken about his death and resurrection, they had not understood what the next day would bring. Many of the questions pressing on them remained open, and the following day would make their circumstances more frightening before they understood what he had promised. It is into that room that he says, my peace I give to you. He does not say, "You will have peace once you understand." He gives peace before their answers, on the night those answers were furthest away.

Three things about the verse deserve care. The peace is **his**: "my peace". It is not a technique for producing calm or a feeling generated by trusting harder; it is bound up with his presence, his promise to come to them, and the Spirit he will send, and so it is relational before it is emotional. "**Not as the world gives**": the world often offers peace that depends on favourable conditions or the removal of threat, while Jesus gives peace while trouble remains. And "**let not your hearts be troubled**" is an instruction spoken by someone whom John twice describes as troubled in spirit; as Lesson 3 said, it addresses what we do with fear, not whether our pulse rises. These words were first spoken within the unique setting of Jesus' departure and the Spirit's coming, and the application is not that every reader must feel calm but that Christ's people are not left orphaned when circumstances remain unresolved.

Because the word will carry the rest of the lesson, let me define it. In this lesson, the peace of Christ means a settled relationship with God and a sustaining security grounded in Christ's presence and promises. It may produce emotional calm, but it is not identical to calm, and it can coexist with grief, bodily anxiety, unanswered questions and responsible action.

Caleb's sentence was "I can't rest until we know," and most people who cannot rest have one like it: *I will permit myself peace when I know that ______.* The structure feels responsible, and is often drawn from real love, but it has problems that appear only with time. **The answer keeps moving**: answers arrive in sequence, so the scan leads to another scan, and the horizon moves back by as far as you walked towards it. **Some answers never come** — whether you did the right thing, whether someone who has died knew how you felt — and if rest is conditional on those, rest has been refused permanently. **The condition trains the mind to scan**, which neither shortens the wait nor improves the outcome. **Deferred rest becomes a deferred life.** If Caleb's scans were good for twenty years, he would have spent twenty years at his post, and the life he was guarding was the life he was not living.

That needs one careful distinction, because a lesson about rest can be misheard as a lesson against care. **Necessary watching** follows medical or safety guidance, has a defined purpose, produces information or action, can be scheduled or shared, and supports the person receiving care, because it *is* care. **Anxious watching** attempts to control an outcome that cannot be known, scans continuously rather than at defined points, produces repeated imagined scenarios rather than information, and over time removes the carer from the ordinary presence the person actually needs. Caleb was doing both and could not tell them apart, because both felt like love. When he separated them on paper, the necessary watching took a few lines; the anxious watching was the rest of his waking hours. Nothing here asks anyone to give up the first kind.

"Resting before it is resolved would be irresponsible. If I stop watching, it means I do not care." This made Caleb a sentry, and it has a moral force that is hard to argue with. But the anxious watching did not read the scan or adjust the treatment; appropriate monitoring and practical care still mattered, and followed the clinical team's guidance. What the extra hours did was remove Caleb from the meals, the evenings and the ordinary tenderness that were the actual content of the months they had. That watching was not care. Care was what it was preventing. And Scripture does not connect love with sleeplessness: the psalmist places sleep among God's gifts, and Jesus slept in the stern of the boat during a storm.

So the practice. **Find the sentence** and write it exactly, including the part that feels unreasonable on paper; Caleb's, once he was honest, was "when I know she will not die of this", and no scan could give him that. **Ask what is actually yours**: the dates, then two lists — the monitoring the professionals have recommended, and the vigilance that produces no useful action. The first is care; the second can be set down. **Separate unresolved from settled**: on one side what you do not know, on the other what is actually established, including what Christ has given you that no outcome changes. **Review what has been suspended**, and choose one safe, realistic part of ordinary life to resume this week. **Use the horizon test**: if this answer came, and came well, what would be the next "when I know"? **And come to Christ rather than manufacturing peace**, through the psalms, the gathered church, or the Six-Movement Prayer of Lesson 16. These direct attention towards him; they do not guarantee a felt result.

Caleb came back to the midweek group the following Wednesday. He did not feel better. He said afterwards that it was the first evening in two months he had not spent at his post, and that his wife had noticed.

There would be years of them, God willing. The rest he was postponing until he knew was rest he would never take, because the knowing he required was not on offer, not this year and probably not ever.

Consider what the disciples did not know that night. They did not know how the next day would go. They did not know that the cross was coming, or that resurrection was; Jesus had told them, and they had not understood. They did not know what “going to the Father” meant, or when they would see him again, or what the Helper would be like. Every question that mattered to them was open. Jesus’ own mission and intention were not uncertain to him; his hour had come and he knew it. But from the disciples’ perspective, almost everything appeared unresolved and was about to become more frightening, and it is into that room, on that night, that he says: my peace I give to you.

He does not say, “You will have peace once you understand.” He does not say, “After the resurrection you will see, and then your hearts will be untroubled.” He gives peace before their answers, on the night when those answers were furthest away.

Three things about this verse deserve care.

First, the peace is his. “My peace.” It is not a technique for producing calm, and it is not a feeling the disciples generated by trusting harder. It is something Jesus possesses and gives, and in this passage it is bound up with his presence, his promise to come to them, and the Spirit he will send. The peace is relational before it is emotional.

Second, “not as the world gives.” Jesus does not explain the comparison fully here, and I do not want to fill in more than he says. As a pastoral contrast, the world often offers peace that depends on favourable conditions, on control, or on the removal of threat. Jesus gives peace while trouble remains, and grounds it in his relationship with the disciples and the promised Spirit. Whatever his peace is, it does not wait for the world’s conditions.

Jesus is not scolding them for being afraid on the night he is arrested.

One more thing must be said before this verse is applied to anyone reading it now. These words were first spoken to the disciples within the unique setting of Jesus’ departure and the promised coming of the Spirit. Christians today receive them within that same new-covenant relationship: through Christ and the indwelling Spirit, they belong to the community for whom his presence and peace endure. ### What This Chapter Means by Peace

Because the word will carry the rest of the chapter, it needs defining before it is used.

In this chapter, the peace of Christ means a settled relationship with God and a sustaining security grounded in Christ’s presence and promises. It may produce emotional calm, but it is not identical to calm. It can coexist with grief, bodily anxiety, unanswered questions and responsible action.

Put another way: the peace of Christ is not certainty that the answer will be favourable, and it is not an immediate feeling of calm. It is the security of belonging to Christ and not being left alone while the answer remains unknown. That security can support sleep, relationship, worship and ordinary life. But difficulty doing those things is not proof that a person has failed to receive it.

Hold that definition through what follows, because much of the distress in this area comes from measuring a relationship by a feeling.

### The Moving Horizon

Caleb’s sentence was “I can’t rest until we know.” Most people who cannot rest have a sentence like it, and the sentence usually has this shape: I will permit myself peace when I know that ______.

When the child is settled. When the results come. When the money is sorted. When I know whether the relationship will survive. When I know if I did the right thing. When the church is stable. When I am sure they are not angry with me. When I know how it ends.

The structure feels responsible, and sometimes it is drawn from real love. But it has several problems that become clear only with time.

The answer keeps moving. Answers arrive in sequence, not all at once. The scan leads to another scan. The child who settles has a child of her own. The money that is sorted this year needs sorting again. Each answer, when it comes, reveals the next thing to be known, and the horizon moves back by exactly as far as you walked toward it. Caleb had lived this within three months. Some people have lived it for thirty years.

Some answers never come. Whether you did the right thing. Whether a person who has died knew how you felt. Whether a decision would have gone differently. How a story ends when you will not be there for the ending. If rest is conditional on those, rest has been refused permanently, and usually without the person having decided to refuse it.

The condition trains the mind to keep scanning. A mind that has been told “we rest when we know” learns that its job, until then, is to watch. It becomes very good at watching. It watches during meals and at night and in church, and this kind of watching does not shorten the wait or improve the outcome. It only ensures that the person is exhausted when the answer finally arrives.

Deferred rest becomes a deferred life. Caleb had suspended sleep, fellowship, reading and ordinary marriage until an answer that would not come. If the scans were good for twenty years, he would have spent twenty years at his post. The life he was guarding was the life he was not living.

I want to be careful here, because there are situations in which rest is truly difficult, and this chapter must not blame anyone for that. A parent whose child is in hospital does not sleep well, and should not be told that poor sleep proves a lack of faith. The point is not that concern should be absent. It is that concern and rest are not the alternatives Caleb thought they were. He could fear for his wife and still eat, still sleep when sleep came, still sit in the midweek group, still be a husband. His love and practical support mattered a great deal; but continuous mental vigilance was not the treatment keeping her medically safe, and his exhaustion was making it harder for him to offer the presence and care that were genuinely his to give.

### Two Kinds of Watching

That distinction needs to be drawn carefully, because a chapter about rest can be misheard as a chapter against care.

There is watching that is necessary. It follows medical or safety guidance. It has a defined purpose: a symptom the clinical team has asked you to report, a medication schedule, a follow-up to book, a warning sign to act on. It produces information or action. It can be shared with others or scheduled into the day. And it supports the person who is receiving care, because it is care.

There is also watching that is anxious. It attempts to control an outcome that cannot be known. It scans continuously rather than at defined points. It produces not information but repeated imagined scenarios, the chain of Lesson 6 run again and again. It feels impossible to stop; stopping feels like desertion. And, over time, it removes the carer from the ordinary presence the person actually needs.

Caleb was doing both, and could not tell them apart, because both felt like love. When he separated them on paper, the necessary watching took up a few lines: the appointment dates, two symptoms he had been asked to report, the pharmacy. The anxious watching was the rest of his waking hours. Nothing in this chapter asks anyone to give up the first kind. Where real vigilance is medically or practically necessary, it should be clearly defined, written down, and shared wherever that is possible. What this chapter asks is that the second kind be recognised for what it is, so that it is not mistaken for a post.

### What the Peace of Christ Is Not

Having said what peace means in this chapter, I need to say what it is not, or the promise will be misheard and then felt as failure.

It is not the absence of concern. Jesus gave his peace to men he had just told to expect trouble. Paul, who wrote of “the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding,” also wrote of the daily pressure of his anxiety for all the churches. Concern for what you love is not a failure of peace. It can coexist with it.

It is not a required feeling. Some Christians experience the peace of Christ as a felt calm, sometimes suddenly. Many experience it as something steadier and less dramatic: a settledness beneath the fear, a sense of being held that does not remove the shaking. And a Christian may belong securely to Christ and remain, for a season, unable to feel calm or settled at all, in depression, in exhaustion, in grief. John 14 should not be used to require a particular emotional experience of anyone. The promise concerns Christ’s presence and his relationship with his people before it becomes a description of any one person’s immediate feelings. Lesson 16 said that the body may remain activated after a sincere act of trust; that is true here too.

It is not certainty about outcomes. Caleb wanted to know that his wife would be well. Christ’s peace does not ordinarily provide certainty about the outcome of a particular scan, trial or relationship, and John 14:27 does not promise such certainty. What it supplies is something else. Caleb could trust that neither he nor his wife was beyond God’s knowledge and care, and that no outcome could separate Caleb from the love of God in Christ. That is a different thing from knowing the result, and a person who is waiting for the result may find it cold comfort at first. It is nonetheless the peace that is actually on offer, and it is not small.

It is not resignation. Peace before answers does not mean giving up on the answer, ceasing to pray for it, or pretending not to care which way it goes. Caleb went on praying for his wife’s healing, and so did the church. Peace is not the end of hope. It is the thing that lets hope wait without tearing itself apart.

What the peace of Christ is, then, is security rooted in what is already settled while what is unsettled remains unsettled. On the night of John 14 the disciples’ circumstances were, to them, entirely open; Jesus’ relationship to them, his promise to return, his Father’s house and the coming Spirit were entirely settled. The peace was drawn from the second while the first stayed open. That is the shape of it. It is not “everything will be all right.” It is “you are mine, and I am with you, whatever it is.”

### A Common Misunderstanding

“Resting before it is resolved would be irresponsible. If I stop watching, it means I do not care.”

This belief made Caleb a sentry, and it is worth examining, because it has a moral force that makes it hard to argue with. Who wants to be the person who slept while their wife was ill?

But look at what the anxious watching actually did. Caleb’s vigilance did not read the scan. It did not adjust the treatment. Appropriate monitoring and practical care did still matter, and they followed the clinical team’s guidance; but the hours of mental surveillance beyond that added nothing to her care. What they did was remove Caleb from the meals, the evenings, the conversations and the ordinary tenderness that were the actual content of the months they had. That watching was not care. Care was what it was preventing.

Scripture does not connect love with sleeplessness. The psalmist says that God gives to his beloved sleep, or, on another reading of the same verse, gives to his beloved in sleep; either way, sleep is placed among his gifts and not among the failures of love. Jesus slept in the stern of the boat during a storm that terrified experienced fishermen. That is not a prescription for how to handle every crisis, and I do not want to draw more from it than is there, but it does at least show that rest in the middle of an unresolved situation is not, in itself, negligence.

The responsible thing, in a wait you cannot shorten, is to do what is yours: the appointments, the questions, the monitoring you have been asked to do, the practical support, the prayer. And then to live, because the living is also yours. In many long waits, the people you love need sustainable presence more than constant anxious surveillance, and a carer who has collapsed is no longer able to give either.

### Practical Application

Here is how I worked with Caleb, and what he found.

Find the sentence. Write the condition you have placed on rest. I will permit myself peace when I know that ______. Write it exactly, including the part that feels unreasonable when you see it on paper. Caleb’s, once he was honest, was “when I know she will not die of this,” and he saw as he wrote it that no scan could give him that.

Ask whether the answer is due, and what is actually yours. Some answers have dates; write the date, and put it on the later list from Lesson 17. Some answers have no date and may never come; write that, too, because it changes what you are waiting for. Then ask what actions could responsibly influence care, safety or preparedness, and what monitoring the relevant professionals have actually recommended. Write those down as tasks; they belong to today or to their dates. Separate them from the vigilance that produces no useful action. The first list is care. The second can be set down without anything being lost.

Separate what is unresolved from what is settled. On one side of the page, write what you do not know. On the other, write what you do: not optimistic guesses about the outcome, but what is actually established. Some of it will be practical: the treatment was completed, the team is good, the next appointment is fixed. Some of it will be what Christ has given: you are his, he is with you, nothing in this can separate you from his love, and the Spirit is present in the waiting. The second column is where his peace is grounded. Read it slowly, and let it be as true as the first.

Review what has been suspended. Write what you have stopped doing until the answer comes. Sleep, church, friendship, reading, laughter, the ordinary intimacies of marriage or family. Some temporary changes may be necessary and wise: a carer may genuinely lack the hours, an immune-suppressed patient may need the household to avoid crowds, a family may need privacy for a season. Others may be consequences of anxious vigilance rather than actual care. Look at the list honestly and choose one safe, realistic part of ordinary life to resume or adapt this week, not as a reward for a good result, but because it is yours to live now. Caleb came back to the midweek group the following Wednesday. He did not feel better. He said afterwards that it was the first evening in two months that he had not spent at his post, and that his wife had noticed.

Use the horizon test. Ask: if this answer came, and came well, what would be the next “when I know”? If each answer would immediately create another condition for rest, the deeper issue may no longer be only the present question. You may also be waiting for complete certainty, or for permanent safety, and ordinary life provides neither. Seeing that does not make the present wait less real. It does show that peace, if it comes, will not come from the far side of any scan.

Come to Christ, rather than manufacturing peace. Because the peace is Christ’s and is given, the practices that help are the ones that direct attention toward him: the psalms, the Gospel of John read slowly, the Lord’s Supper, the gathered church, the Six-Movement Prayer of Lesson 16, especially its fifth and sixth movements. These can help direct attention toward Christ, his promises and his people. They do not manufacture peace or guarantee a felt result, and you should not measure them by whether you feel calmer afterwards. You do not need to manufacture or prove peace. You may simply come to Christ, practise ordinary life where possible, and refuse to make emotional calm a condition of faithfulness. Some evenings you will feel settled. Some you will not. You belong to him on both.

Caleb’s wife had another scan three months later. I am not going to tell you what it showed, and that is deliberate, because this chapter is about the peace that came before it. What I can tell you is that he was in the midweek group the night before the scan, and that he slept, not well but some, and that when I asked him the next morning whether he had breathed yet, he said that he had decided to stop waiting for permission.

## Closing declaration

Christ spoke peace to his disciples before they understood what was coming. I do not have to make emotional calm or complete certainty the price of ordinary life. I will continue the practical care, monitoring and prayer that genuinely belong to me, and I will seek help when the wait overwhelms my ability to function. I may remain afraid, but I am not alone or beyond Christ's keeping. I can return, gently and imperfectly, to the life and people before me while the answer remains open.
