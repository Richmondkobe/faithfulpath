# Lesson 20: A Rule of Life for a Restless Mind

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to describe the week you actually have and write a small rule of life for this season: one central practice, one or two supports, each tied to a cue and held with grace.

## Before you begin

Acute crisis is not the time for an ambitious new rule. If you are experiencing acute mental-health distress, severe functional impairment, a destabilising bereavement, a hospital admission or another immediate crisis, retain or establish only the few supports necessary for safety and basic functioning, accept help, and return to broader planning later. If you find that your routines are becoming rigid, that missing one produces disproportionate distress, or that you must complete them in a particular way or order for the day to feel safe, that is a sign the rule has become something other than a trellis; rigid routines and severe distress when they are interrupted can have several different causes, and a qualified professional can help you understand what is happening; do not attempt to diagnose it from this lesson. Where eating, tracking or body-focused routines are involved, follow the guidance of the professional supporting you. If sleep, movement and digital limits have been the whole of what you have tried for a persistent anxiety or low mood, and it has not been enough, please do not conclude that you have failed at the rule; it may be that the rule was never going to be enough on its own, and that professional support was the missing item. Never start, stop or change medication because of anything in this course. Write the rule during the day, once for this season.

## Key Scripture: Mark 1:35 (ESV)

> And rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed.

Read Mark 1:21–39 before continuing. It is a Sabbath in Capernaum: teaching, healing, the whole city at the door at sunset. The withdrawal comes while the demands are unfinished, and Luke tells us it was habitual.

## One truth to carry with you

A rule of life does not prevent hardship or manufacture a quiet mind. It places a few chosen practices, boundaries and relationships into ordinary life so that faith, physical limits and support are not remembered only after a crisis begins. The image is a trellis: a supportive structure along which a living thing can grow, so that it grows upward rather than in a heap on the ground. The trellis does not make the plant grow, and it does not control the weather. So write it small, tie it to a cue rather than only a clock, and hold it with grace: missing a day is not breaking the rule, it is a day, and the response to a missed day is the next day, not a reckoning. And in the seasons when no rhythm holds, God was Father before you had a routine and is Father in the week the routine collapsed.

## The lesson

A man I will call Jonathan came back to see me three months after we had finished working through most of this material, and the first thing he said was, "What did I do wrong?" He had done well: he had learned to notice the chain of consequences before it ran, to separate what was said from what he had imagined, to sort tomorrow into today, later and release. Then his small business had its busiest quarter in years, and somewhere in it the old patterns came back: on his phone at midnight, three Sundays missed, the walk gone, and nobody told, because there was nobody he had arranged to tell. I asked which of the tools he had been using. He said, honestly, that he used them when things got bad. "They work. I just wait until I need them." Jonathan had a set of good tools and no life to put them in. What he needed was not another tool. It was a trellis.

It is a Sabbath in Capernaum. Jesus teaches, heals Simon's mother-in-law, and at sunset the whole city gathers at the door. Then, very early, while it is still dark, he gets up, goes out to a deserted place, and prays. Simon finds him and says what people in demanding seasons always say: "Everyone is looking for you." Three observations of a pattern, rather than commands extracted from a verse. **The withdrawal came while the demands were unfinished**, and Luke tells us it was habitual. **The withdrawal was for prayer**, not simply time off. And **prayer belonged within a life of purposeful ministry**, not after it. What the passage does not give is a rule about rising before dawn: a nurse coming off a night shift, or a man whose only quiet hour is after ten at night, is not less faithful because the desolate place comes at a different hour.

The phrase "rule of life" comes from the monastic tradition. A rule of life is a small set of chosen rhythms that describe how you intend to live, held steadily enough to shape you and lightly enough not to crush you. For a restless mind it has a particular purpose: most of this course has been about what to do in the moment a thought arrives, while a rule is about arranging the week so that the mind is not starting each day from exhaustion, isolation, sleeplessness and a phone. Jonathan's tools had not stopped working; the ground they had to work in had.

A few principles shape a rule that helps rather than harms. **Fewer commitments than you think**: a rule with fifteen items will be abandoned in a fortnight, and its abandonment will become one more thing to be anxious about. **Realistic for your actual life**: shift work, small children, a parent needing care, chronic illness are the conditions of the rule, not obstacles to it. **Tied to a cue, not only a clock**, because for a rotating shift the cue holds when the clock cannot. **Flexible by season**, and the change is not failure. **Held with grace**: missing a day is not breaking the rule; it is a day. **Reviewed, not enforced.** And **never a substitute for care**: no arrangement of prayer, sleep and walks treats an anxiety disorder, depression or OCD.

Here are the areas a rule for a restless mind should consider, arranged so that the centre is visible. You will not build a commitment in every area, and should not try. **Centre: prayer and communion with God**, not necessarily long or early, but regular and tied to a cue the rest of the week knows to protect. **Formation: Scripture and Christian community** — steady, unhurried reading rather than verse-hunting for reassurance, and embodied participation in a healthy Christian community, whose form may vary according to safety, health, access and season. The aim is not attendance at any cost, and no one should return to an unsafe congregation to satisfy a weekly rule. **Embodiment: sleep, movement and rest** — protecting the conditions for sleep rather than guaranteeing it, movement that is medically appropriate and realistically repeatable, and rest that is actual rather than theoretical, which for a carer may be a few protected hours rather than a day. **Boundaries: work and digital life**, because for many people the phone is where work continues after it has ended. **Support: relationships and professional care** — arrange contact before you need it, and if you have a doctor, counsellor or therapist, they belong in the rule as a commitment, not an afterthought.

"If I get my routine right, my mind will be quiet." This is the misunderstanding that produced Jonathan's question, and this lesson could easily reinforce it. A rule of life supports a quieter mind; it does not produce one. There are people with excellent routines and anxiety disorders, and people whose lives are chaotic and whose minds are fairly calm. The trellis gives the plant something to grow along; it does not make the plant grow, and it does not control the weather. This matters because someone who believes the routine will produce calm will, in a season when calm does not come, begin to tighten it — more prayer, earlier rising, stricter limits — and the rule has become perfectionism with a new project to be anxious about.

So, in practice. **Look at the week you actually have** before writing any commitment, honestly and without tidying it; Jonathan's honest week had prayer in it on Sunday morning only, and seeing that on paper was most of the work. **Choose a centre and one or two supports.** **Write each commitment small, with a cue**: not "pray more" but "pray the six movements at the kitchen table before I open the laptop"; not "less phone" but "the phone charges in the hall from nine, except when I am on call". The test is whether you could keep it in a week when everything else went wrong. **Write the grace clause** beside each one — what you will do when you miss it, which is always some version of "the next day". **Give the tools a place in waking hours**, and at night use only the Night-Time Thought Download, if writing will not increase alertness; never begin a full analysis in bed. **Review monthly, and adjust by season.**

Jonathan's rule fitted on half a page. After he introduced the order-book boundary he also noticed that his sleep improved, although the boundary was not a treatment or a guarantee. Three months later he said the busy quarter had not been the problem; the empty week had been. "I built it for the busy weeks, and it turned out the busy weeks were fine. It was the ordinary ones where I'd stopped doing anything."

A man I will call Jonathan came back to see me three months after we had finished working through most of what is in this course, and the first thing he said was, “What did I do wrong?”

He had done well. He had learned to notice the chain of consequences before it ran, to separate what was said from what he had imagined, to sort tomorrow into today, later and release. I asked him which of the tools he had been using. He thought about it and said, honestly, that he used them when things got bad. “They work,” he said. “I just wait until I need them.”

Jonathan had not done anything wrong. He had a set of good tools and no life to put them in. Every practice in this course had been something he reached for in a crisis, and nothing in his ordinary week was supporting the practices, the relationships and the physical capacity that helped him respond when pressure increased. What he needed was not another tool. It was a trellis.

### Central Scripture: Mark 1:35

> And rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed. (Mark 1:35, ESV) Read from Mark 1:21 to 1:39 before continuing, because the verse only makes sense inside the day that surrounds it.

It is a Sabbath in Capernaum. Jesus teaches in the synagogue and casts out an unclean spirit, and the town is astonished. He goes to Simon’s house and heals Simon’s mother-in-law. Then, at sunset, when the Sabbath ends, Mark says the whole city gathered at the door, and Jesus healed many who were sick and cast out many demons. Mark does not tell us how late the ministry continued, but the scene communicates sustained and extensive demand.

Then, verse 35. Very early, while it is still dark, he gets up, leaves the house, goes out to a deserted place, and prays. Simon and the others go looking for him, find him, and say what people in demanding seasons always say: “Everyone is looking for you.” And Jesus answers: “Let us go on to the next towns, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out.”

I want to draw three things from this, and I want to say plainly that they are observations of a pattern, not commands extracted from a verse.

First, the withdrawal came while the demands of Capernaum remained unfinished. Jesus did not wait for the town to be done with him; when Simon found him, “Everyone is looking for you” made clear that the expectations had not disappeared. Luke tells us that this was habitual: he “would withdraw to desolate places and pray.”

Second, the withdrawal was for prayer. It was not simply time off. He went out to be with his Father. A specifically Christian rule of life is centred on relationship with God and faithful discipleship. It may include practices also found in ordinary wellness guidance, sleep, movement, limits and relationships, but it understands them within a larger life of worship, love and responsibility.

Third, prayer preceded Jesus’ stated decision to continue into the neighbouring towns. The text does not say that he had been uncertain, or that prayer functioned as a decision-making technique, and I do not want to build a formula from a sequence Mark simply reports. The pastoral observation is more modest: withdrawal and communion with the Father belonged within a life of clear, purposeful ministry. They were not what he did when the ministry was over. They were part of it.

What this chapter does not draw from the verse is a rule about rising before dawn. Jesus rose early that particular morning; the text describes what he did, not what every disciple must do. A nurse coming off a night shift, a mother with a newborn, a man whose only quiet hour is after ten at night, are not less faithful because their desolate place comes at a different hour. What the pattern offers is the shape: withdrawal, prayer, and return, built into a life rather than reserved for emergencies.

### What a Rule of Life Is, and Is Not

The phrase “rule of life” comes from the Christian monastic tradition, and the word “rule” can put people off before they have understood it. The Latin regula refers to a rule, a standard or a guiding measure. Many contemporary writers use the image of a trellis to explain its purpose: a supportive structure along which a living thing can grow, so that it grows upward and toward the light rather than in a heap on the ground. The trellis is a metaphor, not a translation, but it is a good one. A rule of life is a small set of chosen rhythms that describe how you intend to live, held steadily enough to shape you and lightly enough not to crush you.

For a restless mind, a rule of life has a particular purpose. Most of this course has been about what to do in the moment when a thought arrives. A rule is about arranging the week so that the mind is not starting each day from exhaustion, isolation, sleeplessness and a phone, because a mind that starts there will find every tool in the book harder to use. Jonathan’s tools had not stopped working. The ground they had to work in had.

Put plainly: a rule of life does not prevent hardship or manufacture a quiet mind. It places a few chosen practices, boundaries and relationships into ordinary life so that faith, physical limits and support are not remembered only after a crisis begins.

A few principles shape a rule that helps rather than harms.

Fewer commitments than you think. A rule with fifteen items is a to-do list that will be abandoned in a fortnight, and its abandonment will become one more thing to be anxious about. Beginning with two or three anchors is usually more manageable than attempting everything at once. That is practical guidance rather than a required number; some readers will rightly begin with one.

Realistic for your actual life. A rule is written for the week you have, not the week you wish you had. Shift work, small children, a parent needing care, a second job, chronic illness, a house with no quiet room: these are the conditions of the rule, not obstacles to it. A rule that cannot survive contact with a real Tuesday is not a rule; it is a fantasy that will produce guilt.

Tied to a cue, not only a clock. A commitment may be tied to a clock time, a place, or a reliable cue: after waking, during a commute, after medication, before beginning work, when another household member takes over care. For a rotating shift or an unpredictable day, the cue holds when the clock cannot.

Flexible by season. The rule for a season of illness or a newborn is not the rule for an ordinary year, and the change is not failure.

Held with grace. Missing a day is not breaking the rule; it is a day. A rule that produces guilt when it is missed has become the perfectionism of Lesson 11 in different clothing. The response to a missed day is the next day, not a reckoning.

Reviewed, not enforced. A rule is looked at monthly and adjusted. It is not a contract you have failed.

Never a substitute for care. A rule can hold professional support inside it, and should, where that support is needed. It cannot replace it, and no arrangement of prayer, sleep and walks treats an anxiety disorder, depression or OCD. Lesson 5 said this about the body and it is true of the whole rule.

### The Shape of a Rule

Here are the areas a rule for a restless mind should at least consider. I have arranged them so that the centre is visible, because a rule chosen only from “what is most neglected” could leave out the one thing it is meant to be built around. You will not build a commitment in every area, and you should not try. Establish one small central practice, and then one or two supporting practices where they fit.

#### Centre: Prayer and Communion with God

This is what everything else is arranged around. Not necessarily long, not necessarily early, but regular and, where possible, in a place or at a cue that the rest of the week knows to protect. The Six-Movement Prayer of Lesson 16 gives a shape for the days when prayer circles; the psalms give words for the days when there are none. A rule might say: I will pray before I look at my phone, or I will pray on the walk to the station, or I will use the six movements on Sunday evening for the week ahead. The commitment is to a time, a place or a cue, not to a feeling or a length.

#### Formation: Scripture and Christian Community

Scripture. A restless mind needs regular contact with what is true, and Lessons 14 and 15 argued that this contact is not the same as positive thinking. The rule here is about steady, unhurried reading, not study for its own sake and not verse-hunting for reassurance, which Lesson 10 warned against. A psalm a day. A chapter of a Gospel. A passage read twice, slowly, and one line carried through the day. Readers who find that they scan Scripture anxiously for confirmation should read with a set portion and a set time, and stop when it is done.

Church. Embodied participation in a healthy Christian community ordinarily matters, while its form may vary according to safety, health, access and season. The gathered church is where the truth you have been renewing your mind with is spoken aloud by other voices, where the Lord’s Supper is given, where you are prayed for by people who are not you. Jonathan’s three missed Sundays removed one of the regular places where he had received worship, companionship and truth from voices beyond his own; that was one part of the wider change, not a complete explanation of his returning patterns. Some readers are housebound, disabled, immunocompromised, working during service times, or recovering from harmful church experiences. The aim is not attendance at any cost. Seek safe and accessible Christian community in a form your circumstances permit, and do not return to an unsafe congregation merely to satisfy a weekly rule. The rule here asks simply: what form of Christian community is available and safe for me, and what will it take to be part of it in a busy week?

#### Embodiment: Sleep, Movement and Rest

Sleep. Lesson 5 said that a tired mind circles more, and it does. The rule here is about protecting the conditions for sleep, not guaranteeing sleep, which no rule can do: a regular time to go to bed and get up, where your life allows it; a wind-down in the last hour; the phone away from the bed where that is possible; caffeine reduced gradually if it is affecting you, since sensitivity varies. Adapt phone boundaries around accessibility, medical, caregiving and safety needs; a carer or a patient may need the phone within reach, and that is not a failure of the rule. These habits can support sleep, but they are not a guaranteed solution and are not a substitute for assessment or evidence-based treatment of insomnia. If sleep problems are severe, persistent, worsening or affecting safety and daily functioning, speak with a healthcare professional.

Movement. Regular physical activity helps many people’s mood and sleep, and it does not need to be exercise in any formal sense: a walk, a swim, gardening, a game with children, cycling to work. Choose movement that is medically appropriate, realistically repeatable and suited to your circumstances; consistency is usually more sustainable than an ambitious plan that cannot be maintained. If you have a health condition, or have been inactive for a long time, ask your doctor what is appropriate. And this is support for a mind, not a treatment for a disorder; it sits alongside professional care, never in place of it.

Rest. A day, or part of a day, that is not work, and that is different from collapse. Christians differ on whether the Sabbath command continues to bind in the new covenant, and I do not need to settle that here; what is not disputed is that God built rest into creation and that human beings do badly without it. The rule here is about making rest actual rather than theoretical: when it will happen, what will not happen during it, and what will. For a carer or a shift worker or a parent of small children, this may be a few protected hours rather than a day, and it may need to be arranged with other people. Arranging rest before exhaustion is not selfish; it is an acknowledgement that human capacity is limited and interdependent.

#### Boundaries: Work and Digital Life

Work needs an end, and for many people the phone is where work continues after it has ended. Lesson 15 spoke about the news; the principle applies to email, messages and the endless scroll. The rule here is a personal boundary, written by you for your life, and the toolkit includes a Personal Digital Boundary worksheet for it. I am not going to prescribe a number of minutes or an hour of the evening; your work may genuinely need you at nine at night, and a boundary you cannot keep is worse than none. Ask instead: when does my work day end, and what will I do to mark it? Where does my phone live in the evening, allowing for anyone who may need to reach me? What do I check before bed, and does it help me sleep? Jonathan’s answer to the last question was “the order book,” and his boundary became: the order book is closed at eight, and it is not opened again until morning, because nothing in it can be acted on at midnight.

#### Support: Relationships and Professional Care

Relationships. Lesson 12 was about the people you carry; this is about the people who carry you. A restless mind isolates, because rumination is solitary and because telling someone feels like burdening them. The rule here is to arrange contact before you need it: one conversation a week with someone who knows how you are actually doing, one friendship that is maintained rather than only resumed in crisis. The toolkit’s Support Map is for drawing this. Jonathan’s rule ended up including a fortnightly breakfast with a friend from church, and the friend was told, in general terms, why.

Professional support. If you have a doctor, a counsellor, a therapist or a psychiatrist, they belong in the rule as a commitment, not as an afterthought: the appointments kept, the medication taken as prescribed, never started, stopped or changed because of anything in this course. If you have wondered throughout this course whether you need such support, and the help boxes have spoken to you more than once, the rule’s first item may be to make that appointment. Access may be limited by cost, geography or availability; where preferred care is unavailable, begin by asking a primary-care clinician, a recognised health service or a reputable community organisation what options exist. Pastoral care may support that process, but it should not impersonate clinical treatment that is not available. A rule of life is not an alternative to treatment. For many restless minds, it is what makes treatment more likely to take.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I get my routine right, my mind will be quiet.”

This is the misunderstanding that produced Jonathan’s question, and it is worth dismantling gently, because the chapter you are reading could easily reinforce it.

A rule of life supports a quieter mind. It does not produce one, and it does not guarantee one. There are people with excellent routines and anxiety disorders, and there are people whose lives are chaotic and whose minds are, by temperament, fairly calm. The rule is not a machine into which faithfulness is fed and peace comes out. It is a trellis. It gives the plant something to grow along. It does not make the plant grow, and it does not control the weather.

This matters because a person who believes the routine will produce calm will, in a season when calm does not come, begin to tighten the routine. More prayer, earlier rising, stricter limits, and then the rule has become the perfectionism of Lesson 11, and the mind is more restless than before, now with a new project to be anxious about. Lessons 16 and 19 said that a sincere act of trust may not change the body’s state, and that the peace of Christ is not a feeling on demand. A rule of life is a set of practices; it is subject to the same truth.

And there are seasons when no rule holds. A newborn. A parent dying. An illness. A move. In those seasons the trellis comes down for a while, and the grace of God is not tied to the trellis. He was Father before you had a routine and he is Father in the week the routine collapsed. The purpose of the ordinary rhythms is not to earn his presence but to attend to it, and when they are gone he is not.

### Practical Application

Here is how I helped Jonathan build his, and what I would suggest for yours.

Look at the week you actually have. Before writing any commitment, write down what an ordinary week currently contains: when you sleep, when you work, when the phone is in your hand, when you pray, whom you see, when you rest. Be honest, and do not tidy it. Jonathan’s honest week had prayer in it on Sunday morning only, the phone in it at every unfilled moment, and no rest that was not also collapse. Seeing that on paper was most of the work.

Choose a centre and one or two supports. Start with one practice that turns you toward God. Then choose one or two supporting practices from the areas above: the one most neglected, or the one that, if changed a little, would most help the others. For Jonathan the centre was a short prayer at the kitchen table before the laptop opened, and the supports were the order-book boundary and the fortnightly breakfast. Sleep improved on its own once the order book was closed at eight.

Write each commitment small, with a cue. Specific, tied to a time, a place or a reliable cue, and small enough to survive a bad week. Not “pray more” but “pray the six movements at the kitchen table before I open the laptop.” Not “less phone” but “the phone charges in the hall from nine, except when I am on call.” Not “see friends” but “breakfast with Mark every other Thursday.” The test is whether you could keep it in a week when everything else went wrong.

Write the grace clause. Beside each commitment, write what you will do when you miss it. The answer is always some version of “the next day.” Write it anyway, because you will need to read it, and because writing it now is a decision that the rule will not be allowed to become a weapon.

Give the tools a place in waking hours. The practices of this course need somewhere to live. Decide when and where the Thought-Capturing Record of Lesson 13 will be used during the day. At night, use only the brief note Lesson 17 described, if it helps; do not begin a full analysis in bed. Decide when the later list of Lesson 17 is reviewed (Sunday evening, for many people). Use the Body and Mind Check-In of Lesson 5 only if it improves awareness without increasing monitoring or distress; readers with health anxiety, eating disorders or compulsive checking should follow professional guidance about any kind of tracking. The tools are for the moment; the rule tells the moment where to find them.

Consider what support would help. If you have a safe and trustworthy person, consider sharing the commitments you would like support with, and asking them to ask you about them in a month, without judgement. You do not need to disclose the full page, your private reflections, medical information, or anything that could expose you to control or judgement. If there is no such person at the moment, the Support Map may be the place to begin, and a pastor or counsellor may be the first name on it. Jonathan shared three lines of his rule with one friend, and that was enough.

Review monthly, and adjust by season. Put a date in the diary. At the review, ask what was kept, what was not, and whether what was not kept was too big, wrongly placed, or simply not the right thing. Adjust and go on. When the season changes, rewrite the rule for the new season, and do not carry the old one as a reproach.

The rhythm is private, optional, editable and deletable, one version per season; nothing here is scored, and the monthly review generates no reminders or overdue notices. ## Closing declaration

I will build a small and gracious rhythm for the life and season I actually have. I will include practices that turn me toward God, support my body, protect my attention and keep me connected to safe people and appropriate care. I will not use the rule to earn peace, measure my worth or punish inconsistency. When I miss a practice, I will return without a reckoning. The trellis may change or temporarily fall, but God's faithfulness does not.

## If you need more support

If your routines become rigid, or missing one produces disproportionate distress, or they must be completed in a particular order for the day to feel safe, stop and speak with a qualified professional who can help assess what is happening. If sleep, movement and digital limits have been all you have tried for a persistent anxiety or low mood and it has not been enough, that is not a failure at the rule; professional support may be the missing item.
