# What Am I Waiting to Know Before I Permit Myself to Rest?

*Worksheet 19 in the printed toolkit · Lesson 19*

> **Before you begin.** This exercise is not the response to danger: if the wait concerns someone's safety, or yours, present action and qualified help come first. Nothing here asks you to give up necessary monitoring or care — write it down instead, so that release is never confused with neglect. Complete it once for one wait, during the day. If you find yourself redoing it in order to feel certain that the settled column is really settled, stop and read the column instead. And if the wait has broken your rest and the anxiety is severe, persistent or affecting your ability to function, tell your doctor or the clinical team.

Use this for one wait at a time, the one that has most governed your rest. Write it out. Complete it once for that wait; if you find yourself redoing it in order to feel certain that the settled column is really settled, stop, and simply read the column instead.

Step 1. The sentence.

Complete it exactly: I will permit myself peace when I know that ______. Include the part that seems unreasonable on paper.

Step 2. The answer, and what is mine.

Is this answer due? If so, when? If it may never come, write that. Then write two short lists. Necessary: any monitoring, appointment, symptom to report, communication or safety responsibility that the relevant professionals have recommended or that responsibly affects care or preparedness. Not necessary: the watching that produces no information or action. Beside the second list write: This particular watching is not a necessary task. Keep the first list somewhere you can see it, so that release is never confused with neglect.

Step 3. The horizon test.

If this answer came, and came well, what would be the next “when I know”? Write it. If each answer would create another condition, note that you may also be waiting for complete certainty or permanent safety, which no answer supplies.

Step 4. Unresolved and settled.

Write two short lists. What I do not know: the open questions, honestly. What is settled: what is actually established in my circumstances, and what Christ has given me that no outcome changes. Do not put hopes in the second list; put only what is true now.

Step 5. What I have suspended.

Write what you have stopped doing until the answer comes. Beside each, write whether the change is necessary for now, or a result of anxious vigilance. Choose one safe, realistic part of ordinary life to resume or adapt this week, and write when.

Step 6. Coming to Christ.

Write one practice that directs your attention toward Christ rather than toward the answer, and when you will do it this week. Then write a short prayer, in your own words, coming to him before you know, and asking still for what you hope. You do not need to feel anything in particular while writing it.

If the wait concerns danger, yours or someone else’s, this exercise is not the response; present action and qualified help are. And if your rest has been broken by a wait you cannot shorten, and the anxiety is severe, persistent or affecting your ability to function, tell your doctor or the clinical team. Peace before answers is a gift to be received; it is not a reason to go without help. Keep this page. This worksheet is in the Course Journal and Practical Toolkit, and the 30-day plan will return to it in the fourth week.

*Complete this once for one matter, then stop. If you find yourself redoing it to feel certain the answer is right, that is a signal to put it down, not to try again. This worksheet does not treat an anxiety disorder, depression, OCD or trauma; if a professional is supporting you, their guidance takes priority.*
