Welcome to Lesson 15. Here is today's question, and it is one a man once asked me almost word for word. If everything you read is true, why does it feel like poison? The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: the difference between what is accurate and what deserves your sustained attention, and a six-question test to decide. One thing first. Scrolling or monitoring that is hard to control, or that disrupts your sleep or daily life, deserves assessment rather than more willpower. And persistent low mood, or vivid images and nightmares connected to something you experienced or witnessed, are reasons to speak with a doctor or a mental-health professional. The support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is Philippians 4, verse 8, in the English Standard Version. "Whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things." This verse is often quoted alone, and alone it can sound like a recommendation to think about pleasant things. Notice where it sits. Paul is in prison, uncertain whether he will be released or executed. He has just told the church not to be anxious but to pray, and promised that God's peace will guard their hearts and minds. Two things follow. The list is not a list of pleasant things; truth comes first, and "just" and "honourable" are not always comfortable. And the verb means considering, reckoning, giving sustained attention. Paul is not describing the involuntary arrival of thoughts. He is describing what the mind is deliberately given to dwell on.

Let me tell you about a man I will call Andrew. He was not, by his own account, an anxious person until his mid-fifties. What changed was not his circumstances but his evenings. During a period of economic uncertainty he began following the news closely, which was reasonable; his pension was involved. But following the news became reading commentary, and commentary became forums where people predicted collapse, and by the time we spoke he was spending two or three hours each evening absorbing other people's alarm. He could not tell me what had actually happened to interest rates in the past month, because the sources he read were almost entirely about what might happen next. He had stopped sleeping. I encouraged him to see a doctor about the sleep. But we talked about the reading too. "Everything I read is probably true," he said. "I am not reading lies. So why does it feel like poison?"

Andrew's question deserves an answer. He was not, mostly, reading lies. So why did it feel like poison? Because being accurate is necessary, but it is not the only question that governs sustained attention. Material can be accurate but incomplete: a mind fed only on possibilities of collapse is given a picture from which the ordinary, the stable and the good have been removed. Accurate but unresolved: a prediction is not yet an established fact. Accurate but outside any faithful response, producing the feeling of responsibility without the possibility of action, one of the most exhausting states a mind can be in. Accurate but structured around alarm, arranged for urgency rather than proportion. And accurate while carrying an unsupported claim about God. Andrew's evenings failed on nearly every count, and underneath them ran a claim he had never examined: that the future was in the hands of markets and ministers, and God was, at best, a spectator.

"Philippians 4:8 means I should avoid anything negative. Christians should only think about nice things." This reading produces two kinds of harm. It makes people feel guilty for thinking about hard realities that Scripture dwells on at length: the prophets on injustice, the psalmists on suffering, Paul on his own possible execution in this very letter. If verse 8 forbade thinking about hard things, Paul broke it three chapters earlier. And it lets a great deal of falsehood through, provided it is pleasant. The right reading is neither "only nice things" nor "anything at all". It is: give your sustained attention to what is true, whole, honourable and worthy, and withhold it from what is false, distorted, alarmist or degrading, whether it comes from a screen, a conversation, or your own imagination.

The tool is a test with six questions. They are not a word-for-word exposition of Paul's eight terms; they are a pastoral tool inspired by the passage. Established: is this a verified fact, an interpretation, a prediction or an opinion, and what reliable evidence supports it? Proportionate: does it represent the wider picture fairly, or is it a fragment arranged to produce a feeling? Worthy: is it honourable, just, pure and commendable, or contemptuous and built to provoke? Relevant: does sustained attention serve a responsibility, a person, or a faithful action? Some things need to be known once; dwelling is a further step. Theologically honest: have I attached an unsupported claim about God to the facts? And bounded: how much attention does this genuinely deserve, a minute, a morning, or an evening every day?

The mind is fed from two directions, so the practice has two halves. What you take in: for a week, notice what you give sustained attention to, without moralising, then run the test on the two or three sources that occupy you most. Andrew found his evening reading failed on established, proportionate and bounded. He did not stop reading the news. He stopped reading forecasts of it, limited himself to a reliable summary in the morning, and gave his evenings to something else. And what you generate: the mind also feeds itself, through the predictions, replays, rehearsals and mind-readings of Module 2. When you notice yourself dwelling on one, run the same test.

Paul does not stop at telling us what not to dwell on. A mind emptied of alarm will fill with something, and it is better to choose: Scripture, the actual people in front of you, the work that is yours, the good genuinely present in your day, the character of God. It will feel at first like a poorer diet than alarm, because alarm is stimulating. It is not poorer. And this is not a call to ignorance. A Christian cannot weep with those who weep without knowing that they are weeping. The question is how much sustained attention to give, and whether it serves anything. Andrew knew more about the economy than he needed, and less about his wife's week than he should.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Name one source that repeatedly alarms you without improving your understanding: a feed, a forum, a channel, a group. Write what you will do with it tomorrow: pause it for a day, or set a clear limit. Then write what you will give that time to instead. Please do not cut off information you need for your work, your safety, or a time-sensitive responsibility. At the end of tomorrow, notice whether you know less that mattered, or only less that alarmed. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. Not everything true deserves an evening. Some things need to be known once. What you dwell on, you become; so choose what is true, whole, honourable and worthy, and give it your sustained attention.

Let us pray. Father, you know what I have been feeding my mind, and what it has been feeding itself. Teach me to know what needs knowing once, and to let it go. Guard my heart and mind with your peace, and fill the space with what is true and worthy, and with you. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do.
