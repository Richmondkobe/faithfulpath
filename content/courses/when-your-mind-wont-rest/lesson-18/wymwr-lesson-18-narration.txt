Welcome to Lesson 18, the last lesson of Module 3. Here is today's question. What are you still waiting to feel sure of? The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to tell a decision that is missing something from one that is waiting for a feeling, and how to bring one decision to a named response. If the decision is about leaving a relationship in which you are harmed, controlled or threatened, it is a matter of safety, not courage. Plan it with a domestic-abuse service or another qualified professional before any step is taken, and sometimes before anything is said to family or church leaders. If the decision has serious financial, legal, medical or immigration consequences, a qualified adviser belongs in the process. And if even small everyday choices have become paralysing, describe that to a doctor; it is treatable. The support link is under this lesson.

Our passage is Proverbs chapter 3, verses 5 and 6, in the English Standard Version. "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make straight your paths." "Do not lean on your own understanding" is not an instruction to stop thinking; Proverbs praises the person who plans and takes counsel. Use your understanding, but rest your weight on God. "Acknowledge him" means knowing and honouring him in all your ways; it is not a promise that God will announce the decision. And "he will make straight your paths" is confident covenant wisdom, not a technique for getting a preferred result. It does not tell you which fork to take. What follows is a pastoral application of these verses.

Let me tell you about a woman I will call Miriam. She had been deciding the same thing for fourteen months. She had founded her church's youth ministry eight years earlier and led it ever since. It had grown, and she had grown tired. Her husband said, kindly, that the family was paying for it, and her doctor had used the word "exhausted". Her pastor thought she needed rest; two older women she trusted said the same. She had drafted the email twice and deleted it. "I know what I should do," she said. "I've known for a year. I just keep waiting to feel sure." Sure of what? After a long pause: "That nobody will be disappointed. That it isn't giving up. That I won't regret it." None of those things can be known in advance.

Here is the key distinction. Some decisions stay open because something is genuinely missing: important information, relevant expertise, or the people who should be consulted. Then the faithful next step is to go and get it. Other decisions stay open because every responsible part of discernment has been done, and the person is waiting for a feeling of certainty that costs nothing. That feeling is available for very few decisions, and almost never for the ones that matter. This lesson cannot tell you which one you are in, and its title is not a verdict on your decision.

Many people are held in place by one belief: "If I make the wrong decision, I will be outside God's will." Scripture gives a different picture. God's revealed will is about how we are to live: with justice, mercy, faithfulness, and love of God and neighbour. Within that there are usually several morally permissible options, and Scripture nowhere promises that God has secretly chosen one and hidden it. That does not make every option equally good. Wisdom is choosing well among permissible things, and a mistaken decision can cause real harm even in good faith. But it does not put you beyond God's reach.

When the deciding is done, the waiting usually takes one of five forms. Waiting for certainty, which is rarely on offer. Waiting for the decision to stop costing. Waiting until no one is disappointed is hoping circumstances will decide, so the cost belongs to them. Waiting for someone else to say it: not seeking wise counsel, which is a strength, but hoping enough voices will share out the responsibility until none is left. Waiting for a sign, which Lesson 10 covered. And waiting for a feeling of peace. "Let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts" was written to a congregation about its life together; it is not a decision test. A settled conscience matters, and persistent unease may be worth examining. But the absence of a peaceful feeling is not a veto. Jesus knew deep anguish in Gethsemane while remaining obedient.

The tool is the Faithful Decision Sheet: ten questions, answered in writing. One: what decision is actually before me? Two: is any option morally or legally ruled out? Three and four: what do I know, and what important information can still be obtained in reasonable time? If it would change the decision, getting it is the next step. Five: who is affected? A spouse, children or a dependent person may need to be consulted, not just informed. Six: what did counsel actually say, not what I hoped? Seven: what do I desire and fear? Desire is information, not divine authorisation. Eight and nine: what will each option require, risk and close, and can it be undone? A decision that is hard to reverse deserves more care, counsel and time. Ten: what is actually stopping me?

Then one of five responses is the answer. Act: what remains is courage; write what you will do and when. Wait for a defined reason: write what must happen first, because a wait without a trigger is delay wearing wisdom's clothes. Seek qualified help: write whom you will ask, and about what. Move to safety: where there is credible danger, contact emergency or specialist support, and do not announce a plan if that could increase the risk. This comes before everything else. Surrender: write what is not yours to decide. Miriam's sheet ended with act, and a date. Under question ten she wrote a sentence she had never let herself write before: "I am afraid that if I stop, I will find out I was only valued for what I did." That needed a longer conversation, and she had it with her pastor. But it was not a reason to lead the youth ministry for another year.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Choose the decision that has waited longest, and write it as one sentence. Then write what is actually stopping you. Then name the response that fits: act, wait for a defined reason, seek qualified help, move to safety, or surrender. If the answer is act and the decision is hard to undo, the next step is to arrange the review it needs, not to act tonight. The full Faithful Decision Sheet is under this lesson for a time when you think clearly. Pause the audio now and write. Press play when you are ready.

One thing to remember. A decision is not proved wrong because it cost what you knew it would cost. Nor is it proved right; watch the consequences honestly. When the decision reopens in your mind, do not redo the sheet. Ask one question: has anything actually changed? A changed feeling may deserve brief attention, if it points to new facts, an overlooked obligation, pressure or danger. If not, the decision stands. This closes Module 3. You have learned to test a thought, to pray without rehearsing, to give tomorrow back, and to decide without waiting for certainty.

Let us pray. Lord, I have asked, and I have listened. Where something is missing, help me find it. Where the deciding is done, give me courage for the next faithful step, and strength to bear the cost I already see. If I choose wrongly in good faith, keep me within reach of your mercy and direction. In Jesus' name. Amen. You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do.
