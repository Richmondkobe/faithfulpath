# The Philippians 4:8 Pastoral Attention Test

*Worksheet 15 in the printed toolkit · Lesson 15*

> **Before you begin.** Use this for what you are deliberately dwelling on, not for every passing thought. Complete it once for one thought or source, during the day. If scrolling or monitoring feels difficult to control or disrupts sleep, or if what you keep returning to comes with vivid images or nightmares, that deserves assessment by a qualified professional.

Use this exercise for one thought, subject or source at a time. Begin with whatever has been occupying your mind most this week. Remember that the six questions are a pastoral tool drawn from the passage, not a translation of it.

Step 1. What I have been dwelling on.

Write it in a sentence. If it is a source—a feed, a site, a programme, a person’s commentary—name it. If it is a thought you generate, write it as Lesson 13 taught you.

Step 2. Run the six questions.

Answer each briefly, and be honest.

Established. Fact, interpretation, prediction or opinion?

Proportionate. Does it represent the wider picture fairly? What has been left out?

Worthy. Is the content honourable, just, pure and commendable, or contemptuous, degrading or built to provoke?

Relevant. Does sustained attention serve a responsibility, a person or a faithful action?

Theologically honest. Have I attached an unsupported claim about God? Test it once, in context.

Bounded. How much attention does this genuinely deserve?

Step 3. The verdict.

Write one of the following and a sentence explaining why.

Keep and dwell. It is established, proportionate, worthy and relevant; it deserves my attention even if it is hard, within the bounds of Step 2.

Know and set down. It is true but offers no faithful response, or deserves far less attention than I have given it; I will note it and turn away.

Decline. It fails at established or worthy, or its presentation is built around alarm; I will stop feeding on it.

Step 4. What I will give my attention to instead.

Write one true and worthy thing you will deliberately dwell on in the time or space this has been occupying: a passage of Scripture, a person, a task, a genuine good in your day.

Step 5. The practical change.

If this was a source, write what you will do differently: a time limit, a substitution, a removal, a reliable source in place of many. If the subject needs expertise—financial, medical, legal—write who the appropriate professional is. Keep the change specific and small enough to keep.

Once is enough. Complete this exercise once per thought or source. Do not run the six questions on every passing thought; that becomes the monitoring Lesson 13 warned against. Use the test for what you are deliberately dwelling on, and when a declined thought or source pulls at you again, reread Step 3 and turn to Step 4 rather than re-examining it. Keep this page. The Personal Digital Boundary worksheet in the Course Journal and Practical Toolkit builds on Step 5, and the 30-day plan will ask you to use this worksheet in the fourth week.

---

*Complete this once for one matter, then stop. If you find yourself redoing it to feel certain the answer is right, that is a signal to put it down, not to try again. This worksheet does not treat an anxiety disorder, depression, OCD or trauma; if a professional is supporting you, their guidance takes priority.*
