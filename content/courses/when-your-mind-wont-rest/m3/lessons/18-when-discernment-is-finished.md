# Lesson 18: When Discernment Is Finished

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish a decision that genuinely needs more information or consultation from one that may be waiting for certainty, and use the Faithful Decision Sheet to identify a proportionate response.

## Before you begin

A few kinds of decision should not be made with this lesson alone. If the decision concerns leaving a relationship in which you are being harmed, controlled or threatened, it is a matter of safety rather than courage: safety planning should be done with a domestic-abuse service or another qualified professional before any step is taken, and sometimes before anything is said to family or church leaders. If the decision has serious financial, legal, medical or immigration consequences, a qualified adviser in that field belongs in the process. Acute depression, shock, severe sleep loss or overwhelming grief may affect concentration and judgement in ways that are hard to see from inside; where a major decision can safely wait, additional time and support may be wise, and where it cannot wait, involve appropriately qualified people rather than relying on a course alone. If you cannot make small everyday decisions either, and choosing a meal or replying to a message has become paralysing, describe that to a doctor; pervasive indecision can accompany depression, anxiety or another difficulty and is a reason to seek professional assessment. If the pressure of the decision includes thoughts of suicide or self-harm, use Finding Help Where You Live and tell someone safe today; if you may act, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact emergency or urgent crisis services now and do not remain alone. And if you feel pressured to decide quickly and in one direction, that pressure is information — though a deadline is not automatically manipulation. Verify who set it and why. Complete the sheet during the day, at a time when you think clearly, and not in the evening if evenings are when the decision circles.

## Key Scripture: Proverbs 3:5–6 (ESV)

> Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make straight your paths.

A father instructing a son, within the long opening instruction of Proverbs. "Do not lean on your own understanding" is not an instruction to stop thinking; Proverbs is the book most devoted to the careful use of understanding.

## One truth to carry with you

Some decisions remain open because important information or consultation is genuinely missing. Others remain open because every responsible part of discernment has been completed, and the person is waiting for a cost-free feeling of certainty. That feeling is available for very few decisions and almost never for the ones that matter; it usually arrives afterwards, if at all. Every real decision closes something, and waiting until no one will be disappointed is not waiting to decide — it is hoping circumstances will decide, so that the cost belongs to them and not to you. God's revealed will concerns how we are to live, and within it there are usually several morally permissible options. A mistaken decision can depart from wisdom and cause real harm, but it does not place you beyond God's reach. You can take a proportionate step without certainty about the outcome.

## The lesson

A woman I will call Miriam had been deciding the same thing for fourteen months, and could tell me the answer in one sentence. She had founded her church's youth ministry eight years earlier and led it ever since. It had grown, and she had grown tired; her doctor had used the word "exhausted". She had prayed about stepping down for over a year, and asked her pastor and two older women, who all thought she needed rest. "I know what I should do," she said, in the first ten minutes. "I've known for a year. I just keep waiting to feel sure." I asked what of. After a pause: "That nobody will be disappointed. That it isn't giving up. That I won't regret it." None of those things can be known in advance.

Proverbs 3:5–6 is among the most quoted Scripture at people trying to decide something, and it deserves its setting. Three things matter here. "Do not lean on your own understanding" is not an instruction to stop using your mind; Proverbs praises the person who plans, considers and takes counsel. The picture is of leaning: resting your whole weight on something. The son is to use his understanding while trusting God rather than his understanding for the outcome. "In all your ways acknowledge him" is not a promise that God will announce the decision. And "he will make straight your paths" is confident covenant wisdom, not a technique for obtaining a preferred result: it does not tell the trusting person which fork to take. What follows in this lesson is a pastoral application of these verses, and I mark it as such.

Lesson 10 was about discernment that becomes obsession. This lesson is about what comes after, and it begins with a distinction everything else depends on. Some decisions remain open because information or consultation is genuinely missing. Others remain open because discernment is complete and the person is waiting for a cost-free feeling of certainty. This lesson cannot tell you which you are in, and its title is not a verdict on your decision; some readers who have been told they "already know" do not, and the right result for them is to go back and obtain what is missing. But many people in this position are not short of information. What they are doing is one of these. **Waiting for it to feel certain**, which for the decisions that matter is waiting for something not on offer. **Waiting for it to stop costing**, hoping circumstances will decide so that the cost belongs to them. **Waiting for someone else to say it**, hoping the responsibility will be shared out until none is left. **Waiting for a sign**, which Lesson 10 addressed. **Waiting for a feeling of peace.** Colossians 3:15 is addressed to a congregation and concerns their life together as one body; it is not a decision-making instrument. A settled conscience matters, and persistent unease may be worth examining, but a feeling of peace is not the decisive test and its absence is not a veto. Jesus experienced profound anguish in Gethsemane while remaining obedient.

"If I make the wrong decision, I will be outside God's will, and everything after it will be off course." This treats God's will as a narrow path across a chasm, one right choice at every fork and every other choice a fall, and it keeps people frozen for fourteen months. Scripture gives another picture. God's revealed will concerns how we are to live: with justice, mercy, faithfulness, love of God and neighbour. Within that there are usually several morally permissible options, and Scripture nowhere promises that God has secretly chosen one and hidden it. This does not mean every option is as good as every other — wisdom is the skill of choosing well among permissible things, and a mistaken decision can cause real harm. But it does not place the person beyond God's reach.

The tool this lesson offers is ten questions answered in writing, followed by five faithful responses. **One:** what decision is actually before me? One sentence with a yes or a no in it, or with named options. **Two:** is any option morally or legally impermissible? Where nothing is ruled out, say plainly that these are permissible options and the decision is a matter of wisdom. **Three and four:** what facts and expertise do I have, and what important information remains obtainable? If something genuinely missing would change the decision and can be obtained in reasonable time, obtaining it is the next step and the sheet pauses. **Five:** who is affected, and who has a legitimate voice? Commitments to a spouse, children or a dependent person may create obligations, and those people may need consulting rather than informing. **Six:** what has relevant counsel said? Write what each person actually said, not what you hoped. **Seven:** what do I desire, and what do I fear? Desire is information, not divine authorisation. Miriam wanted to stop, and when she wrote it down she wept, because she had been treating the wanting as evidence of selfishness for a year. **Eight and nine:** what will each option require, risk and close, and how reversible is it? **Ten:** what is actually preventing a decision? Missing information, courage, fear of someone's reaction, waiting for certainty or peace, wanting someone else to decide, grief at what the decision closes. Naming it places the difficulty where it actually is, which is often not in the decision.

Then five responses, and one is the answer. **Act**: the information is sufficient, the counsel is in, what remains is courage. **Wait for a defined reason**: write what must happen first and when, because a wait without a trigger is a delay wearing wisdom's clothes. **Seek qualified help**: write whom you will ask and about what. **Move to safety**: where there is credible danger, stop the ordinary decision exercise and follow specialist safety guidance. Moving to safety means following an individual safety plan; it does not necessarily mean leaving, confronting the person or announcing your decision immediately, and leaving can itself be a dangerous period. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services now. This comes before everything else on the sheet. **Surrender**: write what is not yours to decide or control. Miriam's sheet ended with act, a date, and one sentence under question ten that she had never let herself write: "I am afraid that if I stop, I will find out I was only valued for what I did." That needed a longer conversation, and she had it with her pastor. But it was not a reason to lead youth ministry for another year.

A woman I will call Miriam had been deciding the same thing for fourteen months, and she could tell me the answer in one sentence.

She had founded her church’s youth ministry eight years earlier and led it ever since. It had grown, and she had grown tired. Her own children were now teenagers who needed her on the evenings she was out; her husband had said, kindly and more than once, that the family was paying for the ministry; her doctor had used the word “exhausted.” She had prayed about stepping down for over a year. She had asked her pastor, who had said he would support whatever she decided and that he thought she needed rest. She had asked two older women she trusted, who had both said the same. She had read the passages about Sabbath and the passages about perseverance and could quote each against the other. She had made lists. Twice she had drafted the email to the pastor and deleted it.

“I know what I should do,” she said, in the first ten minutes. “I’ve known for a year. I just keep waiting to feel sure.”

I asked her what she was waiting to feel sure of. She thought for a long time, and then she said, “That nobody will be disappointed. That it isn’t giving up. That I won’t regret it.”

None of those things can be known in advance. The available information, the relevant counsel, her health and the effect on her family all suggested that further reflection was no longer adding clarity. What remained appeared to be less a lack of information than fear of the decision’s cost, and decisions of that kind do not stop costing because we wait.

### Central Scripture: Proverbs 3:5–6

Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make straight your paths. (Proverbs 3:5–6, ESV) These are among the most quoted verses in Scripture, and among the most quoted at people who are trying to decide something. They deserve to be read in their setting, because the setting changes what they say.

Proverbs 3 is a father speaking to a son. It belongs to the long instruction that opens the book, in which wisdom is commended, folly is warned against, and the fear of the LORD is called the beginning of knowledge. The chapter’s opening verses ask the son to keep the father’s commandments, to bind steadfast love and faithfulness around his neck, and then, in verses 5 and 6, to trust the LORD wholly, to refuse to lean on his own understanding, to acknowledge God in all his ways, and to expect that God will make his paths straight. What follows immediately is a warning against being wise in one’s own eyes, an instruction to honour the LORD with wealth, and a reminder that the LORD disciplines those he loves.

Three things about these verses matter for a person who cannot decide.

First, “do not lean on your own understanding” is not an instruction to stop using your mind. Proverbs is the book of the Bible most devoted to the careful use of understanding; it praises the person who plans, who considers, who takes counsel, who looks where they are going. The picture in the verse is of leaning: resting your whole weight on something. The son is told not to make his own perception the thing he rests on. He is to use his understanding while trusting God rather than his understanding for the outcome. That is a different instruction from “do not think.”

Second, “in all your ways acknowledge him” is not a promise that God will announce the decision. The Hebrew verb is the ordinary word for knowing. In context, acknowledging God means more than mentioning him before a decision; it points toward knowing and honouring him throughout one’s ways. Describing this as living in conscious relationship with God is a reasonable pastoral application, and it is the one this chapter makes. What the verse does not describe is a technique for extracting a specific answer from God about one question.

Third, “he will make straight your paths” is confident covenant wisdom, not a technique for obtaining a preferred result. “Make straight” conveys God’s direction of the path of those who trust and acknowledge him. It does not promise that every individual choice will be easy, immediately validated or free from painful consequences, and it does not tell the trusting person which fork to take. It is the father’s assurance that God directs the path of those who walk with him. That assurance is real, and it is broad. What it does not do is tell Miriam whether to send the email.

What follows in this chapter is a pastoral application of these verses to decision-making, and I want to mark it as such. The passage’s own concern is the shape of a whole life lived in trust. The Faithful Decision Sheet is a tool built in that spirit; it is not what the father of Proverbs 3 had in mind.

### Two Reasons a Decision Stays Open

Lesson 10 was about discernment that becomes obsession: the person who needs another sign, another word, another confirmation before they will act. This chapter is about what comes after discernment, and it begins with a distinction that the rest of the chapter depends on.

Some decisions remain open because important information or consultation is genuinely missing. Others remain open because every responsible part of discernment has been completed, but the person is waiting for a cost-free feeling of certainty. Wisdom is knowing which situation you are in, and this chapter cannot tell you in advance. Its title is not a verdict on your decision. Some readers who have been told by others that they “already know” do not, in fact, know, and the right result of this chapter for them will be to go back and obtain what is missing. The tool at the end is built to allow for that.

But many of the people I sit with in this position are not, on honest examination, short of information. What they are doing is one of these.

Waiting for the decision to feel certain. Certainty of that kind is available for very few decisions, and almost never for the ones that matter. Whether to take the job, end the arrangement, move, step down, speak, stay: these are decisions made with evidence and wisdom, not with certainty, and the feeling of certainty is usually a feeling that arrives afterwards, if at all. Waiting for it beforehand is waiting for something that is not on offer.

Waiting for the decision to stop costing. Miriam’s three fears were all about cost: disappointment, the appearance of quitting, regret. Every real decision closes something. If you are waiting to decide until no one will be disappointed, you are not waiting to decide; you are hoping the decision will be made for you by circumstances, so that the cost belongs to the circumstances and not to you.

Waiting for someone else to say it. Some people know, and go on asking, not because they need more counsel but because they want the decision to be someone else’s. This is different from seeking wise advice, which is a strength, and different again from consulting those who will be affected, which is an obligation. It is the hope that if enough people say the same thing, the responsibility will have been shared out until none of it is left. It does not work that way. Miriam’s pastor and her two friends had said what they thought. The decision was still hers, and she knew it, and that was the difficulty.

Waiting for a sign. Lesson 10 said what needs to be said about this, and I will not repeat it, except to note that a person who has already been given wisdom, counsel and conviction, and who is now asking God for a sign, may be asking him to take a responsibility he has entrusted to them.

Waiting for a feeling of peace. This one deserves its own word, because it is so common among Christians and so often traced to a verse that does not mean what it is made to mean. Colossians 3:15, “let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts,” is addressed to a congregation and concerns the peace that should govern their life together as one body. It is not a decision-making instruction. That is not to say that peace is irrelevant to deciding; a settled conscience matters, and a persistent unease may be a signal worth examining, as we will see. But a feeling of peace is not the decisive test, and its absence is not a veto. Faithful people have made right decisions with their stomach in knots. Jesus experienced profound anguish in Gethsemane while remaining obedient to the Father. Emotional calm is therefore not a reliable test of whether a decision is faithful.

None of these is a moral failing. They are what a mind does when it wants to act rightly and is afraid of what acting will cost. But where the information and counsel are in, they have this in common: they cannot be resolved by more discernment. They are resolved by deciding.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I make the wrong decision, I will be outside God’s will, and everything after it will be off course.”

This picture treats God’s will as a narrow path across a chasm: one right choice at every fork, every other choice a fall. It makes deciding terrifying, and it keeps people like Miriam frozen for fourteen months, because the stakes of being wrong appear infinite.

Scripture gives a different picture. God’s revealed will, the will he has actually made known, concerns how we are to live: with justice, mercy, faithfulness, love of God and neighbour, and obedience to what he has commanded. Within that revealed will there are usually several morally permissible options for any given decision, and Scripture nowhere promises that God has secretly chosen one of them and hidden it for us to find. When Paul planned his journeys, he weighed openings and obstacles and sometimes changed course. When the apostles chose deacons, they looked for wisdom and good repute. When the Jerusalem council wrote to the Gentile believers, they said that a matter “seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us,” a phrase that holds together the Spirit’s guidance and the church’s own deliberation. Decisions in Scripture are made by people who trust God and use their judgement.

This does not mean every option is as good as every other. Some are wiser, some are kinder, some fit the person and the season better. Wisdom is precisely the skill of choosing well among permissible things. A mistaken decision can genuinely depart from wisdom, and it can cause real harm, even when it was made in good faith; a wrong turning taken sincerely is still a wrong turning. But it does not place the person beyond God’s reach or outside his purposes for them. Lesson 7 spoke about regret that is not sin and about what can be repaired, learned from, continued or released. That chapter applies to decisions too.

Nor does it mean that God will visibly correct every mistaken choice. He may. He may also let its consequences run, and be present in them, and bring good out of them slowly. Proverbs 3:6 is an assurance about the direction of a life walked in trust, not a guarantee that any single wrong turn will be flagged before it is taken. That is one more reason to decide with care and counsel. It is not a reason never to decide.

### The Faithful Decision Sheet

Here is the tool this chapter offers. It is a set of ten questions to be answered in writing about one decision, followed by five faithful responses: the four from Lesson 10, with one addition. It draws on everything in the book so far, and it is meant to be used once for a decision, honestly, rather than repeated in the hope of a different feeling. It is a pastoral tool, not a biblical procedure.

One. What decision is actually before me? Write it as one sentence with a yes or a no in it, or with named options. Many decisions feel impossible because several have been fused together. Miriam’s was not “what should my life look like.” It was “will I tell the pastor this month that I am stepping down from leading youth ministry at the end of the term.” Separating the decision from its neighbours, the questions of who would replace her, what she would do instead, and how it would be announced, made it something that could be answered. Those neighbours were real decisions too, but they were later ones.

Two. Is any option morally or legally impermissible? Ask whether Scripture clearly prohibits or requires an option. Then ask whether there are ethical obligations, promises already made, laws, professional duties or effects on vulnerable people that must also be considered. If an option is ruled out, it comes off the page, and that is a real answer. If interpretation is disputed, and sincere Christians do disagree about the application of Scripture to some decisions, do not declare the moral question settled merely because no verse names your situation; write down the disagreement honestly and seek help with it. Where nothing is ruled out, what remains are morally permissible options, and the decision is a matter of wisdom rather than of obedience. Say that to yourself plainly. It changes the weight of the thing. Neither of Miriam’s options was sin.

Three. What facts and relevant expertise do I have? List what is established, what is probable, what has been said and by whom, and what competence you or others bring to the question. If the decision involves a field you are not qualified in, note that here; it will matter at question six.

Four. What important information remains obtainable? This is Lesson 10’s question. Is anything genuinely missing that would change the decision, and can it actually be obtained within a reasonable time? If yes, obtaining it is the next step, and the sheet pauses. If the honest answer is that you have what you need, write that down, because the mind will try to reopen it. Be honest in both directions: do not manufacture a missing fact to avoid deciding, and do not declare the facts complete because you are tired of looking.

Five. Who is affected, and who has a legitimate voice? Write who will be materially affected by each option, and who has a legitimate say. Another person should not control a decision that belongs to you, but commitments to a spouse, children, a business partner, a church or a dependent person may create genuine obligations, and those people may need to be consulted rather than informed. Consultation is not the same as surrendering agency. Miriam’s husband and children were not advisers on her decision; they were people to whom she had obligations, and their needs were evidence in it.

Six. What has relevant counsel said? Write what each person you consulted actually said. Not what you hoped they would say, and not only the counsel of the one person who agreed with your fear. Seek counsel relevant to the subject: a small, appropriate circle for a matter of wisdom, and qualified advisers for matters of law, money, health or safety. Different questions may need different advisers. But if you find yourself widening the circle after the counsel has come in, ask whether you are seeking wisdom or seeking a different verdict. Note where the counsel agreed, where it differed, and what it did not address.

Seven. What do I desire, and what do I fear? Write what you actually want. Desire is information: it tells you about your capacities, your fatigue, your gifts, your fears, and sometimes it tells you what you have known for a long time. It is not divine authorisation, and a strong desire is not a sign that God has spoken; but neither is it something to be suspicious of automatically. Miriam wanted to stop. When she wrote it down, she wept, because she had been treating the wanting as evidence of selfishness for a year, and on the page it looked like something else: a tired woman telling the truth. Strong relief at one option can be informative, but it may reflect wisdom, exhaustion, avoidance or temporary emotion; treat it as evidence to consider, not a verdict. Fear is information too. Write what you are afraid of, and ask briefly, in the way Lesson 4 taught, what it would mean to you if it happened. Explore this briefly. Do not keep digging for a hidden explanation, and do not assume that the first answer is a revelation from God.

Eight. What will each option require, risk and close? For each option, write what it will demand afterwards, what could go wrong, and what it closes. Some decisions create ongoing commitments of the kind Lesson 7 called “continue”: responsibilities that will follow the decision for a long time. Every real decision closes something, and naming what it closes is part of counting the cost honestly.

Nine. How reversible is it? Some decisions can be revisited in six months; some cannot. Reversibility changes how much evidence, consultation, contingency planning and time a decision reasonably deserves. No decision can be made with complete certainty, but a reversible decision can be made on the balance of wisdom and adjusted, while an irreversible one deserves proportionately more care, appropriate review, and never a decision taken in the heat of a strong feeling.

Ten. What is actually preventing a decision? This is the question the whole chapter has been building toward. Write the honest answer. Missing information (then return to question four). Lack of courage. Fear of a particular person’s reaction. Waiting for a feeling of certainty or of peace. Wanting someone else to decide. Waiting for a sign. Grief at what the decision closes. Fear of being wrong. External pressure. Several of these may be true at once. Naming them does not remove them, but it places the difficulty where it actually is, which is often not in the decision.

Then the five responses, and one of them is the answer.

Act: the decision is mine, the information is sufficient, the counsel is in, and what remains is courage. Write what I will do and when.

Wait for a defined reason: action will be needed, but something specific must happen first. Write what it is, and when it is expected. A wait without a trigger is a delay wearing wisdom’s clothes; Lesson 17 said the same about “later.”

Seek qualified help: I lack information, skill or support that I cannot supply myself. Write whom I will ask, and about what.

Move to safety: there is credible immediate danger. Contact emergency or specialist support, and do not announce a plan if doing so could increase risk. This is separate from ordinary action, and it comes before everything else on the sheet.

Surrender: the decision is not mine to make, or its outcome is not mine to control. Write what I am releasing, and to whom it belongs.

Miriam’s sheet ended with act, a date, and one sentence under question ten that she said she had never let herself write: “I am afraid that if I stop, I will find out I was only valued for what I did.” That sentence needed a longer conversation, and she had it, with her pastor, over the following weeks. But it was not a reason to lead youth ministry for another year.

> **Before acting on a major or difficult-to-reverse**
>
> decision. If the sheet ends with act and the decision cannot easily be undone—ending a marriage, resigning without another post, moving abroad, a large financial commitment, cutting off contact—do not act on the sheet alone. Seek the kind of review the subject requires. That may involve a spouse, a counsellor, a doctor, a lawyer, a regulated financial adviser, a safeguarding professional or a trusted pastor, depending on what the decision is. Keep the review bounded, so that it does not become the certainty-seeking of Lesson 10, but prioritise relevance and competence over any particular number of people. Where the decision concerns leaving abuse, safety advice may need to come before disclosure to family or church leaders. If you cannot name anyone you would show the page to, that is itself worth noticing, and a pastor or counsellor may be the person to start with.

### Practical Application

Complete the sheet once, in writing, for the decision that has been waiting longest. Do not do it in the evening if evenings are when the decision circles; do it at a time of day when you think clearly.

If the sheet ends with act, set the date. If it is appropriate and safe, tell a supportive person the decision or the next step. Miriam sent the email on a Tuesday because she had told her husband on Sunday that Tuesday was the day. The telling is not accountability in the anxious sense; it is a way of making the decision exist outside your head, where it can no longer be quietly reopened every night. But do not disclose it to someone likely to pressure, retaliate, interfere or place you in danger. The person to tell is the person who will help you keep it, not the person you are afraid of.

Expect the decision to be reopened anyway. The mind will bring it back with a new angle, a new fear, a person you had not considered. When it does, do not redo the sheet. Ask one question: has anything actually changed? A changed feeling alone does not automatically invalidate the decision, but it may deserve brief attention. Ask whether it points to new facts, an overlooked obligation, coercion, danger, or a value that was not considered. If it does, look at that honestly. If it does not, then it may simply be the emotional cost of acting, and this is Lesson 13’s work rather than Lesson 18’s: notice, name, and return to what you decided. The decision was made with wisdom and counsel. A worse feeling on a Thursday is not by itself new evidence.

Expect, too, that the cost you foresaw may arrive. Someone may be disappointed. Miriam’s youth group’s parents were, for a few weeks. That was the cost, and she had known it, and its arrival was not a sign that she had been wrong. A decision is not proved wrong by the fact that it cost what you knew it would cost. Nor, though, does anticipated cost automatically prove the decision right. Consequences should be watched, especially where harm to dependants, health or safety develops beyond what was reasonably expected; that is new information, and it goes back to the sheet.

If the sheet ends with wait, put the trigger on the later list from Lesson 17, with its review date, and do not carry the decision in the meantime. If it ends with seek qualified help, make the appointment or the call this week; the sheet has told you the next step, and the next step is not more thinking. If it ends with move to safety, do that first and only that. If it ends with surrender, use the sixth movement of Lesson 16’s prayer, and then turn to what is yours.

And when the decision is made and you later discover that your judgement was mistaken, return to Lesson 7. Face the truth of it, accept the consequences that are appropriate, repair what can be repaired, learn what can be learned, continue what must be continued, and release the rest. A wrong decision does not place you beyond God’s reach, but his grace does not make the consequences unreal, and pretending otherwise is not faith. His path is a whole life walked in trust, and it has room in it for a wrong turning honestly owned.
