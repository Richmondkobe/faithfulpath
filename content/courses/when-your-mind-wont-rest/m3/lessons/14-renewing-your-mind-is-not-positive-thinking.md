# Lesson 14: Renewing Your Mind Is Not Positive Thinking

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish forced optimism and unsupported reassurance from biblical renewal, and practise replacing one reassurance you cannot support with a sentence grounded in truth.

## Before you begin

This lesson is not against hope, gratitude, encouragement or expecting that good remains possible; those are healthy and biblical. It addresses forced optimism: unsupported positive claims used to deny evidence, suppress emotion or guarantee an outcome God has not promised. Two cautions before you begin. Persistent low mood, loss of pleasure, hopelessness, worthlessness or difficulty functioning may indicate depression or another condition and deserves assessment by a doctor or qualified mental-health professional rather than another attempt at thinking well. If hopelessness includes thoughts of suicide or self-harm, use Finding Help Where You Live and tell a safe person today. If you may act on those thoughts, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact emergency or urgent crisis services now and do not remain alone. And if you have been told by others that your suffering is caused by your own negative thinking or lack of faith, this course does not believe that; you may need to limit conversations in which your suffering is blamed on your faith or your vocabulary. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for one situation.

## Key Scripture: Romans 12:1–2 (ESV)

> I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship. Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect.

The "therefore" points back over eleven chapters describing the mercies of God. Renewal here is oriented towards discernment: a mind that can see clearly and judge rightly, not one trained to see only what is pleasant.

## One truth to carry with you

Renewing your mind does not require you to deny painful facts or manufacture confidence about an outcome God has not promised. Paul's renewal is oriented towards discernment: a mind that can look at what is actually the case, including hard things, and recognise what God asks in the midst of them. A renewed mind becomes more truthful, not less; more capable of hope, not less capable of grief. God does not promise that the business will survive or the diagnosis will be clear. For those who belong to Christ, he has promised that loss cannot place them outside his covenant care. So you can say the true sentence — I do not know how this will turn out, and God is with me in it — and find that it does not need defending. It is simply the case, and it can be lived in whichever way the year goes.

## The lesson

A woman I will call Naomi came to see me some months after her husband's business had begun to fail. The accountant had said plainly that without new work the company would not survive the year. She was not, when she came, primarily distressed about the business but about her faith. A friend at church had told her she must not speak negatively over the situation, that fear was a lack of faith, and that if she declared God's provision and refused every thought of failure, the breakthrough would come. Naomi had tried. She had refused to look at the accounts, and had stopped her husband discussing what they would do if the company closed. And underneath the declarations she was more frightened than she had ever been, because now she was frightened of the failure and frightened that her fear was causing it. "I am saying all the right things," she told me, "and I feel like a liar. And I cannot plan anything, because planning would be doubting."

Three things in Romans 12:1–2 matter here. First, "be transformed" is passive: renewal is not a self-manufactured achievement but God's work, in which believers actively participate, since Paul also calls them to present themselves, resist conformity, test and discern. Second, the renewal is oriented towards discernment, "that by testing you may discern what is the will of God". A renewed mind is one that can see clearly and judge rightly, not one trained to see only what is pleasant. Third, Paul's vision is larger than private self-talk: the renewed mind appears in sober self-assessment, sincere love, generosity, patience in tribulation, weeping with those who weep. It is not a mind that has stopped weeping, but one that weeps truthfully and hopes truthfully at the same time. One more thing plainly: Paul is not contrasting Christian renewal with a modern philosophy of positive thinking, and that comparison is my pastoral application rather than his point.

Four things are sometimes mistaken for biblical renewal. **Denial** refuses to look at what is true because it is painful, and Scripture never commends it: Jesus wept at a grave he was about to empty, and Paul wrote that in Asia he despaired of life itself. **False reassurance** says something comforting that may not be true. A frightened mind usually knows the reassurance is unsupported, so the sentence has to be repeated, louder, to hold the fear down, which is where Naomi had arrived. Scripture's promises are differently shaped: not every desired outcome, but God's presence, forgiveness, sustaining grace, resurrection and life with Christ. **Forced optimism** treats any negative expectation as a failure of faith, and so cannot plan for a possibility it cannot name. **Suppression** pushes the unwanted thought down and lays a hopeful sentence over it. Each of these makes peace depend on the situation turning out well. Biblical renewal makes peace depend on God, and is therefore free to be honest about the situation.

"Faith means refusing to say anything negative. If I acknowledge the problem, I am agreeing with it." Christians hold different views about the place of spoken confession, and there is a genuine biblical practice of speaking truth to oneself; the psalmist tells his own soul to hope in God. But this belief goes further, and it needs examining. Scripture does not teach that acknowledging an unwelcome fact mechanically causes it; Naomi's fear did not lose the contracts. It confuses acknowledging a fact with endorsing it: to say "the company may close" is to describe a possibility accurately so that it can be prayed about and prepared for. Jesus told his disciples plainly that they would have tribulation, and then said, "Take heart; I have overcome the world." It leaves the person alone with the fear, because a fear that cannot be spoken cannot be shared or planned around. And it misplaces the weight of faith. The person who says "God will certainly save the business" has staked their faith on something God has not said. The person who says "I do not know what will happen, and God is faithful" has staked it where it belongs, and will still be standing whichever way the year goes.

So what does renewal sound like? A reassurance is a sentence that makes you feel better and may not be true; a biblical truth is a sentence that is true whether or not you feel better, and it is more durable because it does not have to be defended. *It will be fine* is a prediction you do not know; the truth is *I do not know how this will turn out. God knows, and he is with me in it.* *I must not think about failure* is suppression; the truth is *The company may close. If it does, we will grieve, and we will need a plan, and God will still be God.* Notice what the true sentences share: they are honest about uncertainty and loss, they locate hope in God's character, and they leave room for grief, planning and prayer that says what is actually the case. A caution about promises: not every promise in Scripture was made to every reader in every circumstance, and treating one made to someone else as a guarantee for your business or your health can collapse your faith when it does not come true.

The practice, then. Listen to the sentences you have been using to comfort yourself and write them down exactly; many people are surprised to find their comforts are mostly predictions they cannot support. Test each one with the question from Lesson 13: is this true, is it the whole truth, has God actually said this? If a sentence fails, rewrite it as a sentence that is true. Then say it, and notice that it does not need defending. Then do what the true sentence permits, because very often it opens a door the reassurance had shut: *the company may close* permitted Naomi to open the accounts, to let her husband plan, and to pray for provision without demanding a particular form of it. And be patient. Paul says "be transformed", and transformation is slow. Naomi opened the accounts, and the company closed in the autumn. She told me later that the closure was hard, but that the worst part of the year had been the months of declaring that it would not happen.

A woman I will call Naomi came to see me some months after her husband’s business had begun to fail. The signs were not ambiguous. Two major contracts had been lost, the bank had declined to extend the overdraft, and an accountant had told them plainly that without new work the company would not survive the year.

Naomi was not, when she came, primarily distressed about the business. She was distressed about her faith. A friend at church had told her that she must not “speak negatively” over the situation, that fear was a lack of faith, and that if she declared God’s provision and refused every thought of failure, the breakthrough would come. Naomi had tried. For weeks she had told herself, and anyone who asked, that God was going to turn it around. She had refused to look at the accounts. She had stopped her husband from discussing what they would do if the company closed, because to discuss it was to “give the enemy a foothold.”

And underneath the declarations, she said, she was more frightened than she had ever been, because now she was frightened of the failure and frightened that her fear was causing it.

“I am saying all the right things,” she told me, “and I feel like a liar. And I cannot plan anything, because planning would be doubting.”

We opened Romans 12 together, and I asked her to notice that the renewing of the mind Paul describes there is not the same thing as the practice her friend had recommended. In some ways it is nearly the opposite.

Before going further, I should say what this chapter is and is not against. It is not against hope, gratitude, encouragement, or expecting that good remains possible; those are healthy and biblical. It is addressing forced optimism: the use of unsupported positive claims to deny evidence, suppress emotion or guarantee an outcome God has not promised.

### Central Scripture: Romans 12:1–2

I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship. Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect. (Romans 12:1–2, ESV) The word “therefore” points backwards. Paul has spent eleven chapters describing the mercies of God: the gospel, justification by faith, the gift of the Spirit, the security of those whom God has called, the unsearchable wisdom of his purposes. Romans 12 begins the response. In view of all that, Paul says, here is how to live.

Three things in these two verses matter for our subject.

First, “be transformed” is passive, suggesting that renewal is not a self-manufactured achievement. In the wider witness of Scripture, Christians understand this transformation as God’s work in which believers actively participate. Romans 12 itself calls them to present themselves, resist conformity, test and discern. Paul is not describing a method for controlling one’s thoughts. He is describing a life being reshaped by mercy.

Second, the renewal is oriented towards discernment: “that by testing you may discern what is the will of God.” A renewed mind is one that can see clearly and judge rightly. It is not a mind that has been trained to see only what is pleasant. If anything, it is the reverse: a mind that can look at what is actually the case, including hard things, and recognise what God asks in the midst of them.

Third, Paul’s vision is larger than private self-talk. The renewed mind appears, in the rest of the chapter, in sober self-assessment, service within Christ’s body, sincere love, generosity, hospitality, patience in tribulation, peace, weeping with those who weep, and the refusal of revenge. Personal thought patterns matter, but Romans 12 describes a whole life and community being reshaped by God’s mercy. The renewed mind of Romans 12 is not a mind that has stopped weeping. It is a mind that weeps truthfully and hopes truthfully at the same time.

One more thing should be said plainly. Paul is not contrasting Christian renewal specifically with a modern philosophy of positive thinking; that comparison is a pastoral application, and I make it as one. The “age” to which believers must not conform encompasses patterns of life opposed to God’s mercy and will, of which the refusal to face the truth is only one.

As a pastoral application, then: biblical renewal is not the practice of replacing every painful thought with a pleasant one. It is the continuing transformation of judgement, attention, desire and action in response to God’s mercy. A renewed mind becomes more truthful, not less; more capable of hope, not less capable of grief; and more ready to obey, not more determined to control the outcome.

### Four Things Renewal Is Not

Because “renew your mind” is so often taken to mean “think positively,” it is worth setting the two apart carefully. Here are four things that are sometimes mistaken for biblical renewal.

Denial. Denial refuses to look at what is true because it is painful. Naomi’s refusal to open the accounts was denial. Scripture never commends it. The Psalms look directly at enemies, illness, betrayal and death. Jesus wept at a grave he was about to empty. Paul wrote to the Corinthians that in Asia he had been “so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself.” Faith is not the practice of not looking. It is the practice of looking, and then looking further, to God.

False reassurance. False reassurance says something comforting that may not be true. It will be fine. God will not let this happen. Everything works out. Sometimes these sentences are said by friends who mean well; sometimes we say them to ourselves. The difficulty is that a frightened mind usually knows the reassurance is unsupported, and so the sentence has to be repeated, louder, to hold the fear down, which is where Naomi had arrived. Scripture’s promises are more solid and differently shaped. They do not guarantee every desired immediate outcome, but they offer something deeper: God’s presence, forgiveness, sustaining grace, final justice, resurrection and life with Christ. God does not promise that the business will survive. He promises that he will not leave. And that promised presence does not guarantee that you will always feel his nearness or be spared painful consequences; it means that loss does not place those who belong to Christ beyond his covenant care.

Forced optimism. Forced optimism treats the expression of any negative expectation as a failure of faith. It cannot say “this may not turn out well,” and so it cannot plan for the possibility that it will not. This leaves the person unable to do what Lesson 6 called preparation, and it makes ordinary prudence feel like unbelief. Naomi could not let her husband plan for closure, and closure was, on the evidence, likely.

Suppression. We looked at this in Lesson 13. Suppression tries to force an unwanted thought out of awareness. Forced optimism is often suppression with a slogan attached: the fearful thought is pushed down and a hopeful sentence is laid over it. Renewal, by contrast, brings the thought into the light, tests it, and answers it with what is actually true, which may be a harder truth than the slogan.

There is a common thread. Each of these makes peace depend on the situation turning out well, and then works to convince the mind that it will. Biblical renewal makes peace depend on God, and is therefore free to be honest about the situation.

It may help to set the two side by side. Forced optimism guarantees a preferred outcome; biblical hope trusts God without controlling the outcome. Forced optimism avoids painful facts; biblical hope faces them truthfully. Forced optimism treats fear as dangerous; biblical hope brings fear honestly to God. Forced optimism makes planning feel faithless; biblical hope makes faithful preparation possible. Forced optimism suppresses grief; biblical hope allows lament and hope together. Forced optimism depends on confident feelings and must be repeated to maintain certainty; biblical hope rests on God’s character and promises, and can admit uncertainty without collapsing.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“Faith means refusing to say anything negative. If I acknowledge the problem, I am agreeing with it.”

Christians hold different views about the place of spoken confession in the life of faith, and some traditions place real weight on declaring God’s promises aloud. I do not want to caricature that, and there is a genuine biblical practice of speaking truth to oneself: the psalmist tells his own soul to hope in God. But the belief in the sentence above goes further than that, and it needs to be examined.

It misunderstands what words can do. Scripture teaches that words can bless, wound, deceive, encourage and shape relationships, and it calls believers to pray in faith. But it does not teach that acknowledging an unwelcome fact mechanically causes that event, or that sufficiently confident declarations guarantee a desired outcome. Naomi’s fear did not cause the contracts to be lost, and her declarations could not win them back.

It confuses acknowledging a fact with endorsing it. To say “the company may close” is not to want it to close, or to cause it to close.

It is to describe a possibility accurately so that it can be prayed about and prepared for. Jesus told his disciples plainly that they would have tribulation. He was not speaking negatively over them. He was telling them the truth, and then he said, “Take heart; I have overcome the world.”

It leaves the person alone with the fear. Because the fear cannot be spoken, it cannot be shared, prayed about honestly, or planned around. It goes underground, where it grows. Naomi’s friend had, without meaning to, cut her off from every ordinary means of help.

And it misplaces the weight of faith. Biblical faith is not confidence that a particular outcome will occur. It is confidence in God, who has not promised the outcome. The person who says “God will certainly save the business” has staked their faith on something God has not said. The person who says “I do not know what will happen to the business, and God is faithful” has staked it where it belongs, and will still be standing whichever way the year goes.

### From False Reassurance to Biblical Truth

If renewal is not forced optimism, what does it actually sound like? Here is the practice this chapter offers: to take the reassurances you have been giving yourself and replace them with truths that will hold.

A reassurance is a sentence that makes you feel better and may not be true. A biblical truth is a sentence that is true whether or not you feel better. The second is more durable, and, in the end, more comforting, because it does not have to be defended.

Consider the difference.

It will be fine. This is a prediction, and you do not know it. The truth is: I do not know how this will turn out. God knows, and he is with me in it.

God will not let this happen. This is a claim about God’s intentions that he has not made. The truth is: God has not promised me this outcome. He has promised never to leave me, and he has told me that in this world I will have trouble.

I must not think about failure. This is suppression. The truth is: The company may close. If it does, we will grieve, and we will need a plan, and God will still be God. I can look at that.

If I have enough faith, this will work out. This makes the outcome depend on the quality of your believing. The truth is: My faith is in God, not in an outcome. He is faithful even when my faith is weak, and the result does not rest on my ability to feel confident.

Everything happens for a reason, so I should not be upset. This uses a truth about providence to forbid an honest feeling. The truth is: I trust that this suffering is not beyond God’s knowledge, presence or redemptive purpose, even though I cannot yet see what he may do through it. I am allowed to grieve what is painful.

Notice what the true sentences have in common. They are honest about uncertainty. They are honest about the possibility of loss. They locate hope in God’s character rather than in an outcome. And they leave room for grief, for planning, and for prayer that says what is actually the case. That is what a renewed mind sounds like. It is quieter than the declarations, and it can be lived in.

A caution about promises. Scripture is full of them, and a renewed mind draws on them. But not every promise in the Bible was made to every reader in every circumstance. Some were made to Israel at a particular moment, some to a particular person, some concern the age to come. Taking a promise made to someone else, in another situation, and treating it as a guarantee for your business or your health can set you up for a collapse of faith when it does not come true. The promises given to those who are in Christ include forgiveness, the presence of the Spirit, access to God, sustaining grace, belonging to Christ’s people, resurrection and final restoration. Each should still be understood in its biblical context, but together they are more than enough to build on, and they do not depend on the year going well.

### Practical Application

Begin by listening to the sentences you have been using to comfort yourself. Write them down, exactly as you say them. Many people are surprised to find that their comforts are mostly predictions they cannot support.

Then test each one, using the question from Lesson 13. Is this true? Is it the whole truth? Has God actually said this? If a sentence fails, do not simply discard it and sit with the fear. Rewrite it as a sentence that is true, in the way the examples above are true: honest about what you do not know, honest about what may be lost, and anchored in what God has actually promised.

Then say the true sentence, aloud if it helps, and notice that it does not need defending. You do not have to repeat it louder. It is simply the case.

Then do what the true sentence permits. Very often a true sentence opens a door that the reassurance had shut. The company may close permits Naomi to open the accounts, to let her husband plan, to talk to the accountant about an orderly closure, to pray for provision without demanding a particular form of it. Renewal is not only a change in what you say. It is a change in what you can then do, and in how you live among the people God has placed around you.

And be patient with the process. Paul says “be transformed,” and transformation is slow. A mind that has spent years reassuring itself will not learn to speak the truth in a week. You are not building a new technique. You are being reshaped, and the reshaping is God’s, over time, through his Word, in his church. Your part is to stop relying on comforting claims that cannot bear the weight placed on them, and to practise telling yourself the truth with kindness.

Naomi, for what it is worth, opened the accounts. The company closed in the autumn. She and her husband grieved it properly, planned the closure well, and found work within a few months. She told me later that the closure was hard, but that it was not the worst part of the year. The worst part had been the months of declaring that it would not happen.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that are true for you, adapt them or remain silent.*

Father, you are not offended by the truth, because you are the truth. I confess that I have sometimes told myself comforting things you never said, and called it faith. I have refused to look at what was in front of me, and I have staked my hope on outcomes you did not promise.

Renew my mind. Not by making me see only what is pleasant, but by teaching me to see what is true and to see you in it. Give me the honesty to say what may be lost, the courage to plan for it, and the hope that rests on your character rather than on the way things turn out.

Where I have been afraid to grieve, let me grieve. Where I have been afraid to plan, let me plan. And where I have been afraid that my fear was causing my trouble, lift that burden from me; it was never mine to carry.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
