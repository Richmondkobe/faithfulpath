# Lesson 17: Give Tomorrow Back to God

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish proportionate preparation from mentally living in an imagined future, and sort one concern into Today, Later or Release for now.

## Before you begin

Some readers' concern about tomorrow reflects real and pressing conditions: an income that does not stretch to the end of the month, insecure housing, a visa or contract that may not be renewed, a diagnosis with an uncertain course, a relationship in which you are not safe. If that is you, your thoughts about the future are not a spiritual problem to be corrected, and this lesson is not asking you to stop thinking about money or safety. What it offers is a way to separate the part of tomorrow that needs action or help now from the part that cannot be acted on yet. That first part may need people: a debt adviser, a housing service, a doctor, a solicitor, a domestic-abuse service, your church's practical support. Seeking that help is preparation of the wisest kind. If worry about the future is broad, persistent, hard to control and has gone on for months, affecting sleep, concentration or daily functioning, describe it to a doctor or qualified mental-health professional; persistent anxiety about many things is treatable and this course is not a treatment for it. If the future feels unbearable and you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, use Finding Help Where You Live and tell someone safe today; if you may act, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact emergency or urgent crisis services now and do not remain alone. And do not begin a full sorting in bed: if a future concern arrives in the dark, use only the Night-Time Thought Download, if writing will not increase your alertness, and return to this sorting exercise in daylight.

## Key Scripture: Matthew 6:25–34 (ESV)

> But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you. Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble.

The passage begins with a "therefore", following Jesus' teaching about treasure and the impossibility of serving both God and money. The anxiety he addresses is anxiety about provision, and he answers it with the Father rather than with a technique.

## One truth to carry with you

Wisdom allows you to prepare for tomorrow without demanding control of it. Jesus does not say tomorrow will be fine; he says tomorrow "will be anxious for itself", and that each day has trouble sufficient to it. He assumes trouble. What he declines to do is let tomorrow's trouble be added to today's. So the question is never whether a Christian may think about the future — the ant stores in summer, Joseph gathered grain, the builder counts the cost — but whether the thinking is producing readiness or simply moving you into a day that has not arrived. Do what today's wisdom actually asks, write down what waits for a date you can name, seek the help that is needed now, and give the rest back. For those who belong to Christ, your Father knows what you need, and he will still be Father tomorrow.

## The lesson

A man I will call Philip lived through the same eleven weeks every evening for a month, and none of them had happened yet. His firm had announced a restructuring; roles like his might be merged, though nobody had said his would be. Philip did the sensible things quickly: updated his CV, spoke to his manager, and sat down with his wife to look at what the household could manage if his income stopped. That took an afternoon. Then, each evening after the children were in bed, he went further: the meeting in which he was told, the months of applications, the savings running down, the smaller house, the conversation with his son about changing schools. "I keep telling myself I'm being prepared," he said. "But I've prepared for the same thing forty times, and I'm no more ready than I was the first night. I'm just more frightened."

Jesus has been speaking about treasure, and about the impossibility of serving both God and money. Then: "do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink." The anxiety he addresses is anxiety about provision, and his listeners were not wealthy people. To that he answers not with a technique but with the Father. The birds do not sow or reap, and the Father feeds them — not because they are inactive, but because their lives are sustained by him rather than secured through anxious mastery of the future. His promise must not be turned into a guarantee of uninterrupted material security; Scripture knows famine and poverty, and Jesus himself had nowhere to lay his head. Nor do his words permit the church to spiritualise another person's material need instead of helping to meet it. Only then does verse 34 arrive: tomorrow "will be anxious for itself", and each day has trouble sufficient to it. He assumes trouble, and declines to let tomorrow's be added to today's. And, as Lesson 3 said, "do not be anxious" is a genuine instruction and not a condemnation of an involuntary state.

So the question is not whether to think about tomorrow but how to tell preparing from living in it, because from the inside the two feel similar. **Preparation moves towards an identifiable result**: clearer understanding, a decision, a plan, a question to ask someone, a next step. When Philip's Saturday ended, he and his wife understood their position in a way they had not that morning; when his evenings ended, he understood nothing he had not understood the evening before. **Preparation has an end**, whereas living in tomorrow does not finish, because the future has no edge. **Preparation works with what is established**: the restructuring was established; the loss of his role was not. **Preparation leaves you with a next step**, whereas the person who has lived through it forty times knows in detail what they will feel and has no clearer idea what they will do. **Preparation may include emotion; it does not consist of it.** The distinction is whether engaging the future produces proportionate readiness or repeatedly immerses you in an imagined experience.

"If I really trusted God, I would not think about tomorrow at all." This usually harms the most responsible people in a congregation: the parent with the budget, the carer, the person whose job it is to plan. They hear "do not be anxious about tomorrow", conclude that their planning is unbelief, and add guilt to the concern they already carried. Jesus is not forbidding thought about the future but a particular relationship to it: taking up tomorrow's trouble as though it were already today's. Philip had the opposite version: "if I do not think it through now, I will be caught out later." Did the fortieth rehearsal find him readier than the first? He was honest enough to say it had not.

When you notice that you have moved into tomorrow, everything you are carrying there can be placed in one of three groups, with a check that comes first. **The check: is anything here urgent?** Before sorting, ask whether the tomorrow you have been living in contains credible danger or immediate need — abuse, violence, stalking, imminent homelessness, a medical symptom that should not wait, a child at risk. If it does, that item does not go into any group. It goes to the top of the page, and the response is to seek safety or qualified help now. This lesson is about worry that outruns the evidence, not about danger the evidence supports: a course must never be the reason someone treated a real threat as overthinking. **Today.** Some of what you carry is a genuine responsibility that exists now and can be acted on today. The response is to act or seek help, and then to stop, because it has been done. **Later.** Some will require action, but not yet; it waits for a date, a decision by someone else, a result. Write it down with the point at which it becomes today's business; the mind lets go of a thing more readily when it knows the thing is kept somewhere. **Release for now.** Some is not presently actionable, because it depends on something that has not happened, or lies beyond your role altogether. "Release" means release from present mental work, not a declaration that the matter will never need attention. The smaller house belonged here; every evening spent in its rooms was taken from the house Philip was in. The four ordinary responses — act, wait, seek help, surrender — apply after the urgency check; where danger is present, the prior response is to move to safety or seek urgent help.

Three cautions about sorting honestly. Do not use "later" to avoid what belongs to today: if a frightening phone call could be made now, it is today's, and putting it on the later list with a comfortable trigger is avoidance wearing the clothes of wisdom. Do not use "release" to avoid "seek help": if the future you are living in is financial or legal or medical and you lack the knowledge to sort it, the right group is often "today", and the action is to ask a qualified person. And do not measure the sorting by how you feel afterwards; correct sorting may not make you calmer, and continued anxiety does not prove an item was placed wrongly.

In practice: notice the crossing. For Philip the sign was that he had begun to feel the meeting rather than plan for it. Vivid detail can be a signal, though not a test, since some people naturally think in pictures. Name where you actually are: *it is a Tuesday evening in September and I am in my own kitchen*. That is not a rebuke; it is a location. Then sort what you are carrying on paper, do today's item and let it be done, review the later list at a set time rather than continuously, give the rest back in words, and return to the day. When tomorrow arrived again, Philip named where he was, checked whether anything had changed, and gave it back again. It became briefer with practice; it did not become unnecessary, and I did not tell him it would. What he regretted was not the concern, which had been reasonable, but the thirty evenings spent in a house he never had to move to, while the children he was afraid of failing were asking for him in the actual one.

A man I will call Philip lived through the same eleven weeks every evening for a month, and none of them had happened yet.

His firm had announced a restructuring. There would be a consultation period, and decisions were expected in eleven weeks. Roles like his might be merged; nobody had said his would be. Philip did the sensible things quickly. He updated his CV, spoke frankly with his manager, and sat down with his wife one Saturday to look at what the household could manage if his income stopped for a while. That took an afternoon.

Then, each evening after the children were in bed, he went further. He sat through the meeting in which he was told. He heard the wording. He drove home and told his wife, and watched her face. He walked through months of applications, the interviews that went nowhere, the savings running down, the smaller house, the conversation with his son about changing schools, the look on his father-in-law’s face at Christmas. He fell asleep in a life he did not live and woke tired from it. By morning, nothing had changed except him.

“I keep telling myself I’m being prepared,” he said. “But I’ve prepared for the same thing forty times, and I’m no more ready than I was the first night. I’m just more frightened, and I’m hardly present with the children.”

Philip had confused two things that feel identical from the inside: preparing for tomorrow, and living in it before it arrives. The preparation had been finished in an afternoon. What filled the evenings was something else, and it was quietly taking the days he actually had.

### Central Scripture: Matthew 6:25–34

But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you. Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble. (Matthew 6:33– 34, ESV) Lesson 6 drew on the final verse of this passage. Read the whole of it now, verses 25 to 34, and read what comes before it as well, because the passage begins with a “therefore.”

Jesus has just been speaking about treasure: where it is stored, what the eye is fixed on, and the impossibility of serving both God and money. Then he says, “Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, nor about your body, what you will put on.” The anxiety he addresses is, in the first instance, anxiety about provision: food, drink, clothing, the ordinary needs of a body in a world where those needs are not guaranteed. His listeners were not wealthy people. Many of them knew what it was to wonder whether there would be enough.

To that anxiety Jesus does not answer with a technique. He answers with the Father. Look at the birds: they do not sow or reap or gather into barns, and the Father feeds them. Jesus is not commending inactivity. The birds still live as creatures do, foraging and building and raising their young. His point is that their lives are sustained by the Father rather than secured through anxious mastery of the future. Look at the lilies, who do not labour at their clothing and are clothed more gloriously than Solomon. Your heavenly Father knows what you need. The Gentiles, who do not know him as Father, run after these things; you have a Father who knows. So seek his kingdom first, and let the rest be added by him.

Jesus’ promise must not be turned into a guarantee that every faithful Christian will always have uninterrupted material security. Scripture itself knows famine, poverty, persecution and believers who share their resources because others genuinely lack. Paul knew hunger; the Jerusalem church needed a collection; Jesus himself had nowhere to lay his head. His words here call his disciples away from organising life around anxious pursuit of provision and toward trust in the Father and participation in his kingdom. They do not permit the church to spiritualise another person’s material need instead of helping to meet it, and they do not ask the reader in real hardship to pretend that the hardship is not there.

Only then does verse 34 arrive, and it is worth reading slowly, because it is more realistic than it is sometimes made to sound. Jesus does not say tomorrow will be fine. He says tomorrow “will be anxious for itself,” and that each day has trouble sufficient to it. He assumes trouble. What he declines to do is let tomorrow’s trouble be added to today’s. There is enough in one day. Tomorrow’s share will be carried when tomorrow comes, and the Father who feeds the birds will still be Father then.

Two things should be said clearly before we go further.

First, the passage is about trust in the Father and seeking his kingdom. It is not a set of instructions for managing thoughts, and what follows in this chapter is a pastoral application of it, not an exposition of everything it means. I want to keep its centre in view: anxiety about tomorrow is answered by who God is, not primarily by better mental habits, though better habits can help us attend to who he is.

Second, “do not be anxious” is a genuine instruction, and it is not a condemnation of an involuntary state. Lesson 3 said this and it needs saying again here. An involuntary bodily reaction to uncertainty should not, by itself, be treated as deliberate disobedience. Jesus’ instruction calls the whole person toward trust without requiring us to deny symptoms we did not choose. What the instruction addresses is what we do with tomorrow: whether we take it up and carry it as though it were already ours to bear.

### Preparing for Tomorrow and Living in It

Scripture commends thinking ahead. The ant stores in summer. Joseph gathered grain in the years of plenty. James does not tell merchants to stop planning their journeys; he tells them to plan with “if the Lord wills,” which is a different matter. Jesus’ image of a builder counting the cost belongs primarily to his teaching about the cost of discipleship, but as a secondary application it assumes that responsible people consider foreseeable requirements before committing themselves. Nothing in Matthew 6 forbids Philip’s afternoon with his CV and his budget. That afternoon was the kind of wisdom the book of Proverbs praises.

So the question is not whether a Christian may think about tomorrow. It is how to tell the difference between preparing for it and living in it before it arrives. From the inside the two feel very similar, because both involve tomorrow’s images and both feel like responsibility. Here are some of the ways they differ.

Preparation moves toward an identifiable result. Clearer understanding, a decision, a plan, a question to ask someone, a boundary, a practical next step. Not every result is a document; sometimes an hour of honest thought leaves a person knowing what they will do, and that is a real product. Repeated worry, by contrast, returns to the same material without producing additional readiness. When Philip’s Saturday ended, he and his wife understood their position in a way they had not that morning. When his evenings ended, he understood nothing he had not understood the evening before.

Preparation has an end. A task is finished or is paused at a clear point. Living in tomorrow does not finish, because the future has no edge; there is always one more turn the scene can take. Philip did not stop because the preparing was complete. He stopped because he fell asleep.

Preparation works with what is established. It addresses what is known or probable and can be answered now. Living in tomorrow works mostly with what is possible, and treats the possible as though it were already scheduled. The restructuring was established. The loss of Philip’s role was not yet established. The smaller house and the school change were several steps beyond that, each resting on the one before, and none of them could be prepared for in any concrete way because none of them had a shape yet.

Preparation leaves you with a next step. Even when the situation is grave, the person who has prepared knows what they will do first if the thing happens. The person who has lived through it forty times often knows what they will feel, in great detail, and has no clearer idea what they will do.

Preparation may include emotion; it does not consist of it. Preparing for a hard future may rightly include emotional acknowledgement. A person facing surgery, bereavement, relocation or a difficult conversation does well to admit in advance that it will be hard, and to grieve some of it before it comes. The distinction is not between thinking and feeling. It is whether engaging the future produces proportionate readiness, or repeatedly immerses the person in an imagined experience without adding useful understanding or action. Philip’s evenings were not emotional preparation. They were the same scene of grief, shame and fear, run again, as though feeling it now would reduce the amount to be felt later. Rehearsing a painful outcome does not guarantee that the future pain will be reduced. It may instead cause a person to experience substantial distress before knowing whether the event will occur at all.

I want to be careful here. Philip could not simply choose to stop the images arriving. Nobody can, and if anyone tells you that a trusting Christian never finds tomorrow in their head at night, they are describing a person who does not exist. What Philip could do, and what this chapter offers, is learn to recognise when he had crossed from preparing into pre-living, and to have somewhere to put tomorrow when he noticed.

The chapter’s central claim can be put in a sentence. Preparing for tomorrow identifies what can responsibly be done now or scheduled for later. Pre-living tomorrow repeatedly enters an imagined outcome without producing further readiness. The answer is not to refuse all thought about the future, but to give future concerns the amount and kind of attention they presently require.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I really trusted God, I would not think about tomorrow at all.”

This belief has done a great deal of harm, and it usually harms the most responsible people in a congregation: the parent with the budget, the carer thinking about the next stage of a parent’s decline, the person whose job it is to plan. They read Matthew 6, hear “do not be anxious about tomorrow,” and conclude that their planning is unbelief. Then they feel ashamed of the very wisdom Scripture praises, and they add guilt to the concern they already carried.

Jesus is not forbidding thought about the future. He is forbidding a particular relationship to it: the taking up of tomorrow’s trouble as though it were already today’s, in a way that displaces trust in the Father and the seeking of his kingdom. The same Jesus who spoke these words also warned his disciples in advance about what was coming, so that they would not be caught unprepared.

There is a second version of this misunderstanding that runs the opposite way, and Philip had it. “If I do not think it through now, I will be caught out later.” Underneath his evenings was a belief that the rehearsal was protective, that living the redundancy in advance would make the real one hurt less or find him readier. It is worth testing that belief against experience. Did the fortieth rehearsal find him readier than the first? He was honest enough to say it had not. The preparation that helped had taken one afternoon. The rehearsal that followed had helped nothing and had taken his evenings and, increasingly, his presence with his family.

Trust in God for tomorrow does not mean refusing to think about it. It means doing what tomorrow’s wisdom asks of today, and then declining to carry the rest, because the rest belongs to a Father who already knows what you need.

### Testing the Tomorrow You Are Living In

Before deciding what to do with tomorrow, it helps to look at which tomorrow you have been living in, because it is often not the probable one. Lesson 6 offered four questions for a mind that predicts disaster, and they belong here too.

What am I predicting? Write it down as a plain sentence. Philip’s was not “the restructuring will happen.” It was, when he finally put it into words, “I will lose my job, we will lose the house, and I will have failed my family.” That is a chain, and each link deserves to be looked at on its own.

What evidence supports or challenges it? The restructuring was announced; that supported the first link. His manager had said his role was under review, not that it was going; that neither confirmed nor removed it. Nothing at all supported the house or the school; those rested entirely on the earlier links. Remember from Lesson 6 that a prediction is not yet established rather than false. Philip might lose his role. But “might” is a different country from “will,” and he had been living in the wrong one.

What is happening now? Now, Philip was employed, paid, and consulted. His family was in its house. His children were asleep upstairs, and he was in the room with them, or would have been if his mind had been.

What does the present require? Here is where preparing and pre-living separate. The present required that he attend the consultation meetings, ask his questions, keep the CV current, and keep the budget his wife and he had made. It did not require that he live in the smaller house tonight.

That last question leads directly to the practice this chapter offers.

### Today, Later or Release

When you notice that you have moved into tomorrow, everything you find yourself carrying there can be placed in one of three groups, with a fourth check that must always come first. This is a pastoral tool, not something the passage sets out, and it draws on the four faithful responses introduced in Lesson 2 and developed in Lesson 10: act, wait, seek help and surrender.

First, the check: is anything here urgent? Before sorting, ask whether the tomorrow you have been living in contains credible danger or immediate need: abuse, violence, stalking, imminent homelessness, a medical symptom that should not wait, a child at risk. If it does, that item does not go into any of the three groups below. It goes to the top of the page, and the response is to seek safety or qualified help now. The rest of this chapter is about worry that outruns the evidence. It is not about danger that the evidence supports, and a course must never be the reason someone treated a real threat as overthinking.

Today. Some of what you are carrying is a genuine responsibility that exists now. It can be acted on today, or the help it needs can be sought today. Philip’s CV belonged here once. His question to his manager belonged here. If there is something in the tomorrow you have been living in that today can actually do, this is the group for it, and the response is to act or to seek help, and then to stop, because it has been done.

Later. Some of what you are carrying will require action, but not yet. It is waiting for something: a date, a decision by someone else, a result, a piece of information that does not exist yet. The response is to wait, and waiting is easier when the thing has been written down with the point at which it becomes today’s business. If my role is confirmed as going, I will register with two agencies that week. Philip did not need to carry that every evening. He needed to write it once, with its trigger, and leave it on the page. The mind lets go of a thing more readily when it knows the thing is kept somewhere and will be returned to.

Release for now. Some of what you are carrying is not presently actionable. Either nothing useful can be done about it now, because it depends on something that has not happened and has no shape yet; or the matter lies beyond your role or control altogether, which is the surrender of Lesson 10 and the release of Lesson 12. “Release” here means release from present mental work. It is not a declaration that the matter will never require attention. The smaller house belonged under release for now. If job loss and sustained financial need later made relocation necessary, it could move into later, or into today. For now, no responsible action depended on imagining its rooms, and every evening spent in them was an evening taken from the house he was in.

A few cautions about using the groups honestly.

Do not use “later” to avoid what belongs to today. If a phone call is frightening and could be made now, it is today’s, and putting it on the later list with a comfortable trigger is avoidance wearing the clothes of wisdom. Lesson 8 spoke about the difference between a delay that is wise and a delay that is fear.

Do not use “release” to avoid “seek help.” If the future you are living in is a financial or legal or medical one and you lack the knowledge to sort it yourself, the right group is often “today,” and the action is to ask a qualified person. Surrender is not a substitute for advice. Let the groups be revised. New information moves things. When Philip’s consultation ended and his role was confirmed, several “later” items became “today,” and that was exactly what the list was for. The groups are a way of carrying tomorrow rightly, not a way of fixing it in place.

And do not measure the sorting by how you feel afterwards. Correct sorting may not make you calmer. The purpose is responsible action, not emotional certainty, and continued anxiety does not prove that an item was placed in the wrong group. Lesson 16 said the same about prayer: the body may stay activated after a faithful act of trust, and that is a reason for patience, not for redoing the list.

### Practical Application

Here is how I suggested Philip use this, and what he found.

Notice the crossing. The first skill is simply to recognise the moment when you have stopped preparing and started living in tomorrow. For Philip the sign was that he had begun to feel the meeting rather than plan for it. For some people vivid detail is a signal: when the imagined scene has acquired furniture and weather, planning may have become immersive rehearsal. But vivid imagery is not a diagnostic test. Some people naturally think in pictures, and images connected with past trauma need different care than this chapter gives. The better question is whether the detail is helping you prepare or merely intensifying distress. Lesson 13’s practice of noticing and naming applies here. I am in the smaller house again. It is a Tuesday evening in September and I am in my own kitchen. That is not a rebuke. It is a location.

Sort what you are carrying. Take what you found yourself carrying in tomorrow, ask the urgent question, and then place each item under today, later or release for now. Do it on paper the first several times. Philip’s first list had nothing urgent, two items under today, four under later with their triggers, and a long column under release for now, and he said that seeing the length of that column was the first time the evenings had made sense to him.

Do today’s item and keep the later list. If there is something under today, do it, or make the call that seeks help with it, and then let it be done. Keep the later list somewhere you can find it, and decide when you will look at it: after each consultation meeting, say, or once a week. The list is reviewed at a set time, not carried continuously. If you find yourself checking it many times a day to make sure it is complete, that is the compulsive pattern earlier chapters described, and it is a reason to review less often, not more, and to speak with someone if you cannot.

Give the rest back, in words. For what has been released, and for whatever remains of tomorrow that is not on any list, a short prayer of handing over, said deliberately, in words. Father, this is not mine to do today, and it is not mine to do on any date I can name. You know what we need. I am giving tomorrow back to you and returning to the day you have given me. The Six-Movement Prayer of Lesson 16 is the fuller form of this, and its sixth movement is exactly this act.

Return to the day. Then go back to what the present contains. This is the redirection of attention that Lesson 2 described, not the suppression of thought. Philip went back upstairs and read to his younger son, which was what the evening had actually contained all along. When tomorrow arrived again later, as it did, he did not treat that as failure. He named where he was, checked whether anything had changed, found that nothing had, and gave it back again. Some evenings that happened several times. It became briefer with practice. It did not become unnecessary, and I did not tell him it would.

If it arrives at night. If a future concern arrives in the dark, make a brief note only if doing so helps you disengage: a line or two on the pad by the bed, to be sorted in the morning. Do not begin a full worksheet in bed; detailed analysis at night keeps many people awake, and for some it becomes another ritual. If writing increases your alertness, or if you find yourself getting up to check that the note is complete, leave the exercise for daytime. The toolkit’s Night-Time Thought Download is designed for a short note, not a full sorting.

Philip’s role, in the end, was merged with another and he was offered the new one at a slightly lower grade. It was not the outcome he had feared, and it was not the one he had hoped for. He told me afterwards that the thing he regretted was not the concern, which had been reasonable, but the thirty evenings spent in a house he never had to move to, while the children he was so afraid of failing were awake and asking for him in the actual one.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that are true for you, adapt them or remain silent.*

Father, you know what I need. Jesus said so, and I believe it, and yet I have spent evenings and nights in days that have not come, carrying trouble you have not yet asked me to carry, and calling it responsibility.

Thank you for the wisdom that plans, that counts the cost, that stores in summer. Help me to do what today’s wisdom actually asks, and to do it well and then to stop. For one tomorrow at a time, once, during the day. ## Closing declaration

Wisdom allows me to prepare for tomorrow without demanding control of it. I will act on what responsibly belongs to today, schedule what depends on a later date or new information, seek urgent help where danger is real, and release what is not presently actionable. When an imagined future returns, I will not treat its arrival as evidence. My Father knows what I need, and I can return to the day and the responsibilities actually before me.

## If you need more support

If tomorrow contains a real and present hardship — money that will not stretch, housing that is not secure, a situation in which you are not safe — the today column may be long and its actions may need other people: a debt adviser, a housing service, a doctor, a solicitor, a domestic-abuse service. Seeking that help is preparation of the wisest kind. Worry about many things that is persistent, hard to control and affecting sleep or functioning is treatable and deserves a doctor's assessment.
