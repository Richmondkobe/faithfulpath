# Lesson 15: Think About What Is True

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to distinguish factual accuracy from worthiness for sustained attention, and use a pastoral test to decide what deserves a continuing place in your mind.

## Before you begin

This lesson is about what you deliberately dwell on, not about thoughts that arrive uninvited; Lesson 13 covers those. Three cautions. If scrolling or monitoring feels difficult to control, causes significant distress, repeatedly disrupts sleep or interferes with daily functioning, consider speaking with a doctor or qualified mental-health professional; it may be connected to anxiety, compulsive reassurance-seeking, trauma or another difficulty that deserves individual assessment. If what your mind keeps returning to is a real atrocity, injustice or catastrophe, whether in the news or one you lived through, and it comes with vivid images, nightmares or a sense of being unable to look away, that deserves assessment by a professional experienced in trauma rather than treatment as an attention habit. If you can only see what is wrong, good things seem unreal, and this has lasted some weeks, do not treat it as a failure to think on what is lovely; persistent low mood of that kind deserves assessment by a doctor. And if hopelessness includes thoughts of suicide or self-harm, use Finding Help Where You Live and tell someone safe today; if you may act, cannot keep yourself safe or are in immediate danger, contact emergency or urgent crisis services now and do not remain alone. Complete the worksheet during the day, once for one thought or source. Do not use the Pastoral Attention Test in the middle of the night: if a thought or source is keeping you awake, use only the Night-Time Thought Download, if writing will not increase your alertness, and return to this lesson in daylight.

## Key Scripture: Philippians 4:4–9 (ESV)

> Finally, brothers, whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things.

Paul writes from prison, uncertain whether he will be released or executed. Verse 8 follows his promise that God's peace will guard their hearts; the attention practice is not presented as a technique for earning that peace.

## One truth to carry with you

Philippians 4:8 is not a recommendation to think about pleasant things. Paul's list begins with *true*, and it includes *just* and *honourable*, which are not always comfortable. Paul himself, in the same letter, thinks openly about his chains, about people who preach Christ from envy, and about the possibility of his own death. Thinking about what is true does not mean averting your eyes from what is hard; it means refusing to dwell on what is false, distorted or unworthy, whether or not it is pleasant. And the verb means considering, reckoning, giving sustained attention: not the involuntary arrival of a thought, but what you deliberately give your mind to. That is a choice, made many times a day, usually without noticing. You can face hard truth without feeding on speculation, distortion or alarm.

## The lesson

A man I will call Andrew was not, by his own account, an anxious person until his mid-fifties. What changed was not his circumstances but his evenings. During a period of economic uncertainty he began following the news closely, which was reasonable; his pension was involved. But following the news became reading commentary, and commentary became forums where people predicted collapse, and by the time we spoke he was spending two or three hours each evening absorbing other people's alarm. He could not tell me what had actually happened to interest rates in the past month, because the sources he read were almost entirely about what might happen next. He had stopped sleeping. Because his sleep difficulty had become persistent, I encouraged him to discuss it with a healthcare professional rather than assume that changing his reading would fix it. But we talked about the reading too. "Everything I read is probably true," he said. "I am not reading lies. So why does it feel like poison?"

Verse 8 is often quoted alone, and alone it can sound like a recommendation to think about pleasant things. Notice where it sits. Paul is in prison, uncertain whether he will be released or executed. He has just told the church not to be anxious but to pray, and promised that God's peace will guard their hearts and minds. Paul places the promise of guarding peace before the instruction about sustained attention; the attention practice is not presented as a technique for earning that peace. Two things follow. The list is not a list of pleasant things; truth is indispensable in it, and *just* and *honourable* are not always comfortable. And the verb translated "think about" carries the sense of considering, reckoning, giving sustained attention. Paul is not describing the involuntary arrival of thoughts. He is describing what the mind is deliberately given to dwell on.

Andrew's question deserves an answer. He was not, mostly, reading lies. Why did it feel like poison? Because factual accuracy is necessary but is not the only question governing sustained attention. Material can be **accurate but incomplete**: a mind fed only on possibilities of collapse is given a picture from which the ordinary, the stable and the good have been removed, and the result is misleading as a whole though accurate in each part. It can be **accurate but unresolved**, since a prediction is not yet an established fact. It can be **accurate but outside any faithful response**, producing the feeling of responsibility without the possibility of action, which is one of the most exhausting states a mind can be in. It can be **accurate but structured around alarm**, arranged around urgency rather than proportion. And it can be **accurate and carry an unsupported claim about God**: the circumstances are true, and the conclusion attached to them — that God has withdrawn, that he does not see — may not be. Andrew's evenings failed on nearly every count, and underneath them ran a claim about God he had never examined: that the future was in the hands of markets and ministers, and God was, at best, a spectator.

"Philippians 4:8 means I should avoid anything negative. Christians should only think about nice things." This reading produces two kinds of harm. It makes people feel guilty for thinking about hard realities that Scripture dwells on at length: the prophets on injustice, the psalmists on suffering, Paul on his own possible execution in this very letter. If verse 8 forbade thinking about hard things, Paul broke it three chapters earlier. And it lets a great deal of falsehood through, provided it is pleasant. The right reading is neither "only nice things" nor "anything at all". It is: give your sustained attention to what is true, whole, honourable and worthy, and withhold it from what is false, distorted, alarmist or degrading, whether it comes from a screen, a conversation or your own imagination.

The practice is a test with six questions, and I should be clear about its status: they are not a word-for-word exposition of Paul's eight terms but a pastoral tool inspired by the passage. **Established.** Is this presented as a verified fact, an interpretation, a prediction or an opinion, and what reliable evidence supports it? A prediction should not be treated as an established event, and an interpretation should not be treated as the only possible reading. **Proportionate.** Does it represent the wider picture fairly, or is it a fragment arranged to produce a feeling? **Worthy.** Is the content honourable, just, pure and commendable, or contemptuous or built to provoke? **Relevant.** Does sustained attention serve a responsibility, a person or a faithful action? Some things need to be known once; dwelling is a further step. **Theologically honest.** Have I attached an unsupported claim about God to the facts? Test it in context, once. **Bounded.** How much attention does this genuinely deserve: a minute, a morning, an evening every day?

The mind is fed from two directions, so the practice has two halves. **What you take in.** For a week, notice what you give sustained attention to, without moralising. Then run the test on the two or three sources that occupy you most. Andrew found his evening reading failed on established, proportionate and bounded. He did not stop reading the news; he stopped reading forecasts of it, limited himself to a reliable summary in the morning, and gave his evenings to something else. Reliability depends on the subject: a pension decision may need a regulated financial professional rather than more commentary. **What you generate.** The mind also feeds itself, through the predictions, replays, rehearsals and mind-readings of Module 2. When you notice yourself dwelling on one, run the test. **What you dwell on instead.** Paul does not stop at telling us what not to dwell on; a mind emptied of alarm will fill with something, and it is better to choose: Scripture, the actual people in front of you, the work that is yours, the good genuinely present in your day, the character of God. It will feel at first like a poorer diet than alarm, because alarm is stimulating. It is not poorer. And this is not a call to ignorance: a Christian cannot weep with those who weep without knowing that they are weeping. The question is how much sustained attention to give, and whether it serves anything. Andrew knew more about the economy than he needed and less about his wife's week than he should.

Truth is indispensable in Paul’s list: whatever else deserves sustained Christian attention, it cannot be built on falsehood. Second, the verb translated “think about” carries the sense of considering, reckoning, giving sustained attention. Paul is not describing the involuntary arrival of thoughts, which we discussed in Lesson 13. He is describing what the mind is deliberately given to dwell on. Some scholars observe that Paul’s list resembles the virtue lists of the surrounding culture, and that he may be commending whatever is genuinely excellent wherever it is found. What is clear in any reading is that Paul expects believers to be selective about what they dwell on, and that both truth and moral worth govern the selection.

### Paul’s Wider Test

Before offering a practical tool, it is worth pausing on Paul’s own words, because they are richer than any tool built from them.

True: consistent with reality rather than falsehood or distortion.

Honourable: worthy of respect and moral seriousness.

Just: consistent with what is right.

Pure: morally clean and not corrupting.

Lovely: conducive to love rather than contempt or degradation.

Commendable: worthy of a good report or responsible approval.

Excellent: possessing genuine moral quality.

Praiseworthy: deserving thoughtful recognition and gratitude.

These descriptions overlap, and each term has interpretive depth that cannot be reduced to one English synonym. Together, they show that Christian attention is guided not only by factual accuracy but also by moral worth. A thing can be accurate and degrading; it can be accurate and contemptuous; it can be accurate and unworthy of an hour of anyone’s mind.

As a pastoral application, then: Philippians 4:8 does not command Christians to avoid everything painful. It calls the church to sustained attention toward what is true and morally worthy. Hard realities may meet that standard; pleasant falsehoods do not. The question is not merely “Does this make me feel good?” or even “Is one part of it accurate?” It is “Does this deserve the place I am giving it in my mind and life?”

### Accurate, but Not the Whole Picture

Andrew’s question deserves an answer. He was not, mostly, reading lies. Why did it feel like poison?

Because factual accuracy is necessary, but it is not the only question governing sustained attention. Information can be accurate in isolation yet selected so narrowly that it creates a distorted overall impression. It can also be accurate but irrelevant to your responsibilities, or unworthy of hours of repeated attention. Consider the ways accurate material can still fail.

It can be accurate but incomplete. Every forecast Andrew read was, in some sense, possible. But a mind fed only on possibilities of collapse is being given a picture from which the ordinary, the stable and the good have been removed. The result is a view of the world that is misleading as a whole though accurate in each part. We saw this in Lesson 9 with people: one observation, many interpretations. It is equally so with the world.

It can be accurate but unresolved. Much of what Andrew read was prediction. A prediction is not yet an established fact. It should be held according to the available evidence rather than treated as either certainly true or certainly false, which is what Lesson 6 taught. A mind that treats every forecast as a fact is carrying a great many things that have not happened.

It can be accurate but outside any faithful response. Some true information may call for prayer, giving, advocacy, preparation or another proportionate response. Other information lies outside your responsibility and offers no meaningful action. The question is not simply whether you can control it, but whether sustained attention serves faithful love or responsibility. Dwelling on what offers no such response produces the feeling of responsibility without any possibility of action, which is one of the most exhausting states a mind can be in.

It can be accurate but structured around alarm. Does the source provide proportionate information, or is its presentation organised primarily around urgency, outrage or alarm? You do not need to determine the creator’s motive to evaluate the effect and quality of the content. Is this telling me what is, in proportion, or is it arranged to make me afraid?

It can be accurate and carry an unsupported claim about God. This is the deepest test. A thought can be accurate about circumstances and still attach a theological conclusion to them that the facts do not support: that God has withdrawn, that he does not see, that the world has slipped out of his hand. Circumstances are true. The claim about God attached to them may not be.

Andrew’s evenings failed on nearly every count. The fragments were accurate. The picture was distorted. The presentation was built around urgency. Almost none of it offered him a faithful response beyond dread. And underneath it ran a claim about God that he had never examined: that the future was in the hands of markets and ministers, and that God was, at best, a spectator.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“Philippians 4:8 means I should avoid anything negative. Christians should only think about nice things.”

This reading is common, and it produces two kinds of harm.

It makes people feel guilty for thinking about hard realities that Scripture itself thinks about at length. The prophets dwelt on injustice. The psalmists dwelt on suffering. Jesus told his disciples about persecution in detail. Paul, as we have seen, thought about his own possible execution in the very letter that contains this verse. If verse 8 forbade thinking about hard things, Paul broke it three chapters earlier.

And it lets a great deal of falsehood through, provided it is pleasant. A comforting sentence that is not true fails Paul’s test at the word true. Lesson 14 was about exactly this: reassurance that feels good and cannot bear weight. Paul does not say “whatever is pleasant.” He says “whatever is true,” and the rest of the list describes what kind of truth deserves sustained attention.

The right reading is neither “only nice things” nor “anything at all.” It is: give your sustained attention to what is true, whole, honourable and worthy, and withhold it from what is false, distorted, alarmist or degrading, whether that comes from a screen, a conversation or your own imagination. That will include hard things. It will exclude a great many comfortable ones.

### A Pastoral Attention Test Based on Philippians 4:8

Here is the practice this chapter offers. It is a test for what you choose to dwell on: not for every thought that arrives, which Lesson 13 covered, but for the thoughts, sources and subjects you are deliberately giving your attention to.

I should be clear about its status. The six questions below are not a word-for-word exposition of Paul’s eight terms. They combine his emphasis on truth and moral worth with the distinctions developed in earlier chapters. They are a pastoral tool inspired by the passage, not an explanation of every word in it.

Established. Is this a fact, an interpretation, a prediction or an opinion? Only the first is established. The others may be worth knowing, but they should be held as what they are.

Proportionate. Does it represent the wider picture fairly, or is it a fragment arranged to produce a feeling? What has been left out?

Worthy. Is the content honourable, just, pure and commendable, in Paul’s sense? Or is it contemptuous, degrading, or built to provoke?

Relevant. Does sustained attention serve a responsibility, a person, or a faithful action: a decision, a duty, a prayer, a gift, a piece of preparation? Some things need to be known once. Dwelling is a further step, and it should earn its place.

Theologically honest. Have I attached an unsupported claim about God to the facts—“this loss proves God has abandoned me”? Test that claim in context, once. Do not repeatedly analyse every difficult fact for a hidden statement about God; that becomes its own kind of scrupulosity.

Bounded. How much attention does this genuinely deserve? A minute? A morning? An evening every day? Even what passes the first five questions has a proportionate share.

A thought or a source that passes these deserves your attention, even if it is hard. One that fails at established or worthy should not be dwelt on however comforting or stimulating it is. One that is established but fails at relevant or bounded can be known and set down.

### Practical Application

The practice has two halves, because the mind is fed from two directions: from what you take in, and from what you generate.

What you take in. For a week, notice what you are giving your sustained attention to: the news you read, the feeds you scroll, the conversations you return to, the programmes you watch before bed. Do not moralise about any of it yet. Simply notice it. Then run the test on the two or three sources that occupy you most. Andrew found that his evening reading failed on established, proportionate and bounded, and, once he looked, on theologically honest. He did not stop reading the news. He stopped reading forecasts of the news, limited himself to a reliable summary in the morning, and gave his evenings to something else.

A word about what counts as reliable. Reliability depends on the subject. A pension decision may require a regulated financial professional rather than more commentary; a health concern may require a clinician rather than an online forum. Reducing sources should not mean replacing necessary expertise with a convenient summary. The Personal Digital Boundary worksheet in the Course Journal and Practical Toolkit is designed for this, and Lesson 20 will place it within a wider rule of life.

What you generate. The mind also feeds itself, and the earlier chapters of this course have shown you how: predictions, replays, rehearsals, mind-readings. When you notice that you are dwelling on one of these, run the test. Many are unverified, incomplete or mixed with interpretation. Replays may refer to something that genuinely happened, while predictions and mind-reading make claims that have not been established. Those that survive the first question usually fail at relevant or bounded. The test is not a way of suppressing them. It is a way of declining to give them the sustained attention Paul reserves for what is worthy.

What you dwell on instead. Paul does not stop at telling us not to dwell on what is false. He tells the Philippians what to think about, and he tells them to practise what they have learned. A mind that has been emptied of alarm will fill with something, and it is better to choose. This is not positive thinking, as Lesson 14 was at pains to say. It is the deliberate giving of attention to what is true and worthy: Scripture, the actual people in front of you, the work that is yours, the good that is genuinely present in your day, and the character of God. It will feel, at first, like a poorer diet than the alarm, because alarm is stimulating. It is not poorer. It is what the mind was made for.

A word about proportion. This is not a call to ignorance. A citizen should know what is happening; a person with a pension should understand it; a Christian should weep with those who weep, and cannot do so without knowing that they are weeping. The question is not whether to know but how much sustained attention to give, and to what, and whether the attention serves anything. Andrew knew more about the economy than he needed and less about his wife’s week than he should. The test helped him rebalance.

I will not give sustained attention merely to what is loud, frightening or pleasant. I will seek what is established, proportionate and morally worthy.
