# Lesson 16: Prayer That Does Not Become More Worry

**What this lesson will do for you.** By the end of this lesson you will be able to notice when prayer has become rehearsal, and use a six-movement shape that lets a prayer come to rest without limiting persistent prayer.

## Before you begin

Repeating a request is not evidence of unbelief; Scripture commends persistent prayer, and this lesson sets no limit on how often you pray. Two cautions. If you repeat particular prayers or phrases until they feel right, pray a request in order to neutralise a fear, or cannot stop praying about something because stopping feels dangerous, this can occur with scrupulosity, in which prayer has become a compulsion; consider a professional who understands both faith and obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and do not use this lesson's practice to add another ritual. If you cannot pray at all, and the words will not come and God seems absent, please do not treat it as spiritual failure; difficulty praying can accompany depression, grief, exhaustion or spiritual distress, and persistent low mood deserves assessment by a doctor while a trustworthy pastor may support the spiritual struggle. A short spoken prayer at night is fine. Do not begin the written Six-Movement Prayer in bed: if you need to record a concern, use only the Night-Time Thought Download and return to this practice in daylight.

## Key Scripture: Psalm 62:8 (ESV)

> Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us.

The psalm opens, and repeats at verse 5, "For God alone my soul waits in silence." Alongside the pouring out there is waiting, and the pouring out is received: the heart is poured out before someone.

## One truth to carry with you

Prayer is not a mechanism for producing calm, and God's hearing is not proved by your feeling better. The psalms contain prayers that end in praise and prayers that end in darkness. Jesus prayed in Gethsemane with such anguish that Luke describes his sweat as drops of blood, and the cup did not pass. Paul asked three times for his thorn to be removed and received grace to bear it instead. None of those prayers failed. So if you rise from your knees still frightened, that is not evidence that you prayed badly or that God did not hear. Anxiety is partly a bodily state, and a sincere act of trust does not necessarily change the body's stress response at once. You may pour out your heart without hiding fear or manufacturing certainty, and you may bring the same need again tomorrow. Persistent prayer is not unbelief.

## The lesson

A woman I will call Elizabeth prayed for her daughter every night, and every night she finished more frightened than she began. Her daughter, in her first year at university, had been struggling with the course, with loneliness, and with a relationship that had ended badly. Elizabeth did what she had always done and took it to God. But her prayers went like this: she would tell God what was wrong, and in the telling remember another thing that was wrong, and imagine what might happen if that got worse, and ask him to prevent it, and wonder whether he would, and whether she had prayed enough; and by the end of forty minutes she had walked through every possible disaster and risen with a heart racing faster than when she knelt. "I think I am praying," she said. "But it feels like worrying with my eyes closed." Her prayer contained genuine love and intercession. What it had lost was the shape that lets a prayer come to rest.

Read the whole of Psalm 62, and notice how it begins: "For God alone my soul waits in silence." Alongside the pouring out there is waiting, and the waiting is silent. Then verse 8 gives the instruction, to everyone: trust in him, pour out your heart, he is a refuge. The psalm does not tell us to present God with a tidy summary. It says pour: everything, unedited. And the pouring is held together with trust and with refuge; the heart is poured out before someone, and that someone receives it. The psalm does not present a six-step method; what follows is a pastoral practice developed from its themes, and I want that clear before offering it.

Prayer sometimes leaves a person more anxious. That can happen because naming pain brings it closer, because the body remains activated, or because the prayer has become repetitive rehearsal, and feeling worse afterwards does not by itself tell us whether the prayer was faithful. But some patterns are worth recognising. It **addresses God but talks to itself**: the words go upward while the mind rehearses and predicts, and adding "Lord" to the front of each sentence does not change what the sentence is doing. It **circles without lament or trust**: many psalms move from lament towards renewed trust, and not all resolve, but what the psalms do not do is rehearse imagined futures in ever-greater detail. It **seeks a guarantee**: *promise me she will be all right*. God gives many firm promises, but not every desired temporal outcome, and a prayer asking for what is not on offer cannot rest. It **cannot come to rest**. And it **can replace action or help**: sometimes what a situation needs is a phone call, a conversation, a doctor.

Because this lesson is about prayer that circles, I need to say something carefully or it will be misread. Repeating a request is not evidence of unbelief. Jesus told a parable so that his disciples "ought always to pray and not lose heart", and in Gethsemane he prayed the same request repeatedly; Paul pleaded three times about his thorn. The question is what the repetition is doing. **Persistent prayer** continues asking without losing heart, open to God's will. **Lament** returns honestly to unresolved pain. **Intercession** faithfully carries an ongoing need day after day; Elizabeth's nightly prayer was intercession, and it was right. **Compulsive repetition** repeats in order to neutralise fear or make the prayer feel complete, driven by the sense that stopping is dangerous. The first three are healthy expressions of faith; the fourth may be reassurance-seeking or scrupulosity and may need professional help. The same words can belong to either; what distinguishes them is what they are for.

"If I still feel anxious after praying, I did not pray properly, or God did not hear me." This turns prayer into a performance measured by a feeling. Scripture does not measure it that way. What prayer does is bring us, and what troubles us, into the presence of God, where the trouble can be seen for what it is and where we are reminded of who he is. Sometimes that produces peace quickly; sometimes the peace comes later, or as steadiness rather than calm. The prayer was still prayer.

So here is the shape, drawn from the themes Psalm 62 holds together. It is not a formula and not a rule about how often to pray; it is somewhere for a prayer to go when it keeps turning into rehearsal. **One: become present.** Be still for a moment before you speak; you are coming before a person who already knows what you will say. **Two: name the reality.** State what is actually known, in a sentence or two — not the chain of consequences, the event. **Three: pour out your heart.** Say honestly what you feel: fear, grief, anger, desire. Do not tidy it; God has read the psalms too. **Four: make your request.** Ask specifically, and remain open to God's will. You may ask again tomorrow; that is intercession. What you need not do is ask again within the same prayer in order to feel that the asking has taken. **Five: remember and discern.** This movement does not require an inner impression, special message or sense of certainty. It means returning your attention to what Scripture clearly says and identifying your present responsibility. Then name what is yours to do today, using the four ordinary responses the course has been building: act, wait, seek help, surrender. Where danger is present, the prior response is to move to safety or seek urgent help, and Lesson 18 adds that fifth response formally. **Six: entrust the outcome.** Place what you cannot control into God's care, deliberately and in words, and then rise and do the thing from movement five. The refuge is real whether or not you feel it yet, and you may return tomorrow with the same need; entrusting is not a door that closes behind you.

The whole of this can take five minutes, or much longer if the pouring out is heavy, and it should. Length does not determine faithfulness. Begin with the matter that has been turning your prayers into worry, and notice where you tend to stay: many who pray anxiously spend most of the prayer pouring out and asking, and rarely reach remembering and entrusting. The answer is not to pour out less but to let the prayer continue. What you are watching for is not repetition but the sense that you must repeat, that stopping is dangerous, that the prayer has not worked until a feeling arrives. And use the psalms: if you cannot find words, they have found them for you.

Her prayer contained genuine love, intercession and honest concern. But it had also become entangled with catastrophic rehearsal and repeated attempts to obtain certainty. Elizabeth was not doing anything wrong by bringing her daughter to God, and she was not wrong to bring her again and again. What she had lost, somewhere in the forty minutes, was the shape that lets a prayer come to rest: the waiting, the remembering, and the entrusting that Psalm 62 holds together with the pouring out.

### Central Scripture: Psalm 62:8

Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us. (Psalm 62:8, ESV) Read the whole psalm before continuing. Its superscription associates it with David, and its setting is one of attack: people are trying to bring the psalmist down, they bless with their mouths and curse inwardly, and he is under real pressure.

Notice how the psalm begins. “For God alone my soul waits in silence.” That line opens the psalm and is repeated almost exactly at verse 5. Alongside the pouring out, there is waiting, and the waiting is silent. The psalmist has placed himself before God and grown still, and out of that stillness he describes his situation with unusual clarity: God is his rock and salvation; his enemies are what they are; and human beings, high and low, weigh less than a breath.

Then, in verse 8, comes the instruction, and it is given to everyone: trust in him, pour out your heart, he is a refuge. The pouring out is real. The psalm does not tell us to keep our fears to ourselves or to present God with a tidy summary. It says pour: everything, unedited. And the pouring is held together with trust and with refuge. The heart is poured out before someone, and that someone receives it.

Psalm 62 holds together waiting, trust, honest outpouring and refuge. The psalm does not present a six-step prayer method, and its literary order does not prescribe a sequence for every prayer. The structure offered later in this chapter is a pastoral practice developed from those themes, and I want to be clear about that before offering it.

### When Prayer Feels Like More Worry

Prayer sometimes leaves a person feeling more anxious. That can happen because naming pain brings it closer to awareness, because the body remains activated, or because the prayer has become repetitive mental rehearsal. Feeling worse afterwards does not, by itself, tell us whether the prayer was faithful. But we can gently examine what happened, without condemning the person or the prayer, and there are some patterns worth recognising.

It addresses God but talks to itself. The words are directed upward, but the mind is doing what it does when it worries: rehearsing, predicting, cataloguing. Adding “Lord” to the front of each sentence does not change what the sentence is doing. Lesson 6’s chain of consequences, spoken aloud with a bowed head, is still Lesson 6’s chain.

It circles without lament or trust. Many psalms move from lament toward renewed trust, but not all resolve emotionally. Psalm 88 ends in darkness. Scripture therefore gives us permission both to move and to remain before God when resolution has not come. What the psalms do not do is rehearse imagined futures in ever- greater detail. Anxious prayer often does exactly that, and mistakes the rehearsal for lament.

It seeks a guarantee. Anxious prayer can begin seeking certainty about a particular outcome: promise me she will be all right. God gives many firm promises, but he does not promise every desired temporal outcome. Persistent prayer for the outcome remains appropriate; demanding a certainty he has not given is different, and it cannot rest, because what it is asking for is not on offer.

It cannot come to rest. The psalmist pours out and takes refuge. Anxious prayer pours out and keeps holding. It is important to say clearly, though, that the continued feeling of anxiety does not prove that surrender was insincere. Release is a posture of entrusting the outcome to God, not a guarantee that concern, grief or bodily activation will disappear.

It can replace action or help. Sometimes what a situation needs is a phone call, a conversation, a doctor, a practical step. Prayer that goes on at length about a matter that needs one decision can become a way of not deciding. Lesson 12 made this point about carrying people; it applies to praying for them too.

None of this is a reason to pray less, or to pray fewer times. It is a reason to notice what the prayer is doing, and to restore what Psalm 62 holds together.

### Four Kinds of Repetition

Because this chapter is about prayer that circles, I need to say something carefully, or it will be misread.

Repeating a request is not evidence of unbelief. Scripture commends persistent prayer. Jesus told a parable so that his disciples “ought always to pray and not lose heart,” and taught persistence again in the story of the friend at midnight. In Gethsemane he prayed the same request repeatedly. Paul pleaded with the Lord three times about his thorn. The question is never simply whether you have prayed about something before. It is what the repetition is doing.

Persistent prayer continues asking without losing heart. It brings a real need to God again because it trusts him, and it remains open to his will.

Lament returns honestly to unresolved pain. It says, again, that the thing still hurts and that God has not yet acted, and it says it to him.

Intercession faithfully carries an ongoing need before God, day after day, for as long as the need lasts. Elizabeth’s nightly prayer for her daughter was intercession, and it was right.

Compulsive repetition repeats in order to neutralise fear, achieve a feeling of certainty, or make the prayer feel complete. It is driven by the sense that stopping is dangerous or that the prayer has not yet “worked.”

The first three can be healthy expressions of faith. The fourth may be reassurance-seeking or scrupulosity and may require professional help. Faithful persistence continues bringing a real need to God while remaining open to his will. Compulsive repetition seeks to neutralise anxiety, achieve a feeling of certainty or make the prayer feel complete. The same words can belong to either; what distinguishes them is what they are for.

### A Common Misunderstanding

“If I still feel anxious after praying, I did not pray properly, or God did not hear me.”

This belief turns prayer into a performance whose success is measured by a feeling, and it causes a great deal of unnecessary distress.

Scripture does not measure prayer that way. The psalms contain prayers that end in praise and prayers that end in darkness. Jesus prayed in Gethsemane with such anguish that Luke describes his sweat as drops of blood, and the cup did not pass. Paul asked three times for his thorn to be removed and received, instead, grace to bear it. None of these prayers failed. None of them was improperly offered. What they show is that prayer is not a mechanism for producing calm, and that God’s hearing is not proved by our feeling better.

What prayer does is bring us, and what troubles us, into the presence of God, where the trouble can be seen for what it is and where we can be reminded of who he is. Sometimes that produces peace quickly. Sometimes it does not, and the peace comes later, or comes as steadiness rather than calm, or does not come in any form we recognise until much later still. The prayer was still prayer. God still heard.

There is also a gentler truth here. Anxiety is, as Lesson 5 said, partly a bodily state. A sincere act of trust does not necessarily produce an immediate change in the body’s stress response. Continued physical anxiety is not proof that the prayer failed. It is a reason to be patient with yourself, and to attend to the body as well.

### The Six-Movement Prayer

Here is the practice this chapter offers. It draws on the themes Psalm 62 holds together: presence, honest reality, pouring out, request, remembering, entrusting. I have arranged them as six movements so that a prayer that tends to circle has somewhere to go. It is not a formula, it is not the only way to pray, and it is not a rule about how often to pray. It is a shape for the times when prayer keeps turning into rehearsal.

One. Become present. Notice where you are and remember to whom you are praying. This is the silence at the start of the psalm. Be still for a moment before you speak. You are not beginning a task; you are coming before a person who already knows what you will say.

Two. Name the reality. State what is actually known, in a sentence or two. Not the chain of consequences; the event. My daughter is struggling at university and has told me she is lonely. This is the discipline of Lesson 6 brought into prayer. It keeps the prayer anchored to what is, rather than to what might be.

Three. Pour out your heart. Say, honestly, what you feel: fear, grief, anger, desire. I am frightened for her. I feel helpless because she is far away. I am angry with the young man who hurt her. Do not tidy it. God has read the psalms too, and he is not surprised by any of it. Naming the feeling is what stops it from driving the rest of the prayer unnamed. This is the pouring out, and it belongs to genuine prayer; it is not something to be got through.

Four. Make your request. Ask specifically, and remain open to God’s will. Please give her a friend. Please help her with the course. Please keep her safe. Please show me what I should do. You may ask again tomorrow, and the day after; that is intercession. What you need not do is ask again within the same prayer in order to feel that the asking has taken. If you notice that happening, it is a signal to move on to what follows, not because the request was wrong but because the repetition has stopped being prayer and started being reassurance.

Five. Remember and discern. Recall what Scripture truly says, and identify today’s responsibility. Bring to mind what is true about God: that he is a refuge, that he loves your daughter more than you do, that he is not far from her because she is far from you. If a passage comes to mind, read it slowly. This is not listening for a voice, and you should not expect one; it is turning your attention from the need alone toward the God to whom the need is brought. It may bring peace, but it is not a technique guaranteed to change your emotional state. Then ask what is actually yours to do, today, and name it, using the four faithful responses introduced in Lesson 2 and developed in Lesson 10. Act: I will call her this evening and listen. Wait: I will not drive up there this week; she has asked for space. Seek help: I will ask her whether she has spoken to the university’s counselling service. Surrender: the relationship that ended is not mine to fix.

Six. Entrust the outcome. End by placing what you cannot control into God’s care, deliberately and in words. Father, I have told you what is happening and how I feel. I have asked. I have remembered who you are. I know what is mine to do today. The rest is yours. Then rise and do the thing from movement five. The refuge is real whether or not you feel it yet, and you may return tomorrow, or in an hour, with the same need; entrusting is not a door that closes behind you.

The whole of this can take five minutes. It can take much longer if the pouring out is heavy, and it should. Length does not determine faithfulness. What the shape provides is somewhere for the prayer to go, so that it is relational, honest and open to action rather than dominated by rehearsal.

### Practical Application

Begin with the matter that has been turning your prayers into worry. Pray the six movements for it, in writing if that helps; many people find that writing a prayer keeps it from circling.

Then notice where you tend to stay. Many people who pray anxiously spend most of the prayer in movements three and four, pouring out and asking, and rarely reach five and six. If that is you, the answer is not to pour out less, but to let the prayer continue into remembering and entrusting. When you notice a request being repeated within the same prayer to make it feel complete, take that as the signal to move to the next movement.

When the fear returns later in the day, you may bring it to God again; that is persistent prayer, and it is commended. You may find that a brief return to movement six is enough: I have entrusted this to you, and I am doing what is mine. Or you may need to lament again. What you are watching for is not repetition, but the sense that you must repeat, that stopping is dangerous, that the prayer has not worked until a feeling arrives. That is the pattern the box above describes, and it deserves help rather than more effort.

Use the psalms. If you cannot find words, they have found them for you, and they model both the movement from complaint to trust and the honest remaining in darkness when trust has not yet come. Psalm 62 itself, read slowly, contains every movement in this chapter.

And if long prayer repeatedly becomes mental circling, experiment with a shorter, structured prayer. This is not because short prayers are better; long prayer is not inferior, and some nights the pouring out needs an hour. It is because a structure can help prayer remain relational, honest and open to action rather than dominated by compulsive repetition. Elizabeth’s longer prayers contained genuine intercession, but much of their length was occupied by repeated catastrophic rehearsal. Recognising that mixture helped her pray more freely. It did not prove that the prayers were invalid.

## Guided prayer

*You may use the words that are true for you, adapt them or remain silent.* *If analysing your prayer would become another form of checking, do not use the confession below; pray only the opening and closing lines.*

Father, you have told me to pour out my heart before you, and I have. I confess that I have sometimes poured it out and then rehearsed every disaster in your presence, and called the rehearsal prayer. I have asked you for the same thing until I felt safe, rather than until I trusted you, and I have measured your hearing by my feelings.

Teach me to be still before I speak, to say what is actually happening and what I actually feel, to ask and keep asking as a child asks a father, and then to remember who you are. Show me each day what is mine to do, and give me the courage to entrust the rest to you, again and again, as often as I need to.

You are a refuge. Let me actually take refuge, and not merely describe my fears at the door.

In Jesus’ name. Amen.
