Welcome to Lesson 2. Last time we learnt to notice a circling thought. Today we go one step further. Is the thinking you did today careful thinking, or has it quietly stopped being useful? From the inside, both feel like being responsible. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: five questions that tell you when thinking has done its work, and four things you can do once it has. Five questions, four responses. That is the whole tool, and you will use it for the rest of this course.

Our verse is Ecclesiastes chapter 3, verse 1, in the English Standard Version. "For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven." The Teacher then lists fourteen pairs: a time to plant and a time to pull up, a time to keep silence and a time to speak. His point is that wisdom means knowing the right time. The passage does not mention thinking, but the principle applies with real force. There is a time to think, and a time to stop thinking.

Let me tell you about a man I will call Daniel. He could not decide whether to take a job in another city. The offer had come six weeks earlier, and the deadline had been extended twice. He had prayed, made lists, asked his wife, his pastor, his father and two friends, and read every verse on guidance he could find. So I asked him: what have you learnt in the last two weeks that you did not know before? A long pause. "Nothing, really." "Then what have you been doing for two weeks?" "Thinking about it." Daniel believed he was being careful, and at first he was. But somewhere around week four, careful thought quietly became something else, and he never saw the moment it changed. This lesson is about seeing that moment.

Part of Daniel's problem was one word: thinking. He called everything "thinking", so he could not tell the healthy from the unhealthy. Here are seven kinds. Four go somewhere. Reflection looks back to understand. Planning looks forward and gives you a step. Discernment weighs a choice in the light of God's wisdom; unhurried, but not endless. Problem-solving works on something that has a solution. All four are good. Three circle. Worry looks forward like planning, but produces no steps. Rumination looks back like reflection, but produces no insight; it replays the same pain in a different order. Obsessive thinking returns with a force that feels almost physical, demanding an answer before it lets you go, and the relief lasts minutes. One honest word. If your thoughts feel involuntary, come with strong urges to check, confess or repeat, or keep circling around whether you are truly saved, please also talk with a mental-health professional who understands these patterns. That is wisdom, not weakness. The support link is under this lesson.

Here is the main truth. The healthy four and the circling three are not separated by subject. You can reflect on a conversation, or ruminate on it. Plan for a meeting, or worry about it. Same subject. What separates them is the fruit. Jesus said you know a tree by its fruit. So ask: what is this thinking producing? Reflection produces understanding. Planning produces steps. Worry, rumination and obsession produce more of themselves. That is why "Is it important?" and "Am I being thorough?" cannot tell you if your thinking is healthy. Daniel's decision was important and he was thorough, and he was stuck, because he measured his thinking by how serious it was, not by what it produced.

So here is the tool. When your mind returns to a subject, ask five questions. One: is this producing a clear action, something I could write down and do? Two: am I learning anything new, or rearranging the same pieces? Three: is this within my control? Thinking hard about something outside your control does not bring it inside your control. Four: have I already decided this, and reopened it with no new information? Five: is this helping me obey God today? Even when a big decision is unclear, today's duties are usually plain: love the people in front of you, do your work, and rest. Ask the five once. If the answers are mostly no, the season for working on this question has ended, at least for now.

When the thinking is finished, what comes next is not more thought. It is one of four responses. Act: a clear responsibility is mine now. Wait: action may be needed, but not yet. Seek help: I lack information, skill, safety or support. Surrender: the outcome does not belong to me. You will meet these four again and again, and later lessons show you how to do each one well. For now, it is enough to see that when thinking has done its work, thinking is no longer the right tool.

Back to Daniel. When I suggested there was a time to stop, everything in him resisted: "If I stop thinking about it, I am being careless." Many overthinkers are conscientious people, and they hear "stop" as "stop caring". But look at his fruit. He stopped learning anything new in week three. By week six, the thinking was not care. It had become a way of avoiding the frightening moment of choosing. Stopping when the thinking has done its work is not carelessness. It is trust. And stopping does not mean forcing a thought out of your head; that makes it come back stronger. It means you stop deliberately working on the question and turn to what is in front of you. If the thought drifts back, that is not failure. You simply decline to reopen the file.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Choose one subject your mind returned to today. Just one. Ask the five questions once, and write a yes or a no beside each. Producing a clear action? Learning anything new? Within my control? Already decided? Helping me obey God today? If the honest picture is that nothing new is happening, you may say this once: "The season for working on this has ended for today." Then turn to one ordinary thing: a dish to wash, a person to speak to, a walk. The words do not have to bring relief before you move on. Pause the audio now and ask your five questions. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. There is a time to think, and a time to stop thinking. Careful thinking has a shape: it gathers what is needed, weighs it honestly, seeks counsel, prays, and then acts on the best wisdom available, accepting that certainty is not on offer. Endless thinking is not a stronger form of carefulness. It is a different thing wearing carefulness as a disguise. You do not have to keep circling to prove that you care.

Let us pray. Father, you know the questions I keep reopening. You know how much I want to be sure before I move. Show me when my thinking has done its work. Give me courage to act, patience to wait, humility to ask for help, and faith to let go of what was never mine to carry. Teach me to know the time. In Jesus' name. Amen. ...
