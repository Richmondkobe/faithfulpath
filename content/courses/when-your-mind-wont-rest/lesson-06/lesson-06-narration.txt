Welcome to Module 2, and Lesson 6. In Module 1 we learnt to notice what is happening. In this module we name the patterns, one at a time. Here is today's question. How fast can your mind get from a short email to losing your house? For some of us, about three hours. The teaching takes about eight minutes. Near the end, you will pause for one small writing practice, so have a pen nearby.

Here is what you will learn today: how to name a prediction as a prediction, and how to separate three things dread mixes together: what is possible, what is probable, and what is actually happening now. One caution first. If there is evidence of real danger, abuse, violence, coercion or a medical emergency, do not use this lesson to persuade yourself the danger is probably imaginary. Move to safety and contact appropriate help. And if vivid images of disaster arrive suddenly and drive you to check or seek reassurance again and again, a mental-health professional can help; the support link is under this lesson.

Our verse is Matthew 6, verse 34, in the English Standard Version. "Do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble." Notice that Jesus does not promise tomorrow will be trouble-free. He says the opposite: tomorrow will have its trouble, and so does today. This is an honest description of life, spoken by the one person who knew exactly what tomorrow held for him. Then he says today has enough in it. Israel gathered manna by the day. We ask for daily bread. Trying to live through every possible tomorrow in advance leaves us empty for today.

Let me tell you about a teacher I will call Esther. At half past four on a Thursday she got a short email from the head of her school: "Could you come and see me in my office at eight tomorrow morning? Thanks." By five she had decided she was being dismissed. By six, a parent must have complained. By eight she had worked out how long her savings would last and which subjects she might tutor. She slept for about three hours. At eight the next morning the head asked whether she would take on an extra class, because a colleague was going on maternity leave. The meeting lasted six minutes. "The worst part," Esther told me, "is that I knew while I was doing it. I knew I had no evidence. I did it anyway." If you recognise that sentence, this lesson is for you.

Esther's pattern is often called catastrophising. It is not simply noticing that something bad could happen; wisdom includes seeing danger and preparing for it. Catastrophising is a movement. A trigger: an email, a symptom, a delayed reply. A leap to the most threatening interpretation, within seconds. A chain, in which each imagined consequence becomes the basis for the next. And then real distress in the body, produced by an imagined future. It is hard to see from the inside because it is fast, and because it is plausible. Every link in Esther's chain was possible. That is exactly what made it convincing.

Here is the main truth. What is possible is not the same as what is probable, and what is probable is not the same as what is happening now. Dread collapses these three into one. The way out is to separate them again. On Thursday evening, the only thing established was that a meeting had been requested. That is the present. Everything else was possible, and none of it was supported by anything Esther actually knew. You can acknowledge uncertainty without treating the worst outcome as established fact.

The tool is four questions, asked in order. One: what am I predicting? Say it as a prediction, not a fact. "I am predicting that I will be dismissed." Naming it lets it be examined instead of obeyed. Two: what do I actually know that supports or challenges it? Not what you imagine; what you know. A brief email tells you very little either way. And for health, legal, money or safety questions, ask the qualified person rather than relying on this lesson. Three: what is actually happening right now? This is the question catastrophising most wants you to skip. For Esther: I have received an email asking for a meeting. I do not know what it is about. That is all. Four: what does the present require of me? Sometimes something: reply, book the appointment, ask a question. Often nothing. Esther's present required her to eat dinner, mark some books, and go to the meeting.

Many overthinkers half believe this: "If I imagine the worst, at least I will be ready for it." In my experience it does not work: people who rehearse disasters all night arrive at trouble already exhausted. And it is not preparation. Real preparation is specific, bounded, and gets finished. Catastrophising revisits the same scene without producing anything you could act on. If you have "prepared" for the same disaster forty times, you have not been preparing. You have been suffering. Very often the motive is love: you are afraid for people you care about. But love that has become dread is not serving anyone, least of all the people you are afraid for.

When Esther wrote her chain down, link by link, she could see that five of her six links rested on nothing she knew. So here is the practice. Slow the chain down enough to see it. Name it: "I am predicting." Write the chain exactly as your mind produced it. Then ask the four questions of the first link only. If the first link does not hold, the rest of the chain has nothing to hang from. Ask them once, carefully, and then turn to the meal, the task, the person in front of you. If the prediction comes back an hour later, remind yourself briefly of your answers; do not run the whole exercise again. Running it repeatedly turns a tool for clarity into a ritual for reassurance.

Now it is your turn. Take your pen. Choose one thing you have been forecasting this week. Write two lines. First: what is actually true right now, and nothing more. Second: what does today require of me in relation to it. Then do that much, and no more. Do not add actions the imagined chain requires. If today requires nothing, let the day be as ordinary as it actually is. Pause the audio now and write your two lines. Press play when you are ready. ...

One thing to remember. My mind can imagine many tomorrows. I have been given only today. Bring the present reality, not the imagined future, to God. There is a kind of prayer that is only catastrophising with "Lord" attached to the front of every sentence; we will look at that in Lesson 16. For now: tell him what is actually true, ask him for what today requires, and leave tomorrow where he has put it.

Let us pray. Father, you know the tomorrows I have been living in advance. You know the chains my mind builds out of one small trigger. Here is what is true today: no more, no less. Give me what today requires, and the strength to do that much. Let tomorrow stay in your hands, where you have put it. In Jesus' name. Amen. ... You may stop here for today. Nothing is lost if you do. When you are ready, mark the lesson complete.
