## Referral: When to Send Someone On

The front-matter list in “When This Course Is Not Enough” is your referral threshold, and you should keep it immediately accessible in every session; during a crisis, a written protocol is safer than memory. It says: thoughts of suicide or self-harm; panic attacks; an inability to function at work, at home or in relationships; symptoms following trauma; abuse or danger; severe or persistent anxiety or low mood that has not responded to ordinary support; obsessive- compulsive behaviours, including religious scrupulosity. Any of these means professional help, and the course is at most a companion to it.

Two more thresholds belong to the group setting.

First, the practices themselves. If a member reports that an exercise has become compulsory, that they are redoing worksheets to feel certain, that missing a practice produces disproportionate distress, or that they cannot stop, the course’s tools have met something they are not designed for. Stop the member’s use of the relevant practice, and help them reach a professional who understands obsessive- compulsive presentations. Do not respond by refining the worksheet.

Second, decline. If a member is visibly worse over the weeks, missing sessions, withdrawing, or increasingly hopeless, do not assume the course will get there eventually. Speak with them privately and help them find more.

When you refer, do it well. Say what you have noticed, without diagnosing. Say that this is beyond what the group or you can offer, and that this is not a judgement on their faith. Help them with the practical steps; the hardest part of getting help is often the first phone call. Ask whether they would like you to remain involved, and, if they consent, whether their clinician would find it useful for you to be in contact; pastoral and professional care can sometimes be coordinated, but only with the person’s consent and never over their head. Then keep caring for them. Referral is not handing someone off.

Never advise anyone to start, stop or change medication. Never describe medication as a failure of faith or as something to be prayed away. If a member asks your view, say that it is a matter for them and their prescriber, and that you will support whatever they decide together.
