1 · Burnout and boundaries

Welcome to Lesson 9. In Lesson 8 we looked at the situations that one good conversation cannot fix.

Today we look at the leader. How do I keep leading without wearing out, and without making those closest to me pay the bill?

2 · Leaders erode before they collapse

For many leaders, exhaustion develops gradually rather than dramatically. Like stone worn by water, it erodes.

It can look like this. You start dreading Sunday morning slightly. You notice you are short with your family, or your friends, on the way home. A volunteer cancels and your reaction is out of all proportion. Prayer feels like paperwork. And you find yourself thinking you would be relieved if the whole thing quietly ended.

Burnout can affect even deeply committed and conscientious leaders. It is not proof of laziness or weak faith. These warning signs deserve attention, although they do not by themselves tell us what is wrong. But they can signal that your workload, your support or your health needs care.

Scripture gives us a leader who reached the end of himself.

3 · “Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you”

Our passage is from 1 Kings chapter 19. We will read selected excerpts, slowly, as a story, and I will give the verse numbers as we go.

From verse 4. “He himself went a day’s journey into the wilderness and came and sat down under a broom tree. And he asked that he might die, saying, ‘It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers.’

And he lay down and slept under a broom tree. And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, ‘Arise and eat.’ And he looked, and behold, there was at his head a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water. And he ate and drank and lay down again.

And the angel of the LORD came again a second time and touched him and said, ‘Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.’ And he arose and ate and drank, and went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God.“

Then, at Horeb, from verse 11. “But the LORD was not in the wind. And after the wind an earthquake, but the LORD was not in the earthquake. And after the earthquake a fire, but the LORD was not in the fire. And after the fire the sound of a low whisper. And when Elijah heard it, he wrapped his face in his cloak and went out and stood at the entrance of the cave.”

4 · After Mount Carmel

To hear this story rightly, we need its setting.

In 1 Kings 18, Elijah has just seen God answer by fire on Mount Carmel, and rain return after a long drought. It was a moment of great victory.

Then Queen Jezebel sends him a message, threatening his life. And the next chapter tells us, “Then he was afraid, and he arose and ran for his life.” He travels south, leaves his servant behind, and goes on alone into the wilderness.

This is a real man at the end of his strength. Sometimes a leader feels the strain most sharply after a demanding season, even one that has gone well.

As we read on, I want to be careful. This story tells us what God did for Elijah. It is not a pattern that every recovery must follow, and God’s care for each person may look different. But what God did, and the order he did it in, is worth noticing.

5 · What God did, and the order of it

Look at what God does, and the order he does it in.

First, sleep. Elijah lay down and slept.

Then, food. An angel touches him, and there is bread and a jar of water. He eats and lies down again. And the angel comes a second time: “Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.” Only after all of that does the journey to Horeb begin.

Then, presence. At Horeb there is wind, earthquake and fire, and the LORD is not in any of them. And then, the sound of a low whisper.

Then, a wider picture. Elijah says, “I, even I only, am left.” But God tells him, “Yet I will leave seven thousand in Israel, all the knees that have not bowed to Baal.” Elijah’s distress was real. But he did not know the whole picture.

And God gives him further instructions, including to anoint Elisha “to be prophet in your place.” We will meet Elisha again in Lesson 10.

Elijah was not simply tired. He was afraid, isolated and so distressed that he asked God to take his life. We must take those words seriously. God provided sleep and food, met Elijah at Horeb, addressed his understanding of the situation and gave him further instructions. Those instructions included the appointment of Elisha, who would eventually succeed him. The passage shows God’s continuing care, but it does not give us a fixed formula for recovering from exhaustion.

Notice, too, how physical the first part of God’s care was. He let his prophet sleep, and he fed him twice.

6 · The sound of a low whisper

Notice the phrase in verse 12. In Hebrew it is qôl dĕmāmâ daqqâ. The ESV translates it, “the sound of a low whisper.”

The first word means a sound, or a voice. The second carries the idea of stillness, or a whisper. The third means thin, or fine. Older translations put it, “a still small voice.” Translators find different ways to say it, because the phrase itself is quiet and hard to pin down.

We should not build a rule on this, as if God only ever speaks quietly. In Scripture he also speaks in thunder and fire. But here, after the wind, the earthquake and the fire, God met his exhausted servant in the quiet.

Sometimes the leader who is running on noise needs to stop long enough to hear.

7 · Jesus told his team to stop

Now think of Jesus. In Lesson 5 we saw the disciples come back from their mission. Jesus said to them, “Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while.” Mark adds the reason: “For many were coming and going, and they had no leisure even to eat.”

The demand was real. The ministry was fruitful. And Jesus called a halt. Rest was his instruction, not their indulgence.

Behind that lies the Sabbath. In Genesis 2, “God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it God rested from all his work that he had done in creation.” And the fourth commandment says, “Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy.” Christians differ in how they keep the Sabbath today. But rest is written into the way God made the world. It is a gift, not a reward for finishing.

Psalm 127 says it plainly. “Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain… It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep.”

Ignoring rest is not extra devotion. It can be a quiet claim that the work depends on you.

8 · Point 1: The bill those closest to you may be paying

Our first point: the bill those closest to you may be paying.

In 1 Timothy 3, among the qualifications for an overseer, Paul says, “He must manage his own household well,” and then asks, “for if someone does not know how to manage his own household, how will he care for God’s church?” Paul is writing directly about overseers. The wider application for every leader is this: Christian leadership must not become an excuse for neglecting legitimate responsibilities and relationships.

This is where new leaders often do the most damage without noticing, because ministry provides such good cover. Sometimes a pastoral emergency genuinely requires a change of plans. But few people will criticise you for missing dinner again and again for good causes. The people at home cannot compete with a good cause, so after a while they may stop trying.

If you live alone, the bill may be paid in other ways: by friendships you no longer have time for, by your health, or by your own walk with God.

And a harder question. Is any part of you using ministry to avoid something at home? Ministry can be a socially applauded way to be absent. If that question landed somewhere, it is worth talking to someone you trust about it this month.

9 · Point 2: Learning to say no

Our second point: learning to say no.

Most overload is not one huge commitment. It is fifteen small yeses, given in corridors.

So pause before accepting a new commitment. “Let me check my calendar and the commitments this would affect. I’ll come back to you tomorrow.” If you share a household, involve those it would affect.

Decide your capacity in advance. In Lesson 2 you wrote down the hours you can realistically give in a normal week. When something new comes, ask what it replaces. If nothing can come off, the answer is no. And that is a full answer.

And say no to good things. In Mark 1, Jesus rose very early and went out to a desolate place to pray. Simon and the others found him and said, “Everyone is looking for you.” And he said, “Let us go on to the next towns, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out.”

There were still people in Capernaum who wanted him when he left. Jesus did not meet every need in front of him, because he knew which work was his. You will not meet every need either. That is not failure. It is having a calling rather than a compulsion.

10 · Point 3: What to put in place

Our third point: what to put in place.

One protected day a week. The same day where possible, with your phone away, and the people around you knowing it. Take it even when you feel fine. Especially then.

A finishing time on ministry evenings. Meetings that end when you said they would, as we saw in Lesson 6, are partly for your own sake.

A regular conversation, perhaps every few months, with your spouse, a family member or a trusted friend, about the effect of ministry on your life. Ask what they notice, rather than only reporting on the work.

A break each year that is longer than a weekend, with cover arranged in advance rather than hoping for a quiet fortnight.

The monthly person from Lesson 2, with permission to ask how you are really doing.

And look after your body. Sleep, food and some walking are spiritual disciplines in disguise. God started there with Elijah.

11 · When it is more than tiredness

Be honest with yourself about the difference between being tired and being unwell.

If the flatness does not lift after rest, if you have lost interest in things you used to enjoy, if your sleep has changed a great deal, or if you feel hopeless about life generally, and not just about the ministry, that is worth taking to a doctor, and not only to a prayer meeting.

Seeing a doctor is not a lack of faith, any more than having a broken arm set is.

There is more guidance on the lesson page, including what to do if you need help urgently. And tell someone you trust. Your worth is not measured by your usefulness to a church.

12 · “My yoke is easy”

Jesus says, “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.”

Jesus does not promise a life without costly service. But he invites us to learn from him. We should ask whether some of the weight we carry comes from unnecessary commitments, fear of disappointing people, or expectations God has not placed upon us.

13 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Notice the erosion early, before it becomes a crisis. Remember God’s continuing care for Elijah: rest, food, his presence, the truth about his situation, and further direction. Receive rest as God’s gift and Jesus’ instruction, not a reward for finishing. And count the cost to those closest to you, learn to say no to good things, and put rest in place.

“For he gives to his beloved sleep.”

14 · Watch it happen: a real day off

Let me show you what this looks like. Do you remember the man who leads the youth group, from Lesson 2? He has been taking a day off in theory, but in practice his phone is always on.

So he chooses a real day. He chooses Monday, the day after the busiest day of his week.

Then he arranges cover, so that rest does not leave the ministry uncovered. After discussing the arrangement with his pastor, he asks Chris, an experienced and authorised team leader, to provide cover on Mondays. They agree which matters Chris can handle, which require escalation, and how safeguarding concerns must be reported.

Then he tells the team. “From next week, Mondays are my day off. For anything urgent, please call Chris.”

15 · Watch it happen: keeping it, and listening at home

On the first Monday, a parent messages about the Friday youth evening. Chris handles it. Nothing falls apart.

That evening, he and his wife sit down together, and he asks her a question he has been avoiding. “Has my ministry been good for us this year?”

She is quiet for a moment. Then she says that Friday evenings have become the hardest night of the week at home. He wants to explain. Instead, he listens.

Together they agree one change they would like: one Friday evening a month, another leader would run the youth evening, and that evening would be theirs. The next day he asks his pastor, and they agree who will lead on those Fridays. Only then does he write it in his calendar and tell the team.

16 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. Think again about the signs of erosion: dreading Sunday, being short with those you love, prayer that feels like paperwork. Which ones are true of you right now?

The other four ask where you might need more information or support before concluding that you are alone, whether your ministry has been good for those closest to you this year, what you said yes to that you should have declined, and when your next day off and your next proper break will be.

17 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action.

Choose a protected day off within the next two weeks, and identify a possible break of several days within the next six months. Put the proposed dates in your calendar. If you need approval or cover, speak to your pastor or supervisor this week, and agree how to make the arrangement possible.

Then have one honest conversation with someone close to you, your spouse, a family member or a trusted friend, about the effect of ministry on your life. Listen before explaining.

18 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.” And, “He gives to his beloved sleep.”

The journey is too great for you on your own. That is why God gives rest, and people, and himself.

19 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord God, you let Elijah sleep, and you fed him when the journey was too great. You came to him not in the wind or the fire, but in the quiet. Teach me to rest without guilt, and to say no without fear. Guard those closest to me from paying for my ministry. Help me to take your yoke, and to lay down what you never gave me to carry. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
