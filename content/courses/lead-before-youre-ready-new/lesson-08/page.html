1 · Criticism, control and ongoing pastoral needs

Welcome to Lesson 8. In Part 1 of the course we started with ourselves. In Part 2 we learned the craft: inviting people, keeping them, running meetings, and having honest conversations.

Now we begin Part 3, Face What Tests You. Over the last three lessons we will look at the long-running pressures of leadership: opposition, weariness, and the question of who comes after you.

In Lesson 7, we learned how to begin an honest conversation. That can resolve many ordinary misunderstandings. But some situations continue even after we have spoken carefully and respectfully. They require more than one conversation. They may require better structures, shared responsibility, appropriate oversight or ongoing support. How do I respond to the situations one good conversation cannot fix?

2 · Three different situations

You may recognise some of these. The member who criticises everything you do. The long-standing volunteer who treats one area of the church as personal property. The person in genuine pain who is absorbing every hour you have.

These are three different situations. Criticism to assess. Over-centralised responsibility to address. And ongoing care needs to support. Each needs a different response, and sometimes they overlap in the same situation.

So throughout this lesson, we will describe what is happening and what help is needed, rather than putting a fixed label on the person.

We begin with a man who faced sustained opposition, and kept building.

3 · “I am doing a great work”

Our passage is from Nehemiah chapter 6. We will read selected excerpts from the chapter, and I will give the verse numbers as we go.

Verse 3. “And I sent messengers to them, saying, ‘I am doing a great work and I cannot come down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and come down to you?’”

Verse 8. “Then I sent to him, saying, ‘No such things as you say have been done, for you are inventing them out of your own mind.’”

Verse 9. “For they all wanted to frighten us, thinking, ‘Their hands will drop from the work, and it will not be done.’ But now, O God, strengthen my hands.”

From verse 11. “But I said, ‘Should such a man as I run away?’” And Nehemiah ends, “I will not go in.” In verse 13 he explains why: “For this purpose he was hired, that I should be afraid and act in this way and sin.”

And verse 15. “So the wall was finished on the twenty-fifth day of the month Elul, in fifty-two days.”

4 · Outside opponents of the work

To hear this passage rightly, we need its setting.

Nehemiah was cupbearer to the Persian king. With the king’s permission, he returned to Jerusalem to rebuild its broken walls, as we saw in Lessons 4 and 6.

Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem were regional figures who opposed the rebuilding from outside. They were not members of the community that was building. That matters. The people who criticise you in your church are not Sanballat and Tobiah, and this lesson does not teach you to treat them as enemies.

But Nehemiah’s pattern under pressure is worth learning: pray, answer briefly, and keep building.

5 · Four tactics

Across chapters 4 and 6, Nehemiah’s opponents try four different tactics.

First, ridicule. Sanballat mocks the builders, and Tobiah adds, “Yes, what they are building, if a fox goes up on it he will break down their stone wall!” Nehemiah prays, and the building goes on. “So we built the wall. And all the wall was joined together to half its height, for the people had a mind to work.”

Second, distraction. They invite him, again and again, to come down and meet them in the plain of Ono. Nehemiah understands that they mean to harm him, and gives the same answer each time: “I am doing a great work and I cannot come down.”

Third, slander. An open letter accuses him of planning a rebellion. His reply is one sentence: “No such things as you say have been done.” Then he prays, “But now, O God, strengthen my hands.”

Fourth, and most subtle, pressure dressed up as spiritual counsel. A man named Shemaiah urges him to hide in the temple, because men are coming to kill him. Nehemiah refuses, and then sees that Shemaiah had been hired to make him afraid and lead him into sin.

Ridicule, distraction, slander, and pressure dressed as spiritual counsel. Nehemiah’s pattern is the same every time. Pray. Answer briefly. Keep building.

Nehemiah’s example teaches us not to be controlled by hostile distractions. It does not teach us to dismiss legitimate complaints, avoid accountability or neglect people who need help. Different situations call for different responses.

6 · Section 1: Criticism to assess

Section 1: criticism to assess.

Not all criticism is opposition. Before you treat someone as a critic, sort the criticism.

The faithful critic tells you directly, often privately, and is often right. That person is a gift, even when the delivery stings. Protect them, thank them, and never punish them for honesty. A leader who does that ends up surrounded by people who agree with everything and warn him about nothing.

Repeated criticism can become wearing. But how often someone raises a concern does not tell you whether it is valid. Ask for specific examples, check the substance, and set reasonable boundaries for how feedback is raised. Avoid assuming you know their motives.

Anonymous or relayed concerns should be assessed on their content, not dismissed because the person has not come to you. Fear or a difference in power may explain why. Invite further information through a safe channel. And refer any safeguarding concern through the proper route straight away; never delay it while you look for more examples.

For all criticism, ask two questions. What is accurate? And what needs checking? A poorly delivered complaint can still reveal a real problem.

7 · Example: an email that stings

Here is a short example. Do you remember the woman who leads the welcome team, from Lesson 4? One Monday evening she receives an email. “The welcome is cold, and you never listen to anyone.”

It stings. So she waits until the next morning before replying.

Then she sorts it. What is accurate? She checks the rota, and finds that the door was unstaffed twice this month. That is a real problem, and it needs fixing. What needs checking? “You never listen to anyone.” She does not know what that refers to.

So she replies. “Thank you for telling me. You’re right that the door wasn’t covered twice this month, and I’m sorting that out. Could you give me an example of when you felt you weren’t listened to? I’ll come back to you by the end of next week.”

She does not argue about the tone. She addresses the substance first.

8 · Section 2: Over-centralised responsibility to address

Section 2: over-centralised responsibility to address.

In his third letter, John writes, “I have written something to the church, but Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first, does not acknowledge our authority.” John says that Diotrephes is “talking wicked nonsense against us,” that “he refuses to welcome the brothers, and also stops those who want to and puts them out of the church.”

That is an extreme case: one man controlling access and reputation, in a church of the apostles’ time.

You may recognise a different and much more ordinary problem. It is usually attached to one area: the kitchen, the sound desk, the flowers, the accounts, held by one person for many years. The signs are familiar. Information is not shared. Keys are not handed over. New volunteers do not seem to last. And any change is met with, “We’ve always done it this way.”

Not every person who holds too much responsibility is seeking control. Sometimes the church has never trained a successor, documented its procedures or given the volunteer the support needed to share the work. Often it begins with years of faithful service. So describe it as a pattern, not as a person: responsibility that has become over-centralised.

9 · Change the structure, with the right authority

Three principles help.

First, address the dependency by improving the structure, not by fighting over territory. Written procedures. More than one person trained. Rotas set by the leader responsible. Keys held centrally. Explain the changes respectfully to those affected, honour their contribution, and involve the appropriate leader when authority or disagreement is involved. Where a conversation is needed, Lesson 7 shows how to begin it.

Second, be clear about authority. This is where Lesson 3 helps. If you do not know exactly what is yours to decide, the gap will be filled by someone else.

Third, involve the appropriate leader early. Clarify your authority before you change systems or address long-established behaviour. If the concern involves your pastor, use a suitable independent route.

Scripture also shows that there is a limit. Paul tells Titus, “As for a person who stirs up division, after warning him once and then twice, have nothing more to do with him.” And he warns Timothy about Alexander the coppersmith: “Beware of him yourself, for he strongly opposed our message.”

That verse is about someone who stirs up division. It must never be used simply because someone disagrees with a leader or questions a decision. You are not required to absorb divisive behaviour indefinitely. But in a church, reaching that limit is a decision for the church’s leadership, not for a new ministry leader acting alone.

10 · Example: the sound desk

Here is the second example. The worship-team leader from Lesson 3 has a sound desk that only one person can run. That volunteer has served faithfully for twenty years. He holds the only keys, and new helpers rarely last.

First, the leader checks with his pastor that the change is his to make, and agrees the plan. Then he does not argue over who owns the desk. He changes the structure. He writes a simple guide to the desk. He arranges for a second person, Sam, to be trained, using the four steps from Lesson 5. And the keys are now signed out from the church office.

He speaks to the long-serving volunteer first, privately, and thanks him for twenty years of faithful service. Then he asks for his help. “You know this desk better than anyone. Would you help me train Sam?”

The structure changes. The person is honoured. And if the pattern continues, the leader knows who to involve next.

11 · Section 3: Ongoing care needs to support

Section 3: ongoing care needs to support.

This one is often the hardest, because the person is genuinely hurting, and your instinct to help is right.

In John 5, Jesus meets a man who had been an invalid for thirty-eight years. He asks him, “Do you want to be healed?” And the man explains the obstacle he faces: “Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up.” Then Jesus heals him.

This passage is about Jesus’ power and compassion. It must never be used to suggest that someone in long-term illness or repeated distress does not want help. But notice that the man was given room to say what was in the way.

So listen to the obstacles a person describes before suggesting what they should do. Some barriers need practical support, professional care, or a change in circumstances that a short pastoral conversation cannot provide.

A helpful question is: “What support would be useful now, and what has made it hard to access?” Ask it with curiosity, not as a test.

12 · Burdens and load

Notice two words in Galatians 6. In verse 2 Paul writes, “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ.” The word for “burdens” is barē, the plural of a word for weight. Then in verse 5 he writes, “For each will have to bear his own load.” The word for “load” is a different noun, phortion, here in the singular.

Paul uses two different words, and the difference can help us notice two ideas. But we should not make a rule out of the words alone. What holds the two verses together is Paul’s argument in verses 1 to 5: restore someone gently, watch yourself, and test your own work.

Read together, these verses call us to mutual help, without pretending that one person can carry another’s whole life. They are not a test for deciding whose pain is real. They are an invitation to share the weight, wisely and together.

13 · A shape, more than one helper, and honest limits

Practically, three things help.

Set a shape. “I can meet for an hour on the first Tuesday of the month” is more sustainable, and more genuinely helpful, than being available at all hours until you quietly stop replying. But the right arrangement depends on the person’s needs, your responsibilities and the support available. Sometimes monthly contact is suitable. Sometimes more frequent help or prompt referral is needed. Agree a realistic plan rather than assuming one schedule fits everyone.

Do not be their only support. Make sure a small group, a couple of mature believers, or the pastoral team is involved, with the person’s agreement. Depending on one leader alone is not good for them, and not safe for you.

And know where your competence ends. Where there is depression, trauma, addiction, or anything you are not trained for, encourage professional help alongside spiritual support, and involve your pastor. Saying, “I’ll keep walking with you, and I also think you should see a doctor about this,” is not a failure of faith. It is honest care.

Follow your church’s safeguarding arrangements when you meet with anyone who may be at risk, and explain the limits of confidentiality. If someone may be in danger, use the reporting route on the Concerns, Care and Reporting page.

14 · Example: a monthly time

Here is the third example. The woman who leads a home group, from Lesson 1, has been supporting a member through a long, hard season. The messages come late at night, and she has begun to dread her phone.

She sits down with her, and says, “I care about you, and I’d love to keep meeting. Could we meet on the first Tuesday of each month, for an hour?” Then she asks, “Would it help if one of the pastoral team joined us, or met with you too?” And, gently, “I think it would be good to talk to your doctor as well.”

For her, a monthly time, alongside the pastoral team, was the right shape. For someone else it might need to be more frequent, or include prompt referral. The woman agrees to the monthly time and to meeting someone from the pastoral team. The home-group leader keeps her promise, and she is no longer the only one carrying it.

15 · Do not come down off the wall

The sentence to carry out of this lesson is Nehemiah’s: “I am doing a great work and I cannot come down.”

Unresolved conflict and unmet care needs can consume your attention. Set responsible boundaries, and arrange the help that is needed, while you keep attending to the wider team.

Nehemiah’s refusal to meet hostile opponents is not permission to ignore complaints or people in distress. Faithful critics deserve a hearing. People in pain deserve care. But not every invitation to come down off the wall needs to be accepted.

When you face hostile distraction, answer briefly. Pray honestly. Then get back on the wall.

16 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. When the situation is criticism, assess it: what is accurate, and what needs checking? When it is over-centralised responsibility, change the structure, with the right authority. And when it is an ongoing care need, give it a shape, involve more than one helper, and be honest about your limits.

And when you face hostile distraction, remember Nehemiah. Pray. Answer briefly. Keep building. Other situations call for careful listening, prompt action, or continued care.

17 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. Describe the situation currently taking most of your attention. Is it criticism to assess, over-centralised responsibility to address, a care need to support, or a combination?

The other four ask about the last criticism that stung, an area that depends entirely on one person, who you are currently the only support for, and how much of your attention one situation has been taking. Keep your notes general. Do not write names or identifiable pastoral details.

18 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action. Choose one appropriate step for the situation you described.

If it is criticism, name the specific concern, what needs checking, and who should respond. Give a realistic follow-up date where you can.

If it is over-centralised responsibility, identify one responsibility or system held by a single person, and, with the right authority, agree a plan for shared oversight. Record where access is managed, but never copy passwords into a notebook or an ordinary email.

If it is an ongoing care need, agree a realistic next step with the person and, where appropriate, your pastoral leader. That may be a regular time, more frequent help, or a prompt referral. Share personal information only as needed, and through the proper channels.

Whichever you choose, respond thoughtfully and pray for wisdom. But do not delay urgent reporting while you wait to feel calm.

19 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “I am doing a great work and I cannot come down.” And, “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ.”

Stay on the wall. And carry the burdens of others together, not alone.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord God, you strengthened Nehemiah’s hands when others tried to stop the work. Strengthen my hands too. Give me humility to hear the truth in criticism, and wisdom to answer briefly. Give me courage to change what needs changing, with the right authority. Help me to care for those who are hurting, with others beside me, and with honest limits. Keep me faithful to the work you have given me. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
