1 · Knowing your role

Welcome to Lesson 3. In Lesson 1 we saw that God answered Moses first with the promise of his presence, and you wrote down three things you do not yet know how to do. In Lesson 2 we turned to your own soul: life and doctrine together, and time protected with God.

Today we finish the first part of the course, Start With Yourself, with a very practical question: where does your role begin, and where does it end? What is yours to decide, what belongs to someone else, and how do you serve under authority with a clear conscience?

And we begin with a problem nearly every new leader meets in the first year.

2 · Many new-leader problems begin with unclear roles

Many first-year leadership conflicts grow out of one thing: a role that has never been clearly defined.

You made a decision you thought was yours, and discovered it was not. You waited for permission on something everyone assumed you would simply handle. You told a volunteer one thing, and the pastor had already told them something different. Or you were criticised for a decision you had no part in making.

If any of that sounds familiar, take heart. Those situations often reflect unclear boundaries rather than poor character. And unclear boundaries can be fixed. But before we draw any lines, we open the Bible, because Scripture has a good deal to say about how authority works among God’s people.

3 · Shepherd the flock

Our passage is from the first letter of Peter. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. 1 Peter chapter 5, verses 1 to 5.

“So I exhort the elders among you, as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, as well as a partaker in the glory that is going to be revealed: shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you; not for shameful gain, but eagerly; not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock. And when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory. Likewise, you who are younger, be subject to the elders. Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another, for ’God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble.’”

4 · A fellow elder, writing to scattered churches

To hear this passage rightly, we need to know who was writing, and to whom.

Peter addresses his letter to believers scattered across five regions of what is now Turkey: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia and Bithynia. He calls them “elect exiles.” They were small communities, far apart, and the letter speaks again and again about suffering for the name of Christ.

And notice how Peter introduces himself in chapter 5. He was an apostle. He had walked with Jesus. Although Peter writes with apostolic authority, he appeals to them as “a fellow elder.”

The elders were the leaders of those local churches, and Peter’s picture for their work is a shepherd with a flock. This passage is written to elders, and I want to be clear about that. But the pattern it gives, how to hold responsibility for God’s people, reaches every one of us who leads anything in a church. So we apply it, carefully, to your role too.

5 · Not domineering

Now look closely at one word in verse 3. Peter tells the elders not to be “domineering over those in your charge.” The Greek word is katakyrieuontes, from the verb katakyrieuō. It means to exercise mastery over others, or to domineer. Peter is warning leaders not to use their position to control the people entrusted to their care.

The same Greek verb appears in Mark chapter 10, where Jesus warns his disciples against the way Gentile rulers exercise power over others. His disciples had been arguing about who would be greatest, and he says, “those who are considered rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them... But it shall not be so among you.” Peter was there that day. Years later, writing to elders, he reaches for the same word.

So Peter sets three positives against it. Shepherd willingly, not under compulsion. Eagerly, not for gain. And by being an example, not by controlling people. Whatever authority a leader carries in the church, it is meant to serve, not to dominate.

6 · Accountability runs both ways

Then verse 4 lifts our eyes. The elders will give their account when “the chief Shepherd appears.” The person who shepherds is himself under a Shepherd. Leaders in the church are accountable to Christ. That is the deepest accountability. In practice, it also works through ordinary means: the church’s governance, its oversight of each ministry, and its complaints and reporting processes. Both matter, and one does not replace the other.

And verse 5 turns to everyone else: “you who are younger, be subject to the elders.” Then it gathers everybody together: “Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another.”

Read the whole passage together and you see both sides at once. Leaders are called to serve without domination. The church is called to humility and to responsible cooperation with those who lead. Respect for leaders does not require silence about wrongdoing, and accountability belongs on both sides.

With that foundation in place, we can now look at four things that are easily blurred together. We will take them one at a time.

7 · Point 1: Delegated authority — the centurion

Our first point is delegated authority.

In Matthew chapter 8, a Roman centurion comes to Jesus in Capernaum about his servant, who is paralysed and suffering. Jesus offers to come to the house. The centurion says that is not necessary: “only say the word, and my servant will be healed.” And then he explains why: “For I too am a man under authority, with soldiers under me. And I say to one, ’Go,’ and he goes, and to another, ’Come,’ and he comes.”

Jesus marvels, and says, “with no one in Israel have I found such faith.”

Let me be careful here. The main point of this passage is the centurion’s faith in the power of Jesus’ word. That is what Jesus commends. But in explaining his faith, the centurion gives us a clear picture of how delegated authority works, and we can learn from that picture as an application.

8 · Under authority, within limits

Notice the logic. The centurion does not say, “I am a man with authority.” He says, “I am a man under authority.” His orders carried weight because he stood inside a chain of command, and behind him stood the authority of Rome. When he gave an order, what made it work was not his personality.

Now, the centurion’s words illustrate delegated authority. They do not give us a model of church government; a church is not an army. But the picture helps. In a church ministry, your responsibilities are normally entrusted to you through the church’s recognised leadership and agreed arrangements. That authority has limits. It must remain accountable to Scripture, the church’s responsibilities and the proper oversight of the ministry. And it operates within the area it was given for. Your authority to lead the welcome team does not extend to changing the sound desk, and your authority to run the youth group does not include rewriting the church’s safeguarding procedure.

That is not a demotion. It is what makes your authority trustworthy. People can follow you with confidence when they know you are working inside what has been entrusted to you.

9 · Point 2: Accountability

Our second point is accountability. Authority and accountability are related, but they are not the same thing. Authority is what you have been given. Accountability is to whom you answer for how you use it.

As a new leader, you answer to someone: your pastor, a ministry head, an elder, or whoever oversees your area. And here is the part we easily forget: they answer too. Hebrews describes church leaders as “those who will have to give an account.” And Peter has already told us that elders serve under the chief Shepherd.

So accountability is not a ladder with you at the bottom and everyone above you beyond question. Everyone with responsibility answers to someone, and all of us answer to Christ.

10 · Point 3: Obedience and cooperation

Our third point is obedience and cooperation, and here we come to a verse that is often quoted and sometimes misused. Hebrews chapter 13, verse 17.

“Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account. Let them do this with joy and not with groaning, for that would be of no advantage to you.”

11 · Cooperation, read in its setting

Hebrews was written to believers under pressure, some of whom were tempted to drift away from Christ, and the letter urges them to hold fast together. In that setting, the writer asks them to cooperate with their leaders, and gives the reason: these leaders are keeping watch over their souls, and they will have to give an account.

Then he adds a reason that is easy to skip: “Let them do this with joy and not with groaning, for that would be of no advantage to you.” The argument is practical, not authoritarian. Your pastor carries a real weight of responsibility for people. Making that weight heavier through resistance, gossip or quiet undermining costs everyone, including you. Help carry it.

But notice what the verse does not say. It does not say leaders are beyond question. It does not ask anyone to keep silent about wrongdoing. Cooperation is not silence, and it is not agreement with sin. And the New Testament shows us what honest disagreement looks like.

12 · Honest disagreement, in the right room

In Galatians chapter 2, Paul tells us that when Peter came to Antioch, “I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned.” Peter had been eating with Gentile believers, and then withdrew when a group arrived from Jerusalem, and others followed him. Paul saw that this denied the truth of the gospel, and he said so. We should remember that both were apostles, and the issue was the gospel itself. But the passage shows that even the most respected leaders could be challenged when something was truly wrong.

In Acts chapter 15, the church faced a serious dispute about what was required of Gentile believers. They did not bury it. They gathered, they debated openly, they listened to Peter, to Paul and Barnabas, and to James, and they reached a decision together. And at the end of that same chapter, Paul and Barnabas disagreed so sharply about John Mark that they went separate ways. Acts records that disagreement without presenting every part of it as a model. But it also shows that God went on using both men afterwards.

So here is a useful pattern for an ordinary decision within your team’s area. Before the decision, raise your concern honestly, with reasons, in the right room: usually with your pastor, not with your team. After the decision, if you can support it in good conscience, put it into practice without undermining it. And if you cannot, say so directly to your pastor, and be willing to step back from that part of the work. This principle concerns ordinary, lawful ministry decisions. It does not prevent you from seeking review, using an appeal process or reporting wrongdoing when necessary.

13 · Point 4: Reporting wrongdoing — the rebellion warning, used honestly

Our fourth point is reporting wrongdoing. And to get there, we need to handle one passage honestly.

Numbers chapter 16 tells of Korah, Dathan and Abiram, who rose up against Moses and Aaron with two hundred and fifty leaders of the congregation. Korah, a Levite, was seeking the priesthood itself. It was a grave challenge to the roles God had appointed, and the account ends in judgement.

That passage has been badly misused. It gets quoted at anyone who asks a hard question, as if every concern were rebellion. So read it in its own setting. Numbers 16 describes a challenge to Moses and Aaron’s appointed roles in Israel’s history. It is not a simple template for every disagreement in a modern church.

14 · Asking for help is not rebellion

So here is the line between them. Do not spread accusations or deliberately stir up division to gain influence. But do not confuse that behaviour with seeking confidential advice, gathering appropriate evidence, using a complaints process or reporting harm. Numbers 16 must not be used to silence people who raise legitimate concerns.

None of those is rebellion. Sometimes it is wise, or even necessary, to seek help before approaching the person concerned, especially where there is a difference in power or a fear of being punished for speaking.

And if you are ever asked to keep quiet about harm, or if raising a question brings punishment, seek independent advice and use the right reporting route. You do not need the permission of the leader involved. Submission never requires covering wrongdoing. Do not let a misused passage keep you silent.

15 · Getting the boundary drawn: four questions

So how do you get the boundary drawn in practice? Here is the conversation I would have you schedule with whoever you report to. Twenty minutes, and four questions.

First: what decisions can I make alone? Ask for real examples: spending up to what amount, changing a meeting time, choosing songs, adding a volunteer to the rota.

Second: which decisions need approval before I act, and which situations must I report promptly, whether or not I have already acted? These are two different things. Approval is about spending, teaching, appointments and changes of role. Reporting is about situations your church needs to know about, and safeguarding concerns must follow the church’s reporting process. You do not wait for a manager’s permission to raise them.

Third: what do you want to hear about, even when I have handled it? A complaint, a volunteer stepping down, a pastoral situation in your team.

And fourth: how do you want me to raise a disagreement, and when?

Churches order their leadership in different ways, so the person you ask may be a pastor, an elder, a ministry head or a staff member. The four questions work in every church.

16 · Watch it happen: twenty minutes and an email

Let me show you what this looks like.

A man has just been asked to lead the worship team. He books twenty minutes with his pastor and takes the four questions with him, on the Role and Decision Agreement from this lesson’s toolkit.

He asks, “Can I choose the songs each week?” The pastor says, “Yes, that’s yours. Plan the rehearsals however works best.” “Can I move rehearsal to Thursday?” “Not on your own. It affects the building and other teams, so ask me first.” “What can I spend?” They agree an amount for small items; anything above it needs approval. “Can I add someone new to the team?” “Talk to me before they join the team.” “What do you want to hear about even when I’ve handled it?” “If someone steps down, or if there’s a complaint, tell me anyway.” And, “How should I raise a disagreement?” “Come and see me, preferably before Sunday, not in the middle of a rehearsal.”

That evening he writes a short email. “Thank you for your time today. Here is what I understand we agreed.” Four short lines, one for each question. The next morning the pastor replies, “Yes, that’s right.”

His church’s answers are just one example, not a policy every church should adopt. Yours will be different. What matters is the conversation. Twenty minutes, four questions and one email. A clear record like that prevents a great many misunderstandings later, on both sides.

17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Four things that are easily blurred, kept distinct.

Delegated authority: what has been entrusted to you, its limits, and its accountability. Accountability: you answer to someone, and so do they, and all of us answer to Christ. Cooperation: in ordinary, lawful decisions, raise concerns honestly before a decision, and support it faithfully afterwards, or step back from that part, while review, appeal and reporting remain open. And reporting wrongdoing through appropriate channels: that is not rebellion, and it does not depend on the permission of the person concerned.

And over all of it, Peter’s word to the whole church: “Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another.”

18 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

19 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action. It is the same thing you just watched.

Ask your pastor or ministry head for twenty minutes, and take the four questions with you. You will find them on the Role and Decision Agreement on the lesson page. Write the answers down while you are there, and email them back the same day for confirmation: “Here is what I understand we agreed.”

And one more thing. If an urgent concern needs attention, do not hold it for this meeting. Use the right route now.

20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “Shepherd the flock of God that is among you... not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock.” And, “Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another.”

Your authority is a trust. Hold it humbly, use it to serve, and know clearly where it begins and ends.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord Jesus, chief Shepherd of your people, thank you for the trust you have given me. Help me to serve willingly and eagerly, never domineering, but as an example. Give me humility to work under those who lead me, and courage to speak honestly in the right place. Keep me from gossip and division, and never let me stay silent about what is wrong. Make my part of your church a place where people are safe and served. In your name. Amen.
