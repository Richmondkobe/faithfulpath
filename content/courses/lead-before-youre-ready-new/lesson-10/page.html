1 · Growing the next leader

Welcome to Lesson 10, the last lesson of this course. In Lesson 9 we looked at how to keep leading without wearing out.

Today we look beyond ourselves, to someone else. Who will carry this after me, and am I making room for them?

2 · The measure nobody uses

Most of us measure our leadership by what is happening now. How many came. Whether the rota is full. Whether last Sunday went smoothly.

There is a better measure, and it is uncomfortable. What will still be standing five years after you leave, and who will be leading it?

By that measure, a leader who builds a thriving ministry that collapses the week he moves away may not have built something lasting, though a ministry can also falter for reasons beyond one leader’s control. And a leader who quietly raises four people who go on to lead may have done something lasting, even if his own numbers were unremarkable.

This is the last lesson, because it is often the last thing new leaders think about. It should be one of the first.

3 · “Entrust to faithful men”

Our passage is from Paul’s second letter to Timothy, chapter 2, verses 1 and 2. Paul writes:

“You then, my child, be strengthened by the grace that is in Christ Jesus, and what you have heard from me in the presence of many witnesses entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also.”

4 · Paul’s four generations

To hear this rightly, we need its setting. Paul is writing from prison, near the end of his life. In chapter 4 he says, “the time of my departure has come.” This is one of the last things he wrote.

And in one sentence, he looks ahead. Count the generations. Paul, to Timothy, to faithful people, to others. Four generations of learning in a single verse.

Paul is asking Timothy to teach faithfully and to prepare others who can pass that teaching on. He looks beyond one gifted teacher to a continuing chain. And Timothy, the second person in that chain, was already serving during Paul’s lifetime. The handing on had begun long before the end.

Notice the criterion. Faithful, and able to teach others. Not the most gifted. Not the most eager. Reliable, and able to pass it on. These are Paul’s criteria for those entrusted with teaching. Not every helpful volunteer must become a teacher. But every leader can ask who will carry on what they have been given.

And notice where Paul begins. “Be strengthened by the grace that is in Christ Jesus.” Before Timothy entrusts anything to anyone, he needs grace himself.

5 · Moses and Joshua: a long apprenticeship, a public handover

Paul was not the first to think about who comes next. Long before, Moses had been preparing Joshua for years.

Exodus 33 tells us that when Moses returned to the camp, “his assistant Joshua the son of Nun, a young man, would not depart from the tent.” Joshua had been near Moses for many years. Scripture shows him near Moses at important moments: on the mountain, and at the tent of meeting. He was learning by being close.

Then, near the end of Moses’ life, in Numbers 27, Moses himself raises the question of who will lead next, so that the LORD’s people “may not be as sheep that have no shepherd.” And God tells him to take Joshua, “a man in whom is the Spirit,” and, “Make him stand before Eleazar the priest and all the congregation, and you shall commission him in their sight.”

Two things stand out. The handover was public and clear, not a quiet drift where people slowly noticed Joshua doing more. And the public moment was the last step of a long apprenticeship, not the whole thing.

Moses and Joshua had a unique place in Israel’s history, and we are not leading a nation through the wilderness. But the principle carries. People learn to lead by being near someone who leads, over time. That cannot be produced by a course alone. It comes from letting someone be near you while you work.

6 · Elijah’s cloak

In Lesson 9 we saw God tell Elijah to anoint Elisha “to be prophet in your place.” First Kings 19 tells us what happened next.

Elijah found Elisha ploughing, “with twelve yoke of oxen in front of him.” And “Elijah passed by him and cast his cloak upon him.” It was a call to follow.

Elisha said goodbye to his family. Then he sacrificed his oxen, “and boiled their flesh with the yokes of the oxen and gave it to the people, and they ate. Then he arose and went after Elijah and assisted him.” He used his own ploughing equipment to cook the meal. It was a decisive commitment to follow.

Many years later, in Second Kings 2, Elijah was about to be taken. Elisha asked, “Please let there be a double portion of your spirit on me.” And when Elijah had gone, Elisha “took up the cloak of Elijah that had fallen from him,” and went back to the Jordan.

The same cloak. Thrown over him at the plough. Picked up at the Jordan. And between those two moments, years of assisting.

Elijah’s calling as a prophet was unique, and so was Elisha’s. But here is a question for every leader. Whose shoulders is your cloak resting on?

7 · paratithēmi: entrust

Notice the word Paul uses for “entrust.” In Greek it comes from the verb paratithēmi. It means to place something beside someone, and it can mean to deposit something with them for safekeeping.

That is the verb, the action of entrusting. Paul also uses a related noun, parathēkē, for the thing entrusted, “the deposit.” He tells Timothy, “guard the good deposit entrusted to you.” And in his first letter, “O Timothy, guard the deposit entrusted to you.”

So the picture is of something valuable, placed into trustworthy hands, to be kept safe and handed on whole.

We should not build too much on one word. But the passage itself makes this clear: what Paul passes on is the gospel and the teaching he received. It is not his position, and it is not his platform. When we develop the next leader, we are not cloning ourselves. We are passing on something that was never ours to begin with.

8 · Barnabas, the model most of us should copy

If you want one person in Scripture to imitate in this, take Barnabas.

In Acts 9, Saul, newly converted, arrives in Jerusalem. “And they were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles.” Barnabas told them how Saul had seen the Lord, and how he had preached boldly in Damascus.

Somebody had to vouch for a man everyone distrusted. That cost Barnabas something. He put his own reputation beside Saul’s.

Then in Acts 11, the church in Antioch is growing. “So Barnabas went to Tarsus to look for Saul, and when he had found him, he brought him to Antioch. For a whole year they met with the church and taught a great many people.”

Barnabas did not wait for Saul to apply. He went and fetched him.

9 · “Paul and his companions”

Now watch the order of the names. In Acts 13, the Holy Spirit says, “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul.” Barnabas comes first. A few verses later, Luke writes, “Now Paul and his companions set sail.” Paul’s name now comes first. Luke does not tell us how Barnabas felt about that. But his recorded actions are enough: he welcomed Saul when others would not, and he brought him into the work.

And in Lesson 5 we saw what Barnabas did with John Mark, the young man who had turned back. When Paul would not take him, Barnabas did. He stayed with someone who had turned back. Years later, Paul wrote, “Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry.” Scripture does not tell us all that brought about that change. But it shows us a man who did not give up on people.

Barnabas is remembered less for what he built than for the people he helped into the work.

10 · The fear that stops us

Let us be honest about what stops us. Sometimes the obstacle is time, resources or a shortage of suitable people. Those difficulties are real. But sometimes another obstacle lies within us: the fear that someone else may do the work better than we do.

Raising someone up means admitting you are replaceable. If the person you train turns out to be better at this than you, more gifted, more liked, more effective, what are you for?

That fear can be one reason leaders keep the interesting work, hand out the small tasks, and train nobody. It is rarely conscious. It often presents itself as high standards.

Listen to John the Baptist. His disciples come to him worried that everyone is going to Jesus instead. And John says he is like the friend of the bridegroom, who “rejoices greatly at the bridegroom’s voice.” Then he says, “He must increase, but I must decrease.”

John was speaking about Christ. That is what the verse means. It is not about handing on a ministry. But John’s joy at Christ’s increase shows us the spirit we need when someone else grows. If someone else’s growth threatens you, bring that honestly to God, and to a trusted mentor. Their development is part of the work you have been given.

11 · Christians differ: “greater works”

Jesus says in John 14, “whoever believes in me will also do the works that I do; and greater works than these will he do, because I am going to the Father.”

Christians understand those “greater works” in different ways. Some think of the spread of the gospel across the world after Pentecost. Others think of the work of the Spirit through the church. But none of these readings means a disciple becomes greater than Jesus. The verse directs our attention to the continuing work of God through his people, because Jesus has gone to the Father.

Whatever we build, the work was always his.

12 · How to do it: spot, include, entrust

So how do we do it, practically?

First, spot them by faithfulness. Look for the person already doing something small, well, without being chased. Availability and teachability may matter more than natural talent alone, and often you can see something of them before you ask. Faithfulness should be considered alongside character, teachability, relevant ability, the person’s willingness and the requirements of the particular role. And where the role involves children, young people or vulnerable adults, your church’s checks come first.

Second, bring them into the thinking, not just the doing. This is the difference between a helper and a leader in training. Say out loud why you decided something, including the option you rejected. Let them hear the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

Third, give them something real, early. Not only putting out the chairs, but a piece of work with real decisions attached, and their name on it. Use the four steps from Lesson 5: I do it, you watch. We do it together. You do it, I watch. You do it, I’m available.

13 · How to do it: room to learn, their way, their future

Fourth, allow room to learn in low-risk work. Agree what they may decide, and when they must seek help. An awkward first attempt can teach. Avoidable harm cannot be treated as a training exercise. So step in when safety, safeguarding, money or serious consequences are at stake.

Fifth, in low-risk matters, let them do it differently. They will. There, their way at eighty percent, owned, is often better than your way at a hundred, borrowed. That is not a standard for safeguarding, teaching, money or other serious decisions.

Sixth, talk about their future, not your vacancy. Ask what they would like to be doing in three years. Then aim your investment at that, even if it leads them out of your ministry. Barnabas did not keep Saul for himself.

14 · Hand over in the open, then leave well

Seventh, when the time comes for a formal handover, do it in the open. Not every delegated task needs a public commissioning. But when someone is appointed to lead, it should be clear. In Numbers 27, Moses “took Joshua and made him stand before Eleazar the priest and the whole congregation, and he laid his hands on him and commissioned him.” In your church, that means agreeing the change with those who appoint leaders, and then saying publicly that this person now carries this.

And then visibly stop deciding it. Do not hover. Do not take the calls behind their back. When someone brings you a question that is now theirs, say so, and send them on.

You will not lead this forever. When you go, whether you move, take on something else, or simply finish, leave it better documented, better staffed and better understood than you found it. Write down how things work. Introduce your successor personally to the people who matter. Say something warm about them in public. Then let it be clear that they have your full confidence, and resist being consulted about everything from a distance.

15 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Measure your leadership by what continues after you. Entrust what you received to faithful people who can teach others. Expect a long apprenticeship, and then hand over in public. And be a Barnabas: vouch for people, go and fetch them, and make room when they grow.

And remember who must increase. John said it of Christ: “He must increase, but I must decrease.”

16 · Watch it happen: the first conversation

Let me show you what this looks like. Do you remember the woman who leads the welcome team, from Lesson 4? She invited a man in his fifties to lead the welcome at the second service. He has now served faithfully there for most of a year.

First, she speaks with her pastor. She would like to invest in him over the next year, with a view to him carrying more responsibility in time. Her pastor agrees.

Then she meets him at the same café. “You’ve been faithful at the second service all year. People feel welcome because of you. I’d like to invest in you over the next year, with a view to you leading more in time. I can’t promise a particular role. That isn’t mine alone to give. But would you be willing? Take a few days to think about it.”

He comes back on Thursday. He says yes.

17 · Watch it happen: the first real piece of work

Then she hands him one real piece of work, agreed with her pastor. For this term, he will plan how first-time guests are welcomed and followed up at the second service.

They agree the limits. “You decide where the welcome desk goes, who greets, and what the follow-up card says. Ask me first about anything that needs money, anything near the children’s area, and how contact details are kept, because those follow the church’s procedures. Let’s meet in four weeks to see how it’s going.”

He does it differently from her. He moves the welcome desk inside the entrance and adds name stickers. She would not have done that. But it is his to decide, and nothing unsafe is at stake, so she lets it.

Four weeks later, they meet. She asks, “What’s working, and what did you learn?” Some of it worked. Some of it did not. Both of them learned something.

18 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. If you left in three months, what would fall over? And who would pick it up?

The other four ask you to name one faithful person in your ministry, to think about who needs someone to take a risk on them, to be honest about how you would feel if the person you trained became better than you, and to name the part of your role you would least like to hand over.

19 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action.

Identify a possible person. If you need agreement from your pastor or ministry head, seek it first. Then invite them, without pressure, and give them time to decide. Do not promise a role that is not yours to give.

If they accept, agree an appropriate first responsibility with real decisions attached. Agree what they may decide, when they must ask, and when you will meet to talk it through. And in low-risk matters, when they do it differently from you, let them.

If you cannot go ahead yet, because approval is pending, or no one is ready, or the person says no, that is fine. Write down your next preparation or approval step.

20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “Entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also.” And John’s words about Christ, “He must increase, but I must decrease.”

What you have received was never yours to keep. It was given to you to pass on.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord Jesus, everything I have received came from you. Give me grace to be faithful, and eyes to see the faithful people around me. Make me a Barnabas: quick to vouch, willing to go and fetch, and glad to make room for others. Free me from the fear of being replaceable. Help me to pass on what you entrusted to me, whole and unspoiled. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
