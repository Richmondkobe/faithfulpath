Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Helping volunteers stay and grow

Welcome to Lesson 5. In Lesson 4 we learned how to invite someone to serve: one person, one specific role, the whole picture, and real freedom to say no.

Today we ask what happens next. Why do good volunteers so often quietly fade, and what helps them stay and grow?

2 · Recruiting is the easy part

It happens in many churches. People say yes, serve for a few months, drift to the back of the rota, and then quietly stop. Nobody has a conversation about it. The leader concludes that the church is uncommitted, and starts asking for volunteers again.

So before the next appeal, it is worth asking what serving on the team has actually been like.

People leave for many good reasons: work, health, caring responsibilities, a move, or a change of calling. But three problems can be prevented. People never knew exactly what was expected of them. Nobody thanked them in a way that landed, so they could not tell whether they made any difference. And they stopped growing: year three looked exactly like year one.

You can do something about all three. And Paul gives us the foundation.

3 · “To equip the saints for the work of ministry”

Our passage is from Ephesians chapter 4, verses 11 to 16.

“And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ,

until we all attain to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ, so that we may no longer be children, tossed to and fro by the waves and carried about by every wind of doctrine, by human cunning, by craftiness in deceitful schemes.

Rather, speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ, from whom the whole body, joined and held together by every joint with which it is equipped, when each part is working properly, makes the body grow so that it builds itself up in love.“

4 · The gifts of the risen Christ

Paul wrote this letter to believers in Ephesus, a great port city, and probably to churches in the surrounding area too. The first half of chapter 4 calls them to unity. There is “one body and one Spirit,” he says, and every believer has been given grace by Christ.

Then Paul says that the risen, ascended Christ “gave” particular people to his church: apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers.

Christians differ about how these ministries continue in the church today. But Paul is clear about their purpose: to equip God’s people for ministry. A volunteer team leader is not an apostle, and this lesson does not suggest you are. But look at verse 12. These leaders are given “to equip the saints for the work of ministry.” The work of ministry belongs to God’s people. The leaders’ work is to prepare them for it. That purpose applies, in a smaller way, to everyone who leads others in the church.

5 · To equip

Now look closely at that word, “to equip.” In Greek it is katartismos. This is the only time the noun appears in the New Testament. It describes the work of making something ready for use: preparing it, putting it in order, fitting it out.

The related verb helps us hear it. In Mark 1, James and John are “in their boat mending the nets.” And in Galatians 6, Paul tells the church to “restore” someone who has fallen, “in a spirit of gentleness.” The same family of words covers preparing, mending and restoring.

Those pictures help us hear the word family. They do not mean that Ephesians 4:12 is mainly about repairing damaged people. In Ephesians 4, equipping is more than handing out jobs. It is the patient work of making people ready for ministry. And the aim is in verse 16: a body that grows “when each part is working properly.” People are not tools for getting the work done. The work is one of the ways God grows his people.

6 · Three things to notice

Three things are worth noticing.

First, the work of ministry belongs to the whole body. Paul pictures every part contributing. If one person does everything, the body does not grow.

Second, equipping means patient preparation. Training is part of leading, and it takes time.

And third, the aim is growth in love, not only tasks done. Paul says the body “builds itself up in love.” A team that gets the work done while its people quietly wear out has missed the point.

7 · Jesus sent them, then listened

Now think of Jesus. Mark tells us that “he called the twelve and began to send them out two by two.” He did not send them alone. Matthew 10 records the instructions he gave first: where to go, what to say, what to take, and what to do when a town would not receive them.

Then Mark records what happened when they came back. “The apostles returned to Jesus and told him all that they had done and taught.” And Jesus said, “Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while.”

Luke shows the same shape with the seventy-two. They come back full of joy, saying, “Lord, even the demons are subject to us in your name!” Jesus does not dismiss their joy. But he redirects it: “do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.”

This sending was unique. But the shape teaches us: prepare people, send them out together, then listen when they return.

8 · Point 1: Prepare, send, and listen

So our first point: prepare, send, and listen.

Many of us send volunteers out with a vague description and never ask how it went. Then we wonder why they fade.

Prepare people by saying exactly what is expected. Many volunteers live with a low hum of feeling they might be doing it wrong, because nobody ever told them what doing it right looks like.

Send them with someone where you can, especially at the start.

And listen afterwards. Ask how it went. Then ask the second question, the one that is easy to forget: “And how are you finding it?” Like Jesus, listen to the person, not only to the results.

9 · Point 2: The four training steps

Our second point is a sequence that actually passes on a skill. Ephesians 4 tells us that leaders equip. Here is one practical way to do it, in four steps.

Step one: I do it, you watch. Explain what you are doing as you go, including the small judgement calls.

Step two: we do it together. They take part, and you are beside them.

Step three: you do it, I watch. They lead, and you say nothing unless something is going badly wrong, where appropriate for the role. Then give feedback afterwards, privately.

Step four: you do it, I’m available. You step out. You are reachable, but you are not standing there.

Most leaders jump from step one to step four and call it delegation. The person struggles, the leader takes the job back, and both conclude the volunteer was not up to it. Skipping the middle steps is one of the most common reasons delegation fails.

One important qualification. For roles involving safeguarding, finances or confidential information, follow your church’s supervision requirements throughout. These four steps never replace required checks or oversight.

10 · Point 3: Delegate the outcome, not only the task

Our third point: delegate the outcome, not only the task.

There is a difference between “Put out forty chairs in six rows,” and “Make sure the room is ready and feels welcoming by half past nine. Here is what that is meant to achieve.”

The first produces someone who puts out chairs. The second produces someone who notices the room is cold, and deals with it if that is part of their role, or tells the right person if it is not.

Go back to Jethro in Lesson 4. Moses’ helpers were to decide the small matters themselves. Judgement was shared, not just labour.

So when you delegate, agree the outcome, the limits and the standard first. People use their judgement within the responsibilities they have been given. Then allow a different method when it meets those requirements. If something needs correcting, explain why and help the person learn. Safety, accuracy and the responsible handling of money are never optional standards.

11 · Point 4: Say thank you like you mean something specific

Our fourth point: say thank you like you mean something specific.

In 1 Thessalonians 5, Paul asks the believers “to respect those who labor among you,” and “to esteem them very highly in love because of their work.” Paul is speaking there of those who lead and care for the church. But the principle reaches further: work done for Christ’s people deserves to be noticed.

General thanks has its place. “Thanks for all you do.” But specific thanks helps people see the difference they have made. “I noticed you stayed after the service until the newcomer had someone to talk to. That’s exactly why I asked you to do this.” Name the person, name the action, and name why it mattered.

Do it privately and in person as your default. Do it publicly sometimes, but be careful not to praise the same three people every time. Where appropriate, and if the volunteer is comfortable with it, let their family know their contribution is valued. Never share personal details or ministry situations without permission.

12 · Watch for weariness

Paul writes in Galatians 6, “And let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up.” The word for “grow weary” can carry the sense of losing heart, not only of being tired.

Encouragement matters. But it should go alongside a manageable load, not make up for an impossible one.

Exodus 17 gives us a picture. During the battle with Amalek, “Moses’ hands grew weary, so they took a stone and put it under him, and he sat on it, while Aaron and Hur held up his hands, one on one side, and the other on the other side. So his hands were steady until the going down of the sun.”

Somebody has to hold up the arms of the person holding up the work. On your team, that is part of your role. And who holds up your arms is part of Lesson 9.

Practically, ask what else each volunteer is carrying. If someone is caring for a dying parent, offer a lighter load or a break. Do not assume they will ask. And do not make the decision for them without a conversation.

13 · An honest way to continue, change or stop

Every role should have a review date. In Lesson 4, our example was six months; use whatever your church agrees for the role. At the review, ask three questions and mean all three. What part of this do you most enjoy? What part drains you? And do you want to continue, change, or stop?

Make “stop” a genuinely acceptable answer. A volunteer who leaves well, thanked and blessed, tells other people that serving here is a good experience. A volunteer who leaves by disappearing tells them the opposite.

And offer a break if someone wants one. Agree how long it will be and when you will talk again, without promising that a break will solve everything, and without obliging them to return.

14 · When someone isn’t working out

Sometimes a person is in the wrong role. Consider whether a different role would fit better. A capable communicator who struggles at the sound desk may thrive at the door.

Some concerns are different. Concerns about abuse, dishonesty or unsafe conduct need the proper process. Do not simply move a problem to another team.

And never let a problem with reliability or attitude run for months without a direct conversation. It is unkind to the rest of the team. And it is unkind to the person, who deserves to be told rather than quietly frozen out. Lesson 7 is about how to have that conversation.

But hold on to John Mark. In Acts 15, “Barnabas wanted to take with them John called Mark. But Paul thought best not to take with them one who had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia and had not gone with them to the work. And there arose a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other.” Luke does not tell us why Mark had left. But “Barnabas took Mark with him.”

Years later, Paul writes from prison to Timothy: “Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry.”

Barnabas was willing to take Mark with him. And Paul’s later words remind us that an early setback need not be the end of someone’s usefulness in ministry. We will meet Mark again in Lesson 10.

15 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Prepare people, send them out, and listen to them afterwards: to the work and to the person. Pass on skills in four steps, without skipping the middle. Delegate the outcome, with clear limits and standards. And thank people specifically, watch for weariness, and give everyone an honest way to continue, change or stop.

Or, in Paul’s words, this is how the body grows “when each part is working properly.”

16 · Watch it happen: the first two Sundays

Let me show you what this looks like. Do you remember the man who was invited to lead the welcome team in Lesson 4? He took a week, prayed about it, and said yes. The church’s approval and checks were completed. Now his training begins.

On his first Sunday, she says, “Today, just watch, and I’ll tell you what I’m thinking.”

A family comes in, running late, with two small children. She says quietly, “That family is running late, so I won’t keep them talking at the door. I’ll walk them to seats near the back, where the children can get out easily.” Then a man comes in on his own. “He came on his own last month. I’m going to remember his name.”

After the service, they sit down with a cup of tea. She asks, “What did you notice?”

Two weeks later, they do it together. He greets people at the door, and she stays right beside him. After a few minutes she says, “You take the next one,” and steps half a pace back.

17 · Watch it happen: stepping back

The next time, he leads, and she watches from the back of the hall. She says nothing, unless something is going badly wrong. This is the welcome team. In a role with children, money or confidential information, she would follow the church’s supervision requirements throughout.

Afterwards, privately, she gives feedback. “Two things went really well. You noticed the woman who came in on her own, and you found her a seat beside someone friendly. Here’s one thing to try next time: have the notices ready before the doors open.” Then she asks the other question. “And how are you finding it?”

A month later, she steps out. He leads on his own, and she is in the building, available if he needs her. She has given him the outcome, not just the tasks. “The aim is that anyone who comes in on their own is greeted, helped to a seat, and introduced to one person. How you do that is up to you.” And their first-month check-in is in the calendar.

On the way out she stops at the door. “I’ve watched you these last few weeks. I’ve seen you remember people’s names and help them feel welcome. Thank you.”

18 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. Take each person on your team. Could you write, in one sentence, exactly what they think their role is? And would they write the same sentence?

The other four ask when you last listened to how serving is affecting someone, which of the four training steps you skip, whether you have delegated something and then quietly taken it back, and who might be quietly fading.

19 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action.

Think of the volunteer on your team who is most at risk of quietly fading in the next few months. Take them for coffee this week, or arrange a call if you cannot meet, with no agenda about tasks. Ask what they enjoy, what drains them, and what else they are carrying at the moment. Listen more than you speak. Then thank them for one specific thing you have watched them do.

Invite them without pressure. They are free to decline, and they need not disclose personal circumstances. If they raise a concern requiring action, follow the appropriate church process.

If you do not lead a team yet, do the last part. Thank one person who serves alongside you for one specific thing.

That single conversation may do more to help someone stay than anything else in this lesson.

20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. Christ gave leaders “to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ.” And the body grows “when each part is working properly,” so that “it builds itself up in love.”

You are not only getting the work done. You are helping people grow.
