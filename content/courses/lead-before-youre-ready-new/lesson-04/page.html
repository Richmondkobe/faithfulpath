1 · Building a volunteer team

Welcome to Lesson 4. In Part 1 of the course we started with ourselves: our calling, our own walk with God, and where our role begins and ends.

Now we begin Part 2, Learn the Craft. Over the next four lessons we will look at the practical skills of leading: inviting people, keeping them, running meetings, and having honest conversations. And we begin with the people who serve with you. Who should I ask, and how do I ask them well?

And we begin with an announcement.

2 · Why a general announcement may not be enough

Every new leader tries it at least once. You stand up at the front, describe the need, say the ministry is desperate for help, and ask anyone who feels led to see you afterwards.

Sometimes nobody responds. Sometimes someone responds whose gifts or availability do not match the role you had in mind.

A general appeal can leave people unsure whether the role suits them, or what it would really involve. A clear invitation helps. So keep an open, fair route for anyone to say they are interested, and then use the same criteria and the same checks for everyone. Personal invitations can sit alongside that, without turning your team into a circle of favourites.

The Bible gives us leaders who made deliberate choices about who would carry responsibility. And the first is Moses, worn out.

3 · “What you are doing is not good”

Our passage is from Exodus chapter 18. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. We will read verses 13 and 14, then 17 and 18, then 21 and 22.

“The next day Moses sat to judge the people, and the people stood around Moses from morning till evening. When Moses’ father-in-law saw all that he was doing for the people, he said, ‘What is this that you are doing for the people? Why do you sit alone, and all the people stand around you from morning till evening?’”

“Moses’ father-in-law said to him, ‘What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone.’”

“‘Moreover, look for able men from all the people, men who fear God, who are trustworthy and hate a bribe, and place such men over the people as chiefs of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens. And let them judge the people at all times. Every great matter they shall bring to you, but any small matter they shall decide themselves. So it will be easier for you, and they will bear the burden with you.’”

4 · A father-in-law’s visit

To hear this passage rightly, we need to know the setting.

Israel has just come out of Egypt. Moses’ father-in-law, Jethro, the priest of Midian, comes to meet him in the wilderness. Moses tells him everything the Lord has done, and Jethro rejoices and blesses the Lord.

The next day, Jethro watches Moses at work. Moses sits from morning till evening, and the people stand around him all day, waiting. Moses explains why: the people come to him to inquire of God, to settle their disputes, and to learn God’s statutes.

This chapter describes the appointment of judges in Israel’s history, under Moses, at a unique moment. I want to be careful about that. We are not running a nation in the wilderness. But the principle Jethro gives, sharing responsibility with capable and trustworthy people, applies to every leader who is trying to carry too much. If you took Talk Before You Marry, you met Jethro there for a different lesson: how to say a hard thing well. Here he teaches us how to share the load.

5 · Able men

Now look closely at Jethro’s description. “Look for able men.” In Hebrew it is ʾanšê ḥayil. The word ḥayil is a broad one. It can mean strength, capability, or worth, and it is used in many settings, from an army to a person of noble character. In Proverbs 31 the same word describes the “excellent wife.” In this verse it carries the sense of ability, strength or competence. So “able” here means more than available. It means capable.

Then the rest of the verse supplies the moral qualities. People who fear God. People who are trustworthy. And people who hate a bribe. Capability and character together.

6 · Three things to notice

Three things are worth noticing.

First, the criteria are character and capability, not just availability. Not “willing” and not “free on Thursdays.”

Second, they were chosen deliberately. Verse 25 says, “Moses chose able men out of all Israel.” In a church today, we keep a fair route for anyone to say they are interested. But choosing well is still part of leading.

And third, they were given real decisions. Jethro does not suggest that Moses keep all the authority and hand out chores. “Any small matter they shall decide themselves.” Every great matter still came to Moses. So there were limits, as we saw in Lesson 3. But a team that can decide nothing at all is not really a team. It is a set of errands.

7 · Jesus prayed all night, then chose

Then think of Jesus. Luke tells us that before he chose the Twelve, he went out to the mountain to pray, “and all night he continued in prayer to God. And when day came, he called his disciples and chose from them twelve.”

A whole night of prayer before choosing a team. Many of us choose in the corridor after a service.

Mark adds the purpose. Jesus appointed twelve “so that they might be with him and he might send them out to preach.” Being with him came first. The relationship came before the task.

Jesus’ choosing of the apostles was unique. But there is a lesson in it for us. If your only contact with your volunteers is about their tasks, you may be managing a rota without developing a team. Lesson 5 is about the difference.

8 · Point 1: Who to ask

So our first point: who to ask. Look for four things, roughly in this order.

Character. In Acts 6, the church chose people “of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom,” and that was for organising the daily distribution of food. If the early church looked for character for a practical task, so can you.

Faithfulness in something small already. Paul told Timothy to “entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also.” Look for evidence of reliability, and ask about the context. Doing one task well does not prove someone is ready for every role, and leaving a previous ministry does not prove they were unfaithful.

Some evidence of the gift. In 1 Corinthians 12, Paul reminds a divided church that “God arranged the members in the body,” each with a different function, and that the parts that seem weaker are indispensable. So match people to what they are actually good at, not simply to your empty slots.

And teachability. You can train a humble person with average ability, and it is much harder to train someone who believes they already know. But value teachability alongside the competence and suitability the role requires. Some abilities can be developed through training; others must be demonstrated before appointment.

One more thing. Do not recruit only people like yourself. It is the natural instinct, and it produces a team with one set of strengths and one large blind spot.

9 · What the role itself requires

Before we come to how to ask, one important point. Every role in a church has its own suitability and preparation requirements, and your church is responsible for them.

For some roles, a good invitation conversation is most of what is needed. For others, much more is required. If someone will work with children or young people, for example, your church will need references, safeguarding checks, training and supervision before that person begins. An invitation is the start of that process, not a replacement for it.

So find out what your church requires for the role, before you ask. And use the same criteria and the same checks for everyone, however well you know them.

10 · Point 2: How to ask, part one

Our second point: how to ask. The method is unglamorous, and it works.

Ask in person, one at a time. Not by group message, and not from the front. Sit down with them. If you serve remotely, or meeting is not possible, a private video or telephone call works too.

Ask for a specific role, not for help. “We need volunteers” gets very little. “I’d like you to lead the welcome team at the second service” gets an answer.

And say why you asked them. This is the part new leaders skip, and it is often the most powerful sentence in the conversation. “I’ve watched how you talk to people who come in on their own, and I think you’d be very good at this.” People say yes to being seen.

11 · How to ask, part two

Give them the whole picture up front: the tasks, the hours, how long the commitment lasts, the training, and who they will report to. And explain any requirements, references, checks or supervision needed before they begin.

Set a term and a review date. For example: “Would you take this for six months, and then we will both review it?” Use whatever term your church uses for the role. A clear review point makes the commitment easier to understand, and gives both of you an honest chance to continue, adjust or stop.

Let them say no. Say it plainly: “It’s completely fine to say no, and I’d rather have an honest no than a reluctant yes.” Then mean it. A leader who pressures people into serving will spend the next year managing resentment.

And give them time to answer. “Take a week. Pray about it, think about your other commitments, and talk with anyone it affects. Let me know next Sunday.” A considered answer is more useful than one given under pressure.

12 · Nehemiah’s lesson about ownership

Nehemiah chapter 3 can look like a long list of names: the record of who rebuilt which section of Jerusalem’s wall under Nehemiah’s leadership. But notice a phrase that keeps returning. One person after another repaired the wall “opposite his house.” In verse 28 the priests repaired, “each one opposite his own house.”

The chapter names priests, tradespeople and officials taking part, and Nehemiah 6 records that the wall was finished in fifty-two days.

Many worked near their homes. That reminds us to consider where people already have a meaningful connection to the work, rather than simply assigning them to an empty position. So when you invite someone, ask what part of this work they actually care about.

13 · When they say no

Some people will say no. Take it graciously, and take it as information, not rejection.

Sometimes a no is about timing: a new baby, a hard season at work, or an ill parent. Respect it. You might ask whether they would welcome a conversation in the future, rather than assuming you should come back with the same request.

Sometimes the role is simply not a fit. Accept the first clear no. Ask again only if the person welcomes a future conversation, or if their circumstances change and they invite you to come back to it.

And respect their privacy. Do not record guesses about why someone said no, or details of their family or personal circumstances. If a note is needed at all, a simple one is enough: asked, and declined.

14 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Share the load with capable, trustworthy people, and give them real decisions to make. Choose prayerfully, and build relationship before tasks. Know what the role requires, and use the same criteria and checks for everyone. And ask clearly: one person, one specific role, the whole picture, and real freedom to say no.

Or, in Jethro’s words: “So it will be easier for you, and they will bear the burden with you.”

15 · Watch it happen: the invitation

Let me show you what this looks like.

A woman who leads the welcome team asks a man in her church to meet her for coffee. She has watched him on Sunday mornings for months.

She says, “I’d like to explore whether you would be interested in leading the welcome team at the second service, subject to our church’s usual approval and checks. I’ve watched how you talk to people who come in on their own. You notice them, and you make them feel they belong. I think you’d be very good at this.”

Then she gives him the whole picture. “It’s the first and third Sundays of the month, from half past nine to half past eleven. For a first term of six months, and then we’d both review how it’s going. For the first two Sundays you’d serve alongside me, so you can see how it works. You’d report to me, and I report to the pastor. There are our church’s usual checks for anyone who serves, and the pastor approves new team leaders, which I’ll explain.”

Then she says the sentence that matters most. “It’s completely fine to say no. I’d rather have an honest no than a reluctant yes.”

And she gives him time. “Take a week. Pray about it, talk it over at home, and let me know next Sunday.”

He smiles and says, “Thank you for asking. I’ll let you know.”

16 · What made it work

Notice what made it work. One person, asked in person, for a specific role. She told him why she asked him. She gave him the whole picture, with a review date. She made clear it was subject to the church’s approval and checks. She gave him real freedom to say no, and time to answer.

And if he says no, she will thank him, take it as information, and not press him. The conversation itself has still told him that he is seen and valued.

19 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “You are not able to do it alone.” And, “So it will be easier for you, and they will bear the burden with you.”

Moses was never meant to carry that whole burden alone. Where the work is more than one person should carry, invite others to carry it with you.
