Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Called, not qualified

Welcome to Lead Before You’re Ready. I’m Pastor Richmond Kobe. For more than twenty years I have pastored in more than one country and more than one culture, and I have watched a great many good people step into leadership with real love for God and very little practical training.

This course is for you if you are in your first year or two of leading something in a church: a small group, a worship team, a welcome team, a youth ministry, a department. Across ten lessons we will take three parts in order: start with yourself; learn the craft; and face what tests you.

You will see a staircase in every lesson. Each lesson lights one step. Please hear what that picture means. It is not a ladder for climbing above other people. Each step is about growing in service and in responsibility, not in importance.

Here is the line that carries the whole course: you are not required to be ready. You are required to be willing, teachable, and honest about what you do not know. That is where we begin. It is not where we end, because every role also needs character, preparation and support, and we will come to those today.

But we begin with a feeling.

2 · “They’ve made a mistake”

Someone asked you to lead. Maybe it was your pastor. Maybe a ministry head. Maybe nobody asked at all, and the need was simply sitting there until you picked it up. Either way, somebody said the words that changed your Sundays: would you take this on? And you said yes.

And somewhere between saying yes and today, a quiet thought arrived. They’ve made a mistake. I’m not the right person for this.

I want to say something to you before we go one step further. Feeling inadequate does not, by itself, mean you were wrongly chosen. Many leaders in Scripture felt exactly that. Their stories invite us to bring our doubts to God and to seek help, rather than treating confidence as the test of a calling. And the first of them is Moses.

3 · “Who am I?”

Our passage is from the book of Exodus. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Exodus chapter 3, verses 10 to 12. God is speaking to Moses from the burning bush.

“Come, I will send you to Pharaoh that you may bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt.” But Moses said to God, “Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh and bring the children of Israel out of Egypt?” He said, “But I will be with you, and this shall be the sign for you, that I have sent you: when you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain.”

4 · A shepherd in Midian

To hear this passage rightly, we need to know who was standing in front of that bush.

Moses had grown up in Pharaoh’s household. As a young man he once tried to help his people. He killed an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew, the matter became known, and he ran. That story is in Exodus chapter 2. He settled in the land of Midian, married, and worked as a shepherd, keeping the flock of his father-in-law. Stephen, in the book of Acts, says that forty years passed in the wilderness before God appeared to him. And Exodus tells us he was about eighty when he finally stood before Pharaoh.

So this is not a rising young leader full of confidence. This is a man whose one attempt at leading ended in failure and flight, and who had spent decades looking after someone else’s sheep.

5 · Five objections

And Moses does not say yes. He argues for a chapter and a half.

First he asks, “Who am I?” Then he asks what he should say if the people want to know God’s name. Then he insists they will not believe him. Then he protests that he has never been a good speaker. And finally, in chapter 4, verse 13, he says, “Oh, my Lord, please send someone else.”

Five objections. And God answers the first four with patience: a promise, his name, signs, and the reminder that he made the human mouth. It is only at the fifth that the text tells us the anger of the Lord was kindled against Moses.

6 · “I will be with you”

Now look at what God says in reply to the first objection. In Hebrew it is a short phrase: ʾehyeh ʿimmāk. “I will be with you.”

Notice how God begins. He does not start by telling Moses that he is wrong about himself, or that he is really an excellent speaker. Moses questioned his ability. God answered first with the promise of his presence. Later he would also give Moses signs, and his brother Aaron to help him. But the first answer, and the deepest one, was this: I will be with you.

You may notice that two verses later, when God reveals his name, the same Hebrew verb, to be, appears again. That is worth seeing, but it is a different statement in a different setting. In verse 12, God makes a promise of his presence to Moses. In verse 14, God reveals his name. We do not need to join them together to hear the promise. “I will be with you” is enough.

7 · A unique call, and an ordinary one

Why the anger at the fifth? The text tells us that the Lord’s anger was kindled after Moses asked him to send someone else. This suggests that the issue had moved beyond expressing weakness to resisting the assignment God had given him. God had been patient with the earlier questions. And even then, God did not withdraw the call. He provided Moses’ brother Aaron to go with him.

I want to be careful here. Moses’ call at the burning bush was unique in Israel’s history. God spoke to him directly, gave him signs, and sent him to bring a nation out of slavery. Your appointment to lead the welcome team was not that, and it would be foolish to pretend otherwise.

Most of us will never stand before a burning bush. A call to serve in the church usually comes in ordinary ways: a need you can see, a nudge you cannot shake, and the confirmation of people who know you. But the principle we carry from Moses holds. When God calls his people to serve, what he offers is not first a better opinion of ourselves. It is his presence. God’s presence is our foundation, and his help enables us to grow in the responsibilities of our calling.

8 · Point 1: The feeling you’re having is normal

Our first point is simple: the feeling you are having is normal.

The same pattern appears in the prophet Jeremiah. When God called him, Jeremiah objected, “I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth.” The passage does not tell us his exact age. What we do know is the assignment. He was called to speak God’s word in Judah in the decades before Jerusalem fell to Babylon, and it would bring him rejection, imprisonment and danger.

And God’s reply is not a speech about Jeremiah’s hidden potential. It is a redirection: “Do not say, ’I am only a youth’; for to all to whom I send you, you shall go, and whatever I command you, you shall speak. Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you, declares the LORD.” Again, God answers with the promise of his presence.

9 · “They had been with Jesus”

Then think of Peter and John in the book of Acts. They are standing before the Sanhedrin, the trained religious leaders of Jerusalem, and they speak with such boldness that the council is astonished, because they could see these were “uneducated, common men.” They had not been through the formal training of the religious teachers of their day. These were fishermen, standing in front of the theological faculty of their nation.

And the verse ends with the detail that matters: “they recognized that they had been with Jesus.”

Their time with Jesus mattered. Their courage did not depend on elite credentials. Now, please hear this carefully. That does not make training unnecessary. Peter and John had spent considerable time learning directly from Jesus. They lacked the formal religious credentials recognised by the council, but they were not without preparation. Acts 4 reminds us that formal status is not the whole measure of a leader. It is not permission to stop learning.

10 · Point 2: The one thing you cannot borrow

Our second point: there is one thing you cannot borrow.

The apostle Paul was trained under Gamaliel, one of the most respected teachers of his time, and he knew the Scriptures deeply. Yet he writes this to the Corinthian church: “Not that we are sufficient in ourselves to claim anything as coming from us, but our sufficiency is from God, who has made us sufficient to be ministers of a new covenant.” Paul is speaking of his own apostolic ministry. But the principle reaches all of us who serve.

If even Paul knew that his ministry depended on God, then the pressure you are feeling to be enough on your own is a pressure God never put on you. You cannot borrow someone else’s gifting, and you do not need to. What you need is a real, daily, ongoing connection with the One who called you.

That is the subject of the next lesson, and it is not an accident that it comes second.

11 · Point 3: What God asks of a leader

Our third point is what God actually asks of a leader. And here new leaders can easily get the emphasis wrong. We tend to look first for impressive ability. Paul’s instructions give sustained attention to character.

Read 1 Timothy chapter 3, verses 1 to 7, slowly. Paul is writing about overseers in the church, and he describes a person of sound character: faithful, self-controlled, hospitable, gentle, free from greed, and respected even by those outside the church. He also requires the ability to teach, maturity, and responsible care of one’s own household. These are substantial requirements. This is not a call to appoint anyone who happens to be willing.

That passage is about overseers specifically. But it shows us the shape of what matters, and every church should also be clear about the character, training and competence needed for each volunteer role.

Then Paul writes to the church in Corinth, a wealthy, status-conscious port city, and tells them to look at themselves: “not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth.” God chose the weak and the overlooked, so that no one could boast before him.

12 · Willing is a beginning, not a qualification

So let me say clearly what willingness is, and what it is not.

Willingness is a beginning. It opens the door. But willingness on its own does not qualify anyone to lead. The character the role needs, the preparation and training it requires, the competence to do it well, and the supervision of someone you answer to: those are what help you walk through that door well. And some roles, especially work with children and young people, have particular requirements your church must put in place before anyone begins.

So your uncertainty is not your enemy. It can keep you teachable. Let it also show you what you need to learn, and who you need to ask.

13 · Point 4: What “called” does and does not mean

Our fourth point is what “called” does and does not mean. Two cautions before we close.

First, calling is not the same as feeling certain. Many new leaders wait for a supernatural confirmation before they will fully commit. That is not the usual pattern. More often, as we said, a call arrives as a need you can see, a nudge you cannot shake, and the confirmation of people who know you. If your pastor and your church see something in you, take that seriously. God frequently speaks through his people, and not only through our emotions.

14 · Calling is not permission to stay as you are

Second, calling is not permission to stay as you are. God did not wait for Moses to feel capable before sending him. He promised his presence and provided the help Moses needed. And God places great importance on character, and character must continue to grow. But faithful leadership also requires us to develop the skills, judgement and responsibility our particular roles demand. You will need to learn things you do not know yet.

This course exists because leadership has real skills attached to it: running a meeting, recruiting a volunteer, handling a critic. Being called does not download those skills into you. And it is never permission to bypass the training, supervision or safeguarding requirements of the role you have been given.

15 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. The feeling you are having is normal; Moses, Jeremiah and the apostles knew it too. Our sufficiency is from God, not from ourselves. God places great importance on character, and every role also needs its own skills and preparation. And calling is neither the same as feeling certain, nor permission to stay as you are.

Underneath it all is the answer God gave a shepherd in the wilderness: “I will be with you.”

16 · Watch it happen: asking for help

Let me show you what this looks like in practice.

A woman in her first month of leading a home group sits down one evening and does what this lesson will ask of you. She writes three things she does not yet know how to do. First: lead a discussion when people go off topic. Second: know what to do when someone shares something serious. Third: plan the term. The second one surprised her. She had not realised it was worrying her until she wrote it down.

That Sunday, she finds her pastor after the service. She does not apologise for herself, and she does not make a speech. She says, “I’ve written down three things I don’t know how to do yet in this role. Could you help me with one of these?”

Her pastor looks at the list and says, “Let’s start with the second, because it matters most. Can we have thirty minutes on Thursday? I’ll show you what our church does when someone shares something serious, and who in our church to call.”

That is all. One list, one honest question, one appointment. And it sets a pattern. Asking for help in your first month will protect you for years. Waiting until you are drowning sets a very different one.

17 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson. You do not need to answer them now.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. Finish this sentence without softening it: the main reason I feel unqualified to lead is...

The other four take you back to the qualities Paul lists for overseers in 1 Timothy 3, and which of them matter for your own role, to the person who first suggested you might lead, to Moses’ last objection, “send someone else,” and to Peter and John, who had been with Jesus. Be specific. Specific answers help you notice what needs to change.

18 · This week’s action

And here is this week’s action. It is the same thing you just watched.

Write down three things you do not know how to do in this role. Take that list to the person who put you in the position, whether that is your pastor or your ministry head, and ask them to help you with one of the three. If no one formally appointed you, take the list to the pastor, ministry leader or another appropriate person responsible for overseeing your work. Do it this week.

Keep it simple and specific. You are not confessing failure. You are doing what good leaders do from the very beginning: learning from the people God has put around you.

19 · Remember

Remember these two words of Scripture. “But I will be with you.” And, “our sufficiency is from God.” You are not required to be ready. You are required to be willing, teachable, and honest about what you do not know. The God who promised to be with Moses has not abandoned his people. As we serve Christ, we can depend on his grace, seek his wisdom and ask others for the help we need.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord God, you called Moses when he felt unready, and you promised him, “I will be with you.” Thank you that my sufficiency is from you, and not from myself. Where I am afraid, give me courage. Where I am unprepared, make me teachable. Grow in me the character this work needs, and give me the humility to ask for help. Help me to serve the people you have entrusted to me, for your glory and not my own. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
