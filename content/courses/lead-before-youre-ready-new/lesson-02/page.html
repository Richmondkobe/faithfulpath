Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Leading yourself first

Welcome to Lesson 2. In Lesson 1 we heard God’s answer to Moses, “I will be with you,” and Paul’s words, “our sufficiency is from God.” And I said then that what you need most is a real, daily, ongoing connection with the One who called you. Today is about that connection.

The question for this lesson is a searching one: who is tending my own soul while I tend everyone else’s?

And we begin with a vineyard.

2 · The vineyard you forget to tend

There is a line in the Song of Songs that has followed me through twenty years of ministry. The young woman speaking says, “they made me keeper of the vineyards, but my own vineyard I have not kept!”

In its setting, she is explaining why her skin has been darkened by the sun. Her brothers, angry with her, made her work in the family’s vineyards, and she was not able to look after herself. That is what the verse means. It is not about spiritual growth. And yet many leaders read it and go quiet, because, as a picture, it describes exactly what can happen to us. We are put in charge of other people’s growth, and somewhere along the way we stop tending our own.

It can happen within a few months of leading. You notice that you have started reading the Bible only to find something to say. Your prayer life has become planning. You are producing spiritual food for others all the time, and eating almost none of it yourself.

This lesson is here to help prevent that.

3 · Keep a close watch

Our passage is from Paul’s first letter to Timothy. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. 1 Timothy chapter 4, verses 12 to 16.

“Let no one despise you for your youth, but set the believers an example in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity. Until I come, devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture, to exhortation, to teaching. Do not neglect the gift you have, which was given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you. Practice these things, immerse yourself in them, so that all may see your progress. Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching. Persist in this, for by so doing you will save both yourself and your hearers.”

4 · A young leader in Ephesus

To hear this passage rightly, we need to know who Timothy was and where he was.

Paul had left Timothy in the city of Ephesus to deal with people who were teaching things that were not true. Paul says so at the start of the letter. Timothy was younger than many of the people he was called to lead, and some of them may have looked down on him for it. So Paul writes, “Let no one despise you for your youth.”

And notice Paul’s answer to that problem. He does not tell Timothy to assert his authority more loudly. He tells him to “set the believers an example in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity.” Timothy’s life would carry weight that his age could not.

This is Paul’s charge to Timothy, a leader with a particular responsibility in Ephesus. I want to be clear about that. But the heart of it, keeping watch over your own life as well as your teaching, reaches every one of us who leads anything in a church. So we apply it, carefully, to your role.

5 · Keep a close watch on yourself

Now look closely at verse 16. “Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching.” The Greek verb is epeche. It means to pay close attention to something, to hold your attention on it. And Paul uses a present command, which fits the sense of something to keep on doing, day after day, not a single check now and then.

And notice the two things Timothy is to watch. Himself, and the teaching. Both.

6 · Point 1: Life and teaching together

So our first point is this: life and teaching together.

Paul joins them in one sentence. What Timothy teaches matters, and how Timothy lives matters, to the people he serves. Paul is not ranking them, as if teaching came second. He calls Timothy to persevere in both.

A leader’s conduct can support the message he gives, or it can undermine it. People hear what we say through what they see.

And Paul says, “by so doing you will save both yourself and your hearers.” Paul is speaking of persevering in the faith. As Timothy holds fast to the truth in his life and his teaching, he and those who listen to him are kept in the way of salvation. Timothy does not become anyone’s saviour. Christ alone saves. But a leader who drifts can take others with him, and a leader who keeps watch helps others to keep going.

7 · Point 2: Jesus made room to be with the Father

Our second point: Jesus made room to be with the Father.

In Mark chapter 1, Jesus has spent an evening in Capernaum surrounded by need. Mark tells us that at sundown they brought to him all who were sick or oppressed by demons, and “the whole city was gathered together at the door.” He healed many.

Then verse 35: “And rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed.”

8 · Even when the need is real

The need was real. The people were genuinely sick. And when Simon and those who were with him searched for him and found him, they said, “Everyone is looking for you.”

Luke tells us this was not a single occasion. In Luke chapter 5, as the crowds kept gathering to hear him and to be healed, Luke says, “But he would withdraw to desolate places and pray.” That shows us a rhythm of prayer in the middle of real demand. It does not give us a formula for how many hours to spend.

So here is the application. If Jesus, with real and urgent need all around him, deliberately made room to be alone with the Father, then your ministry is not too urgent for it either.

9 · The apostles shared the work

Then think of the apostles in Acts chapter 6. The church in Jerusalem was growing fast, and a complaint arose: the widows of the Greek-speaking believers were being neglected in the daily distribution of food. This was a real problem of fairness, not a trivial grumble.

The twelve said, “It is not right that we should give up preaching the word of God to serve tables.” And they asked the church to choose seven men “of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom” to take responsibility for it. Then they said, “But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word.”

Notice what they did not do. They did not dismiss the problem, and they did not treat serving tables as unimportant; the people chosen for it had to be full of the Spirit and of wisdom. They arranged for suitable people to address it, while protecting their own responsibility for prayer and the word. They shared the work.

You will face a version of this within your first year. A real need will arise, you will be the obvious person, and saying yes will quietly cost you the hours you were using to stay spiritually alive. Learning to say, “This matters, and here is who should carry it,” is a skill we will come back to in Lesson 5.

10 · Point 3: Integrity is mostly private

Our third point: integrity is mostly private.

The heading of Psalm 51 links it to the time when the prophet Nathan confronted King David over Bathsheba and Uriah. And notice where David locates the problem. “Behold, you delight in truth in the inward being, and you teach me wisdom in the secret heart.” And, “Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me.”

He does not ask for his reputation back. He asks God to deal with the inside.

11 · Repentance deals with the inside

The account in 2 Samuel chapter 11 begins with David remaining in Jerusalem while his army was away at war. It then describes how he abused his power over Bathsheba, and arranged the death of her husband Uriah to conceal what he had done. The text does not tell us every private step that led there. But it shows us why repentance must deal with conduct and the harm done to others, not merely with how things look. When a leader has harmed another person, repentance must never be used as a substitute for truth, accountability or appropriate action to address the harm. A prayer for a clean heart does not remove those responsibilities.

Proverbs puts it like this: “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life.” The course of our lives flows from the heart. So attend to the source, not only to what others can see.

12 · After preaching to others

And Paul says something blunter in 1 Corinthians chapter 9. He was writing to a church in a city near the famous Isthmian Games, and he uses the pictures of a runner and a boxer. “But I discipline my body and keep it under control, lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified.”

Christians have understood what Paul meant by “disqualified” in different ways. But this much is clear: Paul took seriously that a person could preach well to others and fail to live it himself. If he took that seriously, so should we.

13 · Point 4: What this looks like on a Tuesday

Our fourth point: what this looks like on a Tuesday. Theology is easy to agree with. Here is the practical version.

Keep some Bible reading for your own growth. Choose a book of the Bible to read without the pressure of turning it into a talk. Notice what you need to receive before you decide what you might teach.

Have a fixed time, not a hopeful intention. “I’ll pray when I get a chance” usually becomes no prayer at all. A modest fixed time that you actually keep beats an ambitious one you abandon by February.

Find a trustworthy person who can ask honest questions. A peer, or a more experienced leader outside your own team, can help you reflect on your conduct, your relationships and your spiritual life. Agree what you will talk about and how confidentiality will work, and meet monthly. Honest accountability does not require handing over your passwords or giving up all personal privacy. And confidentiality has appropriate limits, especially where someone may be at risk of harm.

14 · Rest, and noticing strain

Protect a regular period of genuine rest, and work with those responsible for your ministry to make suitable arrangements for urgent needs. Where possible, take a proper day away from ministry responsibilities. Constant availability may come from pressure, from too few people on the team, or from finding it hard to say no. Whatever the cause, leaders need rest.

And notice strain before it becomes a crisis. Tiredness, cynicism, pulling away from people, or small dishonesties all deserve attention. Fatigue alone is not a moral failure. It is a signal. Ask what is happening, and seek support rather than waiting for things to get worse. We will come back to this in Lesson 9.

You are the instrument God uses here. Keep it tuned.

15 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Keep watch over your life and your teaching; both matter to the people you serve. Make room to be with the Father, as Jesus did, even when the need around you is real. Remember that integrity is mostly private, and that repentance deals with the inside. And put it into practice: a modest fixed time, a trusted person who can ask honest questions, and real rest, with urgent needs covered.

“Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching.”

16 · Watch it happen: a realistic week

Let me show you what this can look like.

A man leads the youth group at his church. He has a full-time job and two young children, so his time is limited. He decides on something modest that he can actually keep.

On Monday, Wednesday and Friday, from half past six to seven in the morning, before the children wake, he reads the Gospel of Mark slowly. He is not preparing a talk. He reads to receive, and he prays about what he reads.

Saturday evening is his evening off. His phone goes away, and his team knows that another leader covers anything urgent.

And on the first Thursday of each month he has coffee with an older friend from another church. They agreed at the start what they would talk about and how confidential it would be. His friend asks him three questions. How is your soul? What are you avoiding? Where are you tired?

That is one example, not a rule. Your week will look different. What matters is that the time is fixed, modest and protected.

17 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. When did you last read the Bible with no intention of using it for anyone else? Be specific about the date if you can.

The other four take you back to Paul’s words about life and teaching, to the apostles in Acts 6, to any habit or influence that may be drawing you away from Christlike character, and to the people who can ask you honest questions.

18 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action.

Put one protected appointment for prayer and personal Bible reading in your calendar this week. Make it modest enough to keep. Then, as a follow-up, contact one of the trustworthy people you thought of, and ask about meeting monthly.

Protect the prayer appointment before you fill your calendar with the next round of ministry tasks.

19 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching.” And, “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life.”

You need to receive from God’s word yourself, not only prepare it for others. Tend your own vineyard.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray.

Lord Jesus, you made room to be alone with the Father, even when everyone was looking for you. Teach me to do the same. Where I have been feeding others and starving myself, feed me again by your word. Create in me a clean heart, and keep me true in private as well as in public. Give me one trustworthy friend, and the humility to be honest with them. In your name. Amen.
