1 · Running a meeting people want to attend

Welcome to Lesson 6. In Lesson 5 we saw how to help volunteers stay and grow: preparing them, listening to them, and thanking them specifically.

Today we look at something that can quietly undo all of that: the team meeting. How do I lead a meeting that respects people’s time and moves the work forward?

2 · Volunteers do not resent work; they resent wasted evenings

A volunteer will often give you a Saturday morning cheerfully. What can gradually wear them down is ninety minutes on a weeknight that achieves nothing they could name on the drive home.

Poor meetings are one of the reasons good people go cold on a ministry. And new leaders often create them with the best intentions. We call everyone together, because togetherness feels like leadership. Then we fill the time, because we called it.

Some meetings are for decisions. Others are for learning, prayer or care. Each one needs a clear purpose, rather than an evening filled by habit.

The early church faced a far greater question than any of ours. And the way they met to address it is worth watching closely.

3 · “It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us”

Our passage is from Acts chapter 15. We will read selected excerpts from across the chapter, and I will give the verse numbers as we go.

Verses 6 and 7. “The apostles and the elders were gathered together to consider this matter. And after there had been much debate, Peter stood up and said to them, ‘Brothers, you know that in the early days God made a choice among you, that by my mouth the Gentiles should hear the word of the gospel and believe.’”

Verse 12. “And all the assembly fell silent, and they listened to Barnabas and Paul as they related what signs and wonders God had done through them among the Gentiles.”

From verse 19, James began his judgement with these words: “Therefore my judgment is that we should not trouble those of the Gentiles who turn to God.” His sentence continues in the next verse.

Verse 22. “Then it seemed good to the apostles and the elders, with the whole church, to choose men from among them and send them to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas.”

From their letter, verse 28. “For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to lay on you no greater burden than these requirements.” The letter goes on to name them.

Verses 30 and 31. “So when they were sent off, they went down to Antioch, and having gathered the congregation together, they delivered the letter. And when they had read it, they rejoiced because of its encouragement.”

4 · A question about the gospel

To hear this passage rightly, we need its setting.

Some men came down from Judea to the church in Antioch, teaching that Gentile believers had to be circumcised and keep the law of Moses in order to be saved. Luke says that Paul and Barnabas “had no small dissension and debate with them,” and that Paul, Barnabas and some others “were appointed to go up to Jerusalem to the apostles and the elders about this question.”

This was not a dispute about the rota or the music. It was about the gospel itself: how a person is saved, and whether Jewish and Gentile believers belong to one church.

This was a unique moment. The decision carried the authority of the apostles, and it settled a question of the gospel for the whole church. Your team meeting is not the Jerusalem council, and I want to be careful about that. But the shape of their process is still worth learning from.

5 · It seemed good

Notice one word that keeps returning. “It seemed good.” In Greek it is edoxen. It appears three times in this passage, in verses 22, 25 and 28.

It was a common word for reaching a considered judgement, the kind of language used when a group made and announced a decision. It is not a mysterious word. But look at how the letter uses it: “For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us.”

The council listened to testimony, considered the Scriptures, debated the matter and reached a judgement. In their letter, they described that judgement as having seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to them. This was a unique apostolic decision, not a pattern for claiming divine authority over our own preferences.

But one habit is worth keeping: seek God, listen carefully, reach a clear judgement, and tell people plainly what has been decided.

6 · Five things the council did

Watch the structure of their meeting.

One clear question. They “were gathered together to consider this matter,” this one matter.

Evidence from people who had been there. Peter spoke from his own experience. Barnabas and Paul reported what God had done among the Gentiles, and the assembly listened in silence.

Genuine debate. There was “much debate,” and it was allowed.

A stated decision. James gave his judgement, tied to Scripture. Then it seemed good to the apostles and the elders, with the whole church, to send men with a letter.

And a written message to those affected. They wrote a letter, chose people to carry it, and explained it in person. And the church in Antioch “rejoiced because of its encouragement.”

These are five features we can observe in this account, not five rules every church meeting must follow. And not every decision in a church today is made by everyone present. But many church meetings struggle with the first of those, and the last two. No clear question. No clear decision. And nobody told.

7 · Acts 6, again — for the process

We met Acts 6 in Lesson 2, when we looked at how the apostles shared responsibility. Now look at the process.

A complaint arises: some widows are being overlooked in the daily distribution. The Twelve summon the disciples, state the problem plainly, and propose a solution. They define the criteria: “seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom.” And they ask the group to choose. Luke says, “what they said pleased the whole gathering.”

A problem, a proposal, clear criteria, and the people affected involved in the choice.

Six verses do not tell us how many hours that took. The lesson is not speed at any cost. It is clarity about who is responsible, and following through.

8 · Nehemiah walked the walls first

Nehemiah gives us another short, effective meeting.

He had arrived in Jerusalem, and before he said a word to anyone, he went out at night. “Then I arose in the night, I and a few men with me,” he writes. “I went up in the night by the valley and inspected the wall.”

Only then did he gather the people. “Then I said to them, ‘You see the trouble we are in, how Jerusalem lies in ruins with its gates burned. Come, let us build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision.’” And he told them of “the hand of my God that had been upon me for good,” and what the king had said.

Their answer: “Let us rise up and build.”

The problem, named honestly. The action, stated clearly. The reason for hope. And notice that Nehemiah had walked the walls himself first. He came knowing what he was asking people to do. His own inspection did not replace their insight; it prepared him to involve them well.

9 · Point 1: Do the thinking before, not during

So our first point: do the thinking before the meeting, not during it.

Proverbs says, “Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed.” That is an argument for consulting people. It is not an argument for doing all your thinking out loud in a room of twelve.

The single biggest improvement many leaders can make is to arrive with a proposal, not a blank page. Write down, before the meeting, what you think should happen and why. Then present it as a proposal you genuinely want tested. “Here’s what I’m thinking, and here’s why. Tell me what I’ve got wrong.”

People engage far better with something concrete. “What should we do about the welcome team?” often produces silence. “I think we should move to two teams alternating fortnightly. What’s wrong with that?” produces useful discussion in a few minutes.

This is not deciding in advance. If nobody can change your mind, you did not need the meeting.

10 · Point 2: Order is a spiritual concern

Our second point: order is a spiritual concern.

In 1 Corinthians 14, Paul is writing about Corinthian gatherings for worship that had become disorderly, with people speaking over one another. He says, “Let all things be done for building up.” He gives his reason: “For God is not a God of confusion but of peace.” And he ends, “But all things should be done decently and in order.”

Paul was writing about worship, not about a planning meeting. But the principle he gives applies more widely. Order is not the enemy of the Spirit. Disorder can stop people from being built up.

Applied to your team meeting, starting on time and finishing on time is a pastoral act, not mere administration. Finishing when you said you would tells a mother of three that you respect her babysitter arrangement. It is one of the clearest ways you can show that you value people.

11 · Point 3: One page — before, during, after

Our third point is the format. One page, sent at least two days ahead.

Before the meeting: state the purpose in one sentence. Give each item a time allowance, and label it decide, discuss or inform. Send any reading in advance. And move routine information to a message, where that will work for your team.

During the meeting: start on time. Pray meaningfully, not as a formality. Take the most demanding item while people are fresh. Invite different perspectives. And record each decision, who owns it, and by when. If an item needs more time, agree a follow-up, rather than assuming everyone can stay.

After the meeting: send a brief record of the decisions, owners and dates, with any context people need. Keep confidential matters in the appropriate restricted record, not in the general notes. And check progress before the next meeting.

That last habit, checking in between meetings, stops your next meeting becoming a review of everything that did not happen.

12 · Point 4: Make room for every voice

Our fourth point: make room for every voice. There are two people you will need to help.

The one who dominates may be the most experienced person there. Handle it through the structure rather than personally: “I’d like to hear from someone who hasn’t spoken yet.” If it continues, have a private conversation. Thank them for their contribution, and ask for their help in drawing out the quieter members. Inviting their help may improve the discussion.

The one who rarely speaks may be reflecting, uncertain, feeling left out, or more comfortable contributing in writing. Invite their view without putting them on the spot: “Grace, would you like to add anything, now or after the meeting?” Then leave time for a reply.

Silence may mean someone is thinking. It may also mean uncertainty or discomfort. Make room for different ways to contribute.

13 · How often?

How often should you meet? As often as the work and the people need. Decisions, coordination, prayer, training and care can all be good reasons to meet. Habit alone is a poor one. A meeting with no decision on the agenda may still be needed for training, coordination or prayer.

If your team meets monthly out of habit, and half those meetings have nothing to decide and no other clear purpose, consider meeting less often for planning and handling the rest by message, where your church allows. Then use the time you have freed for what your volunteers often want more of: someone taking an interest in them personally.

14 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Do the thinking before the meeting, and arrive with a proposal you genuinely want tested. Remember that order is a way of caring for people: start on time and finish on time. Put it on one page: a clear purpose, timed items, and a record of decisions, owners and dates. And make room for every voice.

Then, like the church in Antioch, people may leave encouraged. “They rejoiced because of its encouragement.”

15 · Watch it happen: the one-page agenda

Let me show you what this looks like. Do you remember the man who leads the worship team, from Lesson 3? His team meets once a month. This month there is one real decision to make: how to cover Sundays over the summer, when several people will be away.

On Monday evening he writes a one-page agenda. At the top, the purpose, in one sentence: “To agree how we will cover Sundays over the summer, and to pray for one another.”

Then the items, each with a time and a label. Half past seven: welcome and prayer, ten minutes. Twenty to eight: the summer rota, to decide, twenty minutes. Here’s my proposal: two smaller teams on alternate Sundays in July and August. Eight o’clock: songs for the autumn, to discuss, fifteen minutes. A quarter past eight: praying for each other. And we finish at half past eight.

He notices one more item: the sound-desk training date. That is information only. So he moves it to a message.

Before he sends it, he checks with his pastor that the team is authorised to agree the summer rota. If the change needed approval, the meeting would agree a recommendation instead. The pastor confirms that the team can decide.

He sends the agenda on Tuesday, two days before the meeting.

16 · Watch it happen: the meeting and the record

On Thursday he starts on time, and opens in prayer. When they reach the rota, he says, “Here’s what I’m thinking, and here’s why. Tell me what I’ve got wrong.”

One of the singers points out that two people on the same team are married and will be away together. They adjust the teams. Then he notices that Grace has not spoken. “Grace, would you like to add anything, now or after the meeting?” She says the plan works for her, and offers to lead one of the two teams.

“We agree, then,” he says. “Two teams on alternate Sundays in July and August. Daniel will draft the rota by the twentieth of June.”

The songs discussion is running long. So he says, “Let’s carry the songs to next month, so that we still have time to pray for each other.” They spend the last fifteen minutes praying for one another, and they finish at half past eight.

The next morning he sends a short action record. Use two alternating teams in July and August: he will tell the church office before the rota is published. Draft the revised rota: Daniel, by the twentieth of June. Carry the autumn song choices to the next meeting: he will put them on the agenda. And one line: “Thank you for a good meeting.”

17 · Reflect

On the lesson page you will find five questions to reflect on. Answer them privately, at your own pace, after the lesson.

Here is the first, to start you thinking. Think of your last team meeting. Write down what was decided, who owns each item, and by when. If you cannot, that is your answer.

The other four ask which items could be a message instead, whether you arrive with a proposal or an open question, what you need to go and see for yourself, and who has spoken least in your recent meetings.

18 · This week’s action

Here is this week’s action.

This week, prepare a one-page agenda for your next meeting: one clear purpose, timed items labelled decide, discuss or inform, and a space for decisions, owners and dates. Move routine information to a message where you can. Keep anything that needs explanation, questions or shared understanding.

When that meeting is scheduled, send the agenda at least two days beforehand where practical. And finish at the agreed time, even if you have to carry an item.

If you do not lead a meeting yet, write a one-page agenda for one you attend, as practice, and offer it to the person who leads it if that would help.

Use the Meeting Agenda and Action Record sheet on the lesson page.

19 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “But all things should be done decently and in order.” And, “When they had read it, they rejoiced because of its encouragement.”

A well-run meeting is not about control. It is a way of loving the people who give you their time.
