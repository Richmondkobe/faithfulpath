## In this lesson, you will learn to:

- Notice how money can touch trust, stewardship, generosity and shame.
- Share your real financial picture honestly, including anything that materially affects your shared life.
- Agree one financial rule and name one matter still to check.
- Ask whether any important financial information still needs to be disclosed.

## Take one step

**Prepare, then hold your Money conversation.**

First, alone (about 30 minutes), each of you completes the Money Snapshot from the worksheet, using real figures, not comfortable approximations. Mark any estimate clearly. Do not write passwords or full account numbers on it.

Then set aside 45 to 60 minutes when neither of you is tired. Have the rules you agreed in Lesson 2 in front of you. Exchange the figures that materially affect your shared life, read both lists in silence, and discuss the areas in the worksheet.

Three things are needed from this conversation:

- Disclose and clarify the financial facts.
- Agree one financial rule today.
- Record one matter that still needs checking, and the other matters you need to revisit.

You do not have to settle everything in one evening. The First Household Budget is optional follow-up work for another time.

Your inventory from Lesson 3 is yours. Keep it beside you as a reference. You are not asked to hand over those pages, but you are asked to disclose debts, commitments and risks that materially affect your shared life.

You may stop after preparing and return another day. Mark this lesson complete after you have held the conversation and recorded one rule and one matter still to check.

## If it helps, tell someone

If you would like, you might share what you are learning with a trusted pastor or a mentor couple. You do not have to share your figures, private details or anything you are not ready to discuss.

## Let it settle

- What does money make me feel that I have not told anyone?
- Lord, teach us to be honest with what we have and owe.

## Need more support?

If money is being used to control, threaten or trap you, that is a safety matter, not a budgeting one. If you feel pressured to transfer money, take on debt or give up access to your own funds, pause the joint exercise, seek independent advice, and visit Safety and Support. If this conversation feels too heavy to carry alone, you may also speak with a trusted pastor, mentor or qualified professional.

## Transcript

Welcome to Lesson 6. From here on, we walk through nine rooms: the subjects that shape daily married life. We begin with money.

Let’s start with Scripture. Proverbs chapter 27, verses 23 and 24.

“Know well the condition of your flocks, and give attention to your herds, for riches do not last forever; and does a crown endure to all generations?”

This is a proverb from a farming world, where a household’s livelihood was kept in its flocks and herds. The advice is very practical: know well the condition of what you have. Give it your attention. Do not assume.

And notice the reason. “Riches do not last forever; and does a crown endure to all generations?” Nothing material is permanent, not even a crown. So a wise person does not drift along assuming all is well. The wise person looks.

Notice, too, what the proverb does not say. It does not say, “Become wealthy.” It says, “Know.” The wisdom here is attention and honesty about what is actually there.

Jesus makes a similar ordinary point in Luke 14:28, though his subject is different. He is speaking to crowds about the cost of following him, not about household budgets. He uses the picture of a man building a tower, who first sits down and counts the cost, to see whether he has enough to finish. We borrow only the simple wisdom in the picture, and we leave the passage’s own meaning intact: honest counting should come before costly commitment.

Here is the truth this lesson stands on. Faithful stewardship begins with knowing what you actually have and what you actually owe. And engagement is the season for counting.

Money is one of the places where this matters most, and it is rarely only about money. Notice how it can touch trust, because you have to be honest about what you have and owe. It can touch stewardship, because what we have has been entrusted to us. It can touch generosity, because what we give shows something of what we value. And it can touch shame, which is why so many people avoid it.

So the principle for this room is: marriage requires financial transparency before it requires financial agreement. You do not need identical habits. You do need to know what you are marrying into.

Here is what usually goes wrong.

One partner discovers significant debt that the other never mentioned. The conversation begins: “Why didn’t you tell me?” And the answer comes: “Because I knew you’d react like this.” “So you’ve been hiding things from me?”

Notice that the question “Why didn’t you tell me?” is legitimate. Ask it without humiliation. But do not use fear of the reaction to excuse concealment. You may even need two conversations, one about the facts and one about the breach of trust.

Three other failures are common.

The first is talking about income but not behaviour. They know each other’s salaries. They have never talked about spending habits, debt, what each sends to family, giving, saving, or what counts as a reasonable amount to spend without asking. So they say “we’ll be fine” while picturing two completely different lifestyles.

The second is about privacy and disclosure. Each of you keeps your financial independence before marriage. But debts, commitments and risks that materially affect your shared life need to be disclosed. Transparency does not require surrendering passwords, merging accounts during your engagement, or accepting surveillance. A demand for control is not the same as a request for honesty.

The third is income used as authority. The higher earner begins to think, without ever saying it, “I pay for most of this, so I should have more say.” The lower earner becomes defensive, or dependent, or quietly ashamed. And now money has become about worth and control.

> Pause here. What does money make you feel that you have not told anyone? Take a moment before you go on.

So what do couples who do this well actually do?

They disclose everything that materially affects the life they are considering building together, before they negotiate anything. Real numbers, not vague reassurance. Income. All debt. Savings. Fixed monthly costs. What they send to family. What they give. What they hope to buy or build. They do not wait until after the wedding for one of them to discover that the other sends a substantial amount home every month.

They discuss habits without shaming. Not “you’re terrible with money,” but “what do you normally spend money on, and what does spending it mean to you?” That second question opens something the first one closes. One may save because money means safety: “I grew up with very little, and savings are the only thing that makes me feel secure.” The other spends on experiences because “money is for making memories with the people I love.” Those meanings help explain the disagreement. You still need to check what is affordable.

They turn good intentions into clear agreements. Not “we’ll be careful,” but something specific, with a number attached.

And they are willing to say, “There’s something about my finances I’m embarrassed about, and you need to know it before we marry.” That sentence costs something. It is worth more than every impressive thing you could say about your money.

When someone says it to you, you might answer: “Thank you for telling me. I need to understand the facts, and what this means for trust, before we decide our next step.” You are allowed to be hurt. You are allowed to ask questions and take time. And a single imperfect response does not decide whether honesty will ever be possible again.

When you find a real difference, ask what kind it is. Different habits, one saves and one spends, are workable, with specific rules that you both keep. Different obligations, where one of you supports family and the other does not, is one of the most common serious differences, and it is rarely just about money. It is about duty, culture and what a good son or daughter owes. Do not settle it in one evening. Do name the actual figure and the actual expectation, because the vague version is what causes the damage. There is no single arrangement that suits every couple or every culture. And significant undisclosed debt is more than a difference in financial habits. It changes the information on which you are making your decision, and it may also raise questions about trust. You need to decide together what to do with it. That may take weeks. Take the weeks.

One more word, and it is a serious one. If money is being used to control, threaten or trap someone, that is not a budgeting problem. If either of you feels pressured to transfer money, take on debt or give up access to your own funds, pause the exercise and seek independent advice. When you review documents together, cover sensitive numbers such as account numbers and passwords.

### The conversation (45 to 60 minutes, together)

Work through these in order. Both of you answer each one before moving on. Stop earlier if you need to, record the next question and agree when to continue. Review your Lesson 2 rules first.

**Part one: the facts.** Exchange your Money Snapshots. Read both in silence before either of you speaks.

**Part two: the meaning.**

- What did money feel like in the house I grew up in: plentiful, tight, tense, never discussed?
- What does spending money mean to me? What does saving it mean to me?
- What is the thing I would find hardest to give up if money were short?
- When have I felt ashamed about money?

**Part three: the areas.** Discuss these. Agree one rule today and record the other matters you need to revisit.

- Joint accounts, separate, or both? Which bills come from where?
- What amount can either of us spend without consulting the other? Name the figure.
- What counts as a major purchase in this marriage?
- What do we give, to whom, and who decides?
- What financial obligation do we each have to our families, and what is the limit?
- What are we saving for, and how much a month?
- Who does the admin (pays bills, tracks accounts), and how does the other stay informed?

**Part four: the hard question.** Each of you answers out loud: *Is there anything about my finances I have not told you?* Then wait. Do not fill the silence.

If something comes out, use what you learned in Lesson 5 to listen and clarify. You may need time to check the facts and consider the effect on trust.
