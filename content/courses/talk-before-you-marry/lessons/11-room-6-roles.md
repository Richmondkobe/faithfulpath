## In this lesson, you will learn to:

- Put theological words such as headship, submission and partnership into ordinary language.
- Compare what each person imagines married life will look like.
- Discuss how tasks and major decisions will be handled.
- Consider whether both people can speak, disagree and be heard.

## Take one step

**Describe an ordinary Tuesday, then compare and talk.**

First, separately (about 20 minutes), each of you writes a description of an ordinary Tuesday five years into your marriage, from morning to night: who does what, who decides what, where each of you is at four in the afternoon. Do not compare notes yet.

Then set aside 45 to 60 minutes when neither of you is tired. Have the rules you agreed in Lesson 2 in front of you. Exchange your two descriptions and read both before either of you speaks. Then work through the three main questions in the worksheet. The other questions are important, and you can continue them over the coming weeks.

Three things are needed from this conversation:

- Compare your two Tuesdays, and notice where they differ.
- Put your words (headship, submission, partnership, leadership) into ordinary language, with an example each.
- Talk about how a major decision would be made if you disagreed, and how either person can raise a serious objection safely.

Bring the passages that shape your conviction, but bring them to understand one another rather than to win. Scripture is not a weapon.

You may stop after preparing and return another day. Mark this lesson complete after you have held the conversation and recorded any important difference that remains unresolved.

## If it helps, tell someone

If you would like, you might share what you are learning with a trusted pastor, ideally one who will help you both think rather than one who will simply confirm whichever of you already agrees with them. You do not have to share your answers, private details or anything you are not ready to discuss.

## Let it settle

- What am I assuming my partner will simply do?
- Lord, teach us mutual care that protects each person’s dignity and voice.

## Need more support?

If “roles” language is being used to silence you, control you or override what you say, do not continue the joint exercise. An arrangement in which one person cannot refuse coercion, raise a concern, follow conscience, seek help or speak honestly without punishment is the thing to stop for, whatever theology it is dressed in. Speak with someone outside the relationship, and visit Safety and Support. If this conversation feels too heavy to carry alone, you may also speak with a trusted pastor, mentor or qualified professional.

Welcome to Lesson 11. This room is about roles. It is where two people can use the same words and picture completely different marriages.

Let’s begin with Scripture. Ephesians chapter 5, verses 21 to 33. We will read the whole passage, because it should be read whole.

“submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ. Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife even as Christ is the head of the church, his body, and is himself its Savior. Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands. Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish. In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies. He who loves his wife loves himself. For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church, because we are members of his body. ‘Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.’ This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church. However, let each one of you love his wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband.”

This is one of the most discussed passages in the Bible, so let’s go carefully.

Start with how it opens. Verse 21 immediately precedes the instructions to wives and husbands: “submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ.” Christians agree that it matters for reading what follows, though they differ over precisely how it shapes the distinct instructions to wives and husbands. The section is about conduct inside a Christian household. It speaks here to wives and husbands, and as the household teaching continues into chapter 6, Paul also addresses children, parents, bondservants and masters. Every person in the household is spoken to.

Now look at what is said to husbands. Paul gives husbands an extended command centred on Christ’s self-giving love: “as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her.” That is a picture of costly love. It is a picture of nourishing and cherishing. The husband’s calling here is to sacrifice, not to command.

And notice the end. “Let each one of you love his wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband.” Love and respect. Both are named, and both are meant to be lived.

I want to be honest about one thing: this passage has been read in different ways by serious Christians. Some hear it as describing a husband’s distinct responsibility for leadership. Others read the opening verse as setting the tone for mutual submission throughout. This lesson will not tell you which to choose. Our task is different, and it is one the passage itself supports.

Here is the truth this lesson stands on. Whatever position you reach about marital roles, this passage permits neither selfish authority nor careless treatment. Husbands are called to Christlike, self-giving love, and wives are addressed as responsible disciples whose response is offered in reverence for Christ. And Scripture is never given to us to silence anyone.

That brings us to a problem the passage cannot solve for you. Two people can agree perfectly on the phrase “biblical roles” and still picture two completely different marriages. So the work of this lesson is to say, in plain words, what you actually mean.

Here is how it usually goes wrong.

The conversation starts practically. Who cooks, who pays the bills, who handles the children, who deals with the car. Then, without anyone noticing, it becomes a different question: who has the final say? That is not the same subject, and the two need to be separated before either can be settled.

And the vocabulary hides the disagreement. One person hears “headship” and understands: the husband carries a particular responsibility for the family, and should lead sacrificially. Another hears: the husband gets the final decision whenever we disagree. Those are not the same thing. One hears “submission” and thinks: I willingly respect and support my husband’s leadership. Another hears: my opinion is welcome, but in the end I do what he decides. And “partnership” is just as slippery. One means complete equality of authority. Another means shared responsibility, while still holding that the husband has a distinct role.

So do not assume that using the same theological vocabulary means you have the same marriage in mind. And ask for an example, every time. “What do I mean by headship? Give an example.” “What do I mean by submission? Give an example.” Give an ordinary-life example for each word, so that you can see whether you mean the same thing.

Then ask the questions that bring it down to earth. Suppose we strongly disagree about moving to another city. What actually happens? Who makes the final decision? What does consultation look like before that decision? Can either person raise a serious objection without fear or punishment? Which decisions require the free agreement of both people? Is either person expected to comply when conscience, safety, bodily autonomy or a major legal or financial commitment is involved? And what happens when the person who usually leads makes a decision that turns out badly? That last question is worth more than most of the theology, because it shows whether the arrangement includes any correction, or whether leadership means never being answerable.

**The three main questions (45 to 60 minutes, together)**

Exchange your Tuesdays and read both before either of you speaks.

1. **The two Tuesdays.** Where do they differ? What surprised us? Which differences matter?
2. **The words.** What do I mean by headship? Give an example. What do I mean by submission? Give an example. What do I mean by partnership? Give an example. What does spiritual leadership mean to me, concretely? Give an ordinary-life example for each word so that you can see whether you mean the same thing.
3. **Decisions and voice.** Suppose we strongly disagree about a major decision, such as moving to another city. Who makes the final decision? What does consultation look like beforehand? Can either person raise a serious objection without fear or punishment? Which decisions require the free agreement of both people? Is either person expected to comply when conscience, safety, bodily autonomy or a major legal or financial commitment is involved? What happens when a decision turns out badly, and how is that raised?

**Important conversations to continue before marriage**

You do not need to finish these today. Choose which you will continue, and when.

- **Different convictions.** Each explains their conviction without attacking the other’s. Which passages or principles matter most to me, and how did I come to hold them? What would this belief actually require of my spouse in everyday married life? Listen using Lesson 5: question first, repeat it back, then respond.
- **What counts as a major decision.** How do we make one when we agree? When we do not? Is there a final decision-maker for some matters, and which decisions need both our free agreements?
- **The test cases.** Work through these as real scenarios. For each: who decides, and what does the other do if they disagree?
  - One of us is offered a job in another city.
  - One of us wants to leave our church.
  - One of us wants to stop working when a child arrives; the other did not expect that.
  - One of us wants to lend a significant sum to a family member.
- **The tasks.** See the optional Household Responsibilities Sheet below.

**If you find a real difference**

- **Different task expectations.** Straightforward. Divide the list deliberately rather than by default, and revisit it when circumstances change, particularly when children arrive, which is when most defaults quietly reassert themselves.
- **Same words, different meanings.** Very common, and usually resolved simply by discovering it. Keep using examples until you are certain you mean the same thing.
- **A genuinely different understanding of authority.** Take this seriously and give it more than one evening.
