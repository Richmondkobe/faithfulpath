## In this lesson, you will learn to:

- Decide what is relevant to share, and what is not.
- See what your history taught you and how the past shows up in the present.
- Disclose relevant truth with grace and wisdom.
- Ask the closing question together, and give an honest answer even if that answer is "I need time."

## Take one step

**Prepare privately, then hold your Past Relationships conversation.**

First, alone (about 20 minutes), answer three questions on paper or in thought: Is there anything in my past that could materially affect this marriage? Is there anything my partner could discover later and reasonably ask why I had not told them? Is there anything I am hoping never comes up? Your written answers remain private. If they reveal information that materially affects the marriage, make a safe plan to disclose that information before proceeding with the wedding. You still control unnecessary details.

Take responsibility for choices you made. Harm done to you is not your sin to confess, and you control the details of that story.

Then set aside 45 to 60 minutes, in a private place, when neither of you is tired or rushed. Have the rules you agreed in Lesson 2 in front of you. Either of you may stop at any time. Work through the three main questions in the worksheet. The other questions are important, and you can continue them over the coming weeks.

Three things are needed from this conversation:

- Share the material information your partner needs in order to decide, or say that you have nothing material to add. You do not owe graphic details.
- Talk about how the past shows up in the present, such as comparison, jealousy or fear.
- Ask the closing question together — with what I now know, am I able to continue discerning this marriage freely and respond without shaming, threatening or retaliating? Do I need more time, information or support before answering? — and record only the next steps you both agree are useful.

Do not force a disclosure in this exercise if doing so could put you at risk. If a joint conversation would not be safe, do not hold it. You may mark this lesson complete after doing the private preparation and making a confidential plan with a qualified support person. Do not proceed towards the wedding while a serious safety concern remains unresolved.

You may stop after preparing and return another day. Otherwise, mark this lesson complete after you have held the conversation and recorded whether any further time, information or support is needed.

## Let it settle

- What relevant truth do I need to speak with grace and wisdom?
- How can God's grace help me be honest without living in shame?

## Need more support?

If a painful experience from your past is stirred by this lesson, you control the pace, and you do not owe anyone a graphic account. Abuse or betrayal was not your fault, and it is not your sin to confess, and you control the details of that story. A qualified counsellor can help, before or after marriage. If you fear how your partner will respond, or telling the truth could put you at risk, do not force a disclosure. Seek confidential support independently, and visit Safety and Support. Neither jealousy nor past hurt justifies surveillance, isolation or control.

Welcome to Lesson 12. This lesson is about your past relationships. You may listen privately, and you may stop at any time.

Let's begin with Scripture. Colossians chapter 3, verses 9 and 10.

"Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator."

Paul is writing to believers, and this is not specifically about romance. It is about how followers of Jesus live with one another. And notice its shape. It begins with a command: "Do not lie to one another." Then it gives the reason: you have "put off the old self with its practices" and "put on the new self."

Paul uses a picture of clothing. Taking off one set of clothes, putting on another. The old way of living is set aside. A new way is put on. And notice the new self "is being renewed." It is a process, not a finished product.

So two things sit side by side in this passage. The command to truthfulness. And the fact of renewal. Grace does not replace honesty. Renewal is what makes honesty possible.

That is the truth this lesson stands on. Grace does not remove the responsibility to tell consequential truth.

The New Testament is clear about grace. In 2 Corinthians 5:17 Paul says that anyone in Christ is a new creation. Your past does not have to be your identity. But hear the limit of it plainly. Becoming new in Christ does not erase consequences, and it does not remove the need for honest disclosure. Forgiveness does not require pretending the past did not happen. It means the facts do not define who you are.

So here are two things, one for each of you.

To the person disclosing choices they made: "God has forgiven me" must never become a reason to withhold information your future spouse reasonably needs in order to marry knowingly. Grace received is not a substitute for honesty given.

And to the one receiving it: do not use a disclosure as a weapon. You may still ask relevant questions, need time, or discuss consequences that remain unresolved. Grace does not require an immediate decision, or pretending that every consequence has already been resolved.

The past becomes present tense very quickly in this room, and it happens in three ways.

The first is comparison. One person discloses something, and the other immediately begins measuring. "Did you love them more than you love me?" "Did you do more for them than for me?" "What did they have that I don't?" The conversation has stopped being about understanding a history. It has become a competition with someone who is not in the room, and who cannot lose.

The second is the fear that the story repeats. Someone hears about a relationship that ended badly and thinks, "What if I'm next?" Then they start gathering evidence. Ordinary lateness becomes suspicious. Every disagreement carries a second question underneath it: is this how it started last time? The partner is no longer being assessed on who they are. They are being assessed partly on what somebody else did.

And the third is quieter: inherited fear in the person who was hurt. "The last person left when it got difficult. This one probably will too." So they become watchful, or controlling, or endlessly in need of reassurance, not because of anything their partner has done, but because of a wound that predates them.

So how much should you tell each other? Two pieces of advice circulate in Christian settings, and neither is adequate. One says, "Tell your partner absolutely everything." The other says, "Your past is under the blood, so don't talk about it." The better principle sits between them. Your partner deserves the information they need to make an informed decision about marrying you. They do not need every private detail of your history.

So the question is not "What can I possibly confess?" It is: what does this person need to know because it materially affects our marriage? Material information includes present legal, financial, parenting or health consequences; ongoing contact or obligations; a current pattern that may affect faithfulness or safety; and facts that directly change the commitments you are asking your partner to make. Here is a helpful test: is there anything material, in that sense, that my partner could discover later that would lead them to say, "Why didn't you tell me before we married?"

Examples of material information include a previous marriage, an existing child, continuing legal or financial obligations, relevant health consequences, ongoing contact, or a current pattern that could affect faithfulness or safety.

But hear a distinction first. There is a difference between actions you chose and harm done to you. Take responsibility for your own choices. But abuse, coercion or betrayal committed against you is not your sin to confess. You control whether, when and how you tell the story of what happened. If it has a present consequence that directly affects your partner's health, safety or shared commitments, make a safe plan to disclose that consequence without being required to narrate the experience. An experience of abuse does not become a debt of detailed disclosure simply because someone else wants to know.

**Important conversations to continue before marriage**

You do not need to finish these today. Choose which you will continue, and when.

- What did my most significant previous relationship teach me about myself, what to expect and what to fear? What pattern do I recognise across more than one relationship?
- What am I afraid will happen again? What do I now expect from a partner because of what happened before?
- Am I still emotionally attached to anyone from my past? Memories do not necessarily mean ongoing romantic attachment. Discuss present behaviour and commitments rather than prescribing what someone ought to feel.
- To the one hearing a disclosure: *What exactly am I afraid of when I hear this?* To the one disclosing: *Am I telling this because my partner needs to know, or because I need them to carry my guilt?*

**If you find a real difference**

- **Significant undisclosed history.** Take days or weeks with it, not one evening. Whoever received it: notice whether your distress is about the fact itself, or about not having been told. Those are different injuries and need different repairs.
- **Persistent comparison.** This is not something the other person can fix by being better. It may point to unresolved fear, grief or insecurity that deserves patient attention, and it is worth working through with someone before the wedding.
- **Jealousy that continues despite reassurance.** Repeated checking can strain a relationship without resolving the fear. Seek qualified support. Neither jealousy nor past hurt justifies surveillance, isolation or control.
- **Ongoing emotional attachment to an ex.** Say it now. It is hard to admit and far harder to discover after a wedding.
- **A demand for total disclosure.** If one of you is insisting on a complete account and cannot say what they would do with it, examine that as its own issue. The demand is often fear wearing the language of honesty, and satisfying it will not satisfy the fear.
