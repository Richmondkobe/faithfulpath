Learn to Say the Hard Thing

How do I raise something difficult so that it can be heard?

About 18 minutesEphesians 4:15Part 2 · Learn the Skills

In this lesson

You will learn

  • What Ephesians 4:15 means by speaking the truth in love
  • How to open with a concern rather than a verdict
  • Three steps for saying what happened: what happened, what I felt, what I needed
  • How to say it once, clearly, and how to decide what to raise and what to let go

Before you begin, together

Take out the subject you chose in Lesson 1 and your rules from Lesson 2. Agree who begins (the other introduces a subject next time), when (an actual evening when neither of you is tired), and how long (stop at forty minutes, resolved or not). If you can, watch Lesson 5 before the conversation itself.

Your three sentences (the one raising the subject)

First step: write one sentence describing what actually happened.

Four tests

  • Does it use “always” or “never” as an exaggeration? Replace it with an accurate description.
  • Does it describe their character anywhere? Rewrite that part.
  • Could they answer it? If there is no answerable question or request, you have written a verdict.
  • Am I saying the whole of what I mean? If you have softened it into something you do not mean, put it back.

3Talk together · The First Real Conversation

If either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

After Lesson 5, if you can. Forty minutes, then about ten minutes to review. Nothing is saved.

  1. The conversation (forty minutes). The one raising it reads the prepared opening, then stops. The one receiving it asks a question first: “Help me understand what that was like for you”, or “When did this start?” Then talk normally. Keep the rules. If either uses the break signal, honour it without argument. Stop at forty minutes.
  2. Afterwards (about ten minutes). Review the conversation, not the subject. What went well in how we did that? Where did it nearly go wrong? Which of our rules was hardest to keep? The one who raised it: Did I say the whole of what I meant, or did I hold something back? (“There’s more to this and I wasn’t ready to say it tonight” is a complete and useful sentence.)
  3. Write it down. The subject we discussed · what we agreed, if anything · what we still need to come back to · the date we’ll come back to it.

If it went badly: an awkward first attempt does not settle the future of your relationship. For an ordinary misstep, use the repair steps from Lesson 2 and agree a return date. If you felt unsafe, stop joint practice and seek independent support.

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · Learn to say the hard thing

Welcome back. This is Lesson 4. In Lesson 1 you each named three subjects you had been steering around, and you chose one to come back to. In Lesson 2 you wrote the page of rules. In Lesson 3 you each filled two notebooks with what you actually expect. The lessons behind you were preparation. This one is technique.

And it starts in a very small place: the sentence you use to open. The question for this lesson is this: how do I raise something difficult so that it can be heard?

Slide 2 · Speaking the truth in love

Our passage is a single verse, Ephesians chapter 4, verse 15. We will read it with the verses on either side.

“so that we may no longer be children, tossed to and fro by the waves and carried about by every wind of doctrine, by human cunning, by craftiness in deceitful schemes. Rather, speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ, from whom the whole body, joined and held together by every joint with which it is equipped, when each part is working properly, makes the body grow so that it builds itself up in love.”

Slide 3 · Growing up together

Paul is describing how a church grows up. Children are easily tossed to and fro, carried by whatever is said most cleverly. The opposite of that is not silence, and not cleverness. It is truth spoken in love. That is how the whole body is joined together and builds itself up in love.

Paul wrote this to a church, not to an engaged couple, and we apply it here to two people preparing to marry. It is the same chapter you met in Lesson 2, where Paul says, “speak the truth with his neighbor.” In Lesson 2 we asked whether our words build. Today we ask how to say a hard truth so that it can do its building.

Slide 4 · The word: speaking the truth

One word in that verse gives this lesson its shape. The Greek word behind “speaking the truth” is alētheuō. It means to speak the truth, or to be truthful. In this verse Paul uses a form of it, alētheuontes. It is related to the ordinary word for truth, and it can describe not only what we say but a way of living: being truthful.

And notice what Paul joins it to. Not truth, and then love. Truth in love, in the same phrase. Truth without love becomes a weapon. Love without truth becomes a hiding place. Paul will not let us choose. In this lesson, neither is left out.

Slide 5 · A soft answer

Proverbs puts it in one line: “A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger.” Soft there is not about saying less. It is about the way something is said. As the book says, you are trying to make it possible for the other person to actually hear it. The content stays. Only the packaging changes. And a gentle answer can reduce unnecessary conflict, but it cannot guarantee how another person will respond.

Slide 6 · Jethro sees the problem

Scripture gives us a picture of a hard thing said well. After Israel left Egypt, Moses sat to judge the people “from morning till evening.” When Moses’ father-in-law, Jethro, saw it, he asked, “What is this that you are doing for the people?” Then he said, “What you are doing is not good. You and the people with you will certainly wear yourselves out, for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to do it alone.”

Slide 7 · The shape of a good hard sentence

Look at the shape of what he did. He saw it. He named it, plainly: this is not good. He said why: you will wear yourselves out. And he offered a way forward: share the work with able men, so that, in his words, “you will be able to endure.” He did not attack Moses’ character. He described what was happening, and what it was doing.

Jethro was a priest of Midian and Moses’ father-in-law. He was advising Moses, not commanding him as Israel’s appointed leader. This is not a marriage passage; we are drawing one practical lesson from how he raised a difficult concern.

Slide 8 · Point 1: The first sentence

Our first point is the first sentence. The difference between an opening that works and one that fails is simple to state and hard to do when you are hurt. An opening that works describes a concern. An opening that fails delivers a verdict.

Take a man who feels his partner has been making significant decisions without him. The verdict version: “You always do whatever you want. You don’t respect me at all.” The concern is legitimate. What does the damage is “always” and “at all”. Now the only way to answer is to prove it false: “That’s not true. Last month I asked you about the car.” The decisions are forgotten.

The version with somewhere to go: “Can we talk about something I’ve been sitting with? When decisions get made without us discussing them first, I feel left out, and I’d like us to find a better way of handling those.”

Slide 9 · Not softer, but possible to hear

A practical rule: avoid beginning with “you are”. Start with “I”, or with “Can we talk about”. But there is nothing magic about the word “I”. “I feel like you’re a selfish person” is an accusation with a costume on. The point is not the grammar. Starting with your own experience makes it harder to slip into describing your partner’s character.

Compare “You never listen to me” with “I don’t feel heard when I try to raise something important.” The second is not softer. It may be harder to hear, because it cannot be answered with a counter-example. But it leaves your partner free to be curious rather than cornered. You are not trying to make the hard truth softer. You are trying to make it possible for the other person to actually hear it. Softening it, saying less than you mean, is Lesson 1 territory, and it will not serve you.

Slide 10 · Point 2: Say what actually happened

Our second point is to say what actually happened. Feelings are real information, but not, on their own, enough to work with. “I feel like you don’t respect me” may be sincere, but it is too large to do anything about. The honest reply is, “What did I do that was disrespectful?” And now you are arguing about whether he is a respectful person, which has no end and no evidence.

So name the behaviour. “When I was explaining my worry about the wedding budget and you looked at your phone and walked out of the room, I felt dismissed.” Now there is something on the table. He might say, “I wasn’t dismissing you. My brother was messaging about the car, and I should have said so.” That may not settle it, but you are both discussing the same thirty seconds. And she can finish: “What I needed was for us to stay with the conversation, or for you to tell me if something urgent had come up.” That is the pattern: what happened, what I felt, what I needed.

Slide 11 · Events, not identities

The related trap is turning one incident into a permanent identity. “You forgot to call me” is specific. “You are unreliable” is a character judgement. “You disagreed with me in front of your parents” is specific. “You always embarrass me” is an attack. The first of each pair can be discussed, apologised for, or explained. The second often invites a defence of someone’s character rather than an honest discussion of what happened.

Being specific has another benefit: you may find out you were wrong. It turns out he was reading a message about his mother’s hospital appointment. That cannot happen if you lead with the conclusion.

Being specific does not mean you need a documented case. Some things are genuinely cumulative, and you may say so, as a pattern rather than a verdict: “Over the last few months, several times when I’ve raised something, the conversation went to your phone. I want to talk about the pattern, not just Tuesday.”

Slide 12 · Point 3: Say it once, clearly

Our third point is to say it once, clearly. One person wants something, is not sure they are entitled to ask, so they hint. A woman wants more of her partner’s weekends. What she could say: “I’d like us to have Saturday evenings together. Can we make that ours?” What she actually says, over three weeks: “You’re going out again?” “You’ve got a lot of friends who need you.” “It’s fine. Do whatever you want.” By the third, she feels ignored. He is thinking, with some justification, that she has never once asked him for anything. Then it surfaces: “You should know what I want without me having to ask.” That sentence is where resentment lives. He cannot win, because he was never told the game had started. Do not make your partner pass a test you never explained.

Slide 13 · Simply “Yes” or “No”

Jesus said, “Let what you say be simply ‘Yes’ or ‘No’; anything more than this comes from evil.” He was speaking about oaths. But the principle helps here: plain speech honours the other person. So say the thing. Instead of “I wish you would help me more”, say, “I need help with the wedding invitations. Can we do them together on Saturday?”

Asking clearly is not demanding. The difference is what follows: “This is what I want. How do you feel about it?” That leaves room for no, or partly. A clear no gives you information, and you may need to understand it. A sexual no must be respected immediately; it is not an invitation to negotiate consent. And “once” means do not wear someone down. Saying it once does not mean you may never revisit an important issue. It means making your request clearly, allowing a genuine answer, and agreeing when to return to an unresolved matter rather than pressuring someone into agreement.

Slide 14 · Point 4: What to raise

Our fourth point is what to raise, and what to let go. It cuts both ways. Not every irritation deserves a conversation, and some very small things are pointing at something that does. Three questions help. Is this important? Is this becoming a pattern? And, if I don’t address it, will I eventually resent it? That third question does most of the work, because something that matters does not stay where you put it.

One shirt left on the floor may be a passing irritation. Let it go, or make a brief, courteous request. But if you have asked several times, and each time it was waved off, the clothes stopped being the subject a while ago. What you would be raising now is whether your requests count for anything. That is a real subject, and worth a proper conversation.

Slide 15 · Not everything, and not buried

The opposite error does just as much damage. Your partner does not reply to one message: “You don’t care about me.” They are ten minutes late: “You are so inconsiderate.” They pick a restaurant you would not have chosen: “You never think about what I want.” Do that consistently, and your partner will experience being near you as being under review. Do not turn every irritation into an indictment. Sometimes love is being able to say to yourself, “That bothered me, and it is not important enough to carry.”

But letting go is not the same as burying. If you say “it’s fine” while keeping a private record of every offence, you have not let it go. You have filed it. A recurring feeling is information. It is not proof that you have failed to forgive.

Slide 16 · Let go, discuss, seek support

A distinction I find useful: let go of the insignificant. Discuss the recurring. Seek support for the harmful. A different preference about dinner tonight: let it go. A pattern of your concerns being brushed aside: discuss it. Dishonesty, hidden debt or financial deception, pressure around sexual boundaries, intimidation, anything that frightens you, anything violent: those do not belong in the letting-go category at all, and no amount of wanting peace makes them belong there.

And one more thing, plainly. This lesson assumes goodwill and freedom to disagree. If you fear retaliation, do not attempt a better opening sentence as your solution. Seek confidential, independent support from someone trained to recognise abuse. For everything else, your goal is not to talk about everything. It is to talk about what matters, before it becomes something larger.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Open with a concern, not a verdict. Say what happened, what you felt, and what you needed. Say it once, clearly, and leave room for the answer. And decide honestly what to let go, what to discuss, and what needs support. That is what it looks like to speak the truth in love. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise: the first real conversation. Take out the subject you chose in Lesson 1, and your rules from Lesson 2. If you can, watch Lesson 5 before the conversation, so the one receiving has the listening skills too. Agree who begins. Choose an actual evening when neither of you is tired. And agree in advance to stop at forty minutes, whether or not it is resolved.

Then, on your own, the person raising the subject writes their first three sentences. The event: what actually happened. The effect: what it did to you, in one sentence. The ask: what you want to be different. Then run four tests. Is there an exaggerated “always” or “never”? Does it describe their character anywhere? Could they answer it? And am I saying the whole of what I mean? If you have softened it into something you do not mean, put it back.

Slide 19 · Talk together

You can complete your private preparation now and return to the joint conversation after Lesson 5. You do not need to do both today. When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: the one raising it reads the opening, and then stops talking. The one receiving it does one thing first: ask a question. Not a defence. “Help me understand what that was like for you.” Then talk, keep the rules, and honour the break signal.

Afterwards, review the conversation, not the subject. What went well? Where did it nearly go wrong? Which rule was hardest to keep? And the one who raised it asks: did I say the whole of what I meant? Then write it down, with a date to come back to it. If it went badly, use the repair steps from Lesson 2. If you felt unsafe, stop joint practice and seek independent support.

Slide 20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “speaking the truth in love.” And, “A soft answer turns away wrath.” Truth and love belong together, because that is how Christ speaks to us.

Slide 21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Lord Jesus, you are full of grace and truth. Give us courage to say what is true, and love to say it well. Guard our first words from becoming verdicts, and our silence from becoming a test. Teach us what to raise, what to let go, and when we need help. Grow us up together into you. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
