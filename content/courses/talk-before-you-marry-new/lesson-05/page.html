3Talk together · The Other Side

If either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

About 60 minutes in all: the conversation itself is limited to forty minutes, then about twenty minutes to say what it requires and to review. Nothing is saved.

  1. The conversation (forty minutes). The one raising it speaks, then stops. The receiver, in this order: 1. Ask a question (“Help me understand what that was like”). 2. Summarise what you heard in your own words and ask whether it is accurate. Allow clarification, without making perfect wording a condition for either person to speak. 3. Only now, respond. If you notice your warning sign, ask another question instead.
  2. Say what it requires (about ten minutes). The receiver answers out loud, one of four: “Here is what I’m going to change, specifically…” · “Here is what I understand, and here is where I disagree…” · “I need time to think about this properly. I’ll come back to it on…” · “Here is what I can do, and here is what I can’t…”
  3. Review together (about ten minutes). What did the receiver do that helped? Where did defensiveness nearly get in? The one who raised it answers honestly: Did you feel heard, or did you feel handled?

You do not have to remain in a conversation that becomes harmful. If you felt unsafe, stop joint practice and seek independent support.

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · Learn to hear the hard thing

Welcome back. This is Lesson 5, the last lesson of Part 2. In Lesson 4 you learned how to raise something hard. This lesson is about the other chair: sitting still while something difficult is said about you. Most people find that the harder side. It is easier to raise a hard thing than to hear one.

So the question for this lesson is this: how do I listen when the hard thing is about me?

Slide 2 · Quick to hear

Our passage is James chapter 1, verses 19 and 20.

“Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God.”

Slide 3 · Hearers and doers

James was writing to believers scattered and under pressure. His concern is how they receive God’s word. Just after our verses he says, “receive with meekness the implanted word,” and then, “But be doers of the word, and not hearers only.” James is teaching believers to receive God’s word humbly and put it into practice. His command to be quick to hear, slow to speak and slow to anger also challenges the way we respond to others.

This is not a passage specifically about marriage, but it gives us a principle we can apply to difficult conversations. And notice his reason. Our anger does not produce the righteousness of God. Winning the argument is not the same as doing what is right.

Slide 4 · The word: quick

One word gives this lesson its shape. The Greek word tachys means quick or swift. James uses it in the expression “quick to hear”. In this context, the instruction calls us to be ready to listen rather than rushing to speak or becoming angry. For our conversations, we can picture someone moving towards understanding rather than away from it.

And James sets it against two slow things: slow to speak, slow to anger. So the picture is not a slow listener and a quick talker. It is the other way round.

Slide 5 · The listening ear

Proverbs says the same in its own way. “If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame.” And, “The ear that listens to life-giving reproof will dwell among the wise,” for “he who listens to reproof gains intelligence.” The book also says, “Whoever loves discipline loves knowledge, but he who hates reproof is stupid.”

These verses are a challenge to the listener. Their question is: am I willing to learn? They are never verses to quote at your partner, and not to call your partner a fool. The mirror points at me.

Slide 6 · Point 1: What defensiveness teaches

Our first point is what defensiveness teaches. She says, “I feel like you don’t listen to me when we’re making decisions.” The defensive reply: “That’s not true. I listen to you all the time. You’re always complaining about something.” Notice the two moves. It denies her experience, and then it makes her the problem for having reported it. Both happen in under three seconds.

At first, she may argue back or try to explain. After repeated defensive responses, she may begin saying less. Eventually, she may stop raising certain subjects altogether. And here is what makes this so dangerous: the relationship can appear calmer, even though important concerns remain unresolved.

Slide 7 · When honesty becomes expensive

What has actually happened is that one person has learned a lesson: when I tell the truth about how I feel, I pay for it. So they start editing. “I’m fine,” when they are not. Whole subjects quietly disappear. You will not see it happening. From where you stand, everything is calm. If you punish honesty, do not be surprised when you eventually receive silence.

Defensiveness can come from shame, fear or habit. But your partner cannot see the shame. They can only see the door closing. So have three sentences ready. “Tell me more.” “What did I do that gave you that experience?” And, “I may see this differently, but I want to understand you first.” That third sentence keeps your right to disagree, while putting understanding first.

Slide 8 · Point 2: Listening is not waiting

Our second point: listening is not waiting. Most people think they are good listeners. Very few are, because while the other person is speaking, they are writing. She says, “I feel like your work is becoming more important than our relationship.” He hears the first few words, and he is already thinking, “That’s completely unfair. I’m working this hard for us.” So before she has quite finished, he is speaking: “You know how much I do for us.” He heard the words. He did not hear her.

The alternative is one question, and then silence. “What makes you feel that way?” Then he listens, without correcting and without explaining. Those things can wait five minutes. They will not become less true in that time.

Slide 9 · Understanding is not agreement

Now the qualification that keeps this from becoming surrender. Understanding is not agreement. You can understand exactly why she felt neglected, while also believing your hours were necessary. Both can be true. Listening first does not concede the point. It puts the two halves in a workable order.

So he can arrive somewhere neither of them would have reached the other way: “I understand why you felt neglected, and I didn’t realise the hours were landing on you that way. I’d also like you to understand why I’ve been working them.” That is a conversation. What they were doing before was delivering two closing arguments to a jury that was not present.

Slide 10 · The repeat-back rule

Here is the most useful technique I know: the repeat-back rule. Before responding, summarise the other person’s point and ask whether you have understood. “So you’re not saying I can’t work late. You’re saying that when I work late without telling you, you feel like you weren’t part of the decision. Have I got that right?” It is not agreeing. It is checking. And it often shows you had it wrong. She was not objecting to the work, but to being told afterwards. Without the repeat-back, he could have spent forty minutes defending something she never attacked.

Then both people speak. Neither person should control the conversation by withholding permission to reply. For an ordinary disagreement, understand the concern before you defend, but you do not have to remain in a conversation that becomes harmful. And often the most powerful sentence you have is simply: “I understand why that hurt you.”

Slide 11 · Point 3: Find the information

Our third point is to find the information. Criticism arrives in two parts: what is being said, and how it was said. Most people never separate them. Your partner says, “You never listen to me. I have to repeat myself all the time.” “Never” is not accurate, and both of you know it. So the door opens: “That’s ridiculous. I listen to you all the time.” Now the conversation is about the word “never”. You may win that argument. You will have won nothing.

There may be a genuine concern beneath the exaggeration: there are times when I don’t feel heard by you. That does not make every accusation true. The purpose is to understand what is being reported before deciding what is accurate. There is a question that does both jobs: “The way you said that hurt me. But is there something you’re trying to tell me that I need to understand?” It registers the hurt, and it goes after the content anyway.

Slide 12 · The truth inside what I hear

Another example. Your partner says, “You spend money without thinking.” The reflex: “I am not irresponsible with money.” The alternative: “What have I done that makes you feel that way?” And you may find four or five purchases you never mentioned. To you they were small. To your partner they show that you have different assumptions about what gets discussed. Now you know. Nobody can see themselves from outside.

Not everything you hear will be true. Sometimes your partner has misunderstood, or exaggerated, or had a bad day. You are not choosing between “everything my partner says is true” and “everything my partner says is an attack”. There is a third position, the adult one: let me find the truth inside what I’m hearing. Take that part seriously, and deal with the rest separately. No one has to accept a false accusation to show humility.

Slide 13 · Point 4: What the truth requires

Our fourth point is what the truth requires of you. Listening has to arrive somewhere. He says, “When you make decisions about money without talking to me, I feel like we’re not really building this life together.” She listens properly. She does not defend. She says, “I understand. I can see why that would make you feel excluded.” That is a good response. It is not the end of one. Because two weeks later it happens again. And now the question is not whether he was heard. It is whether what he said mattered enough to produce anything. Do not only acknowledge the truth. Decide what the truth requires of you.

Slide 14 · Four responsibilities

There are four responsibilities. Acknowledge it: “I can see this has been hurting you.” And do not minimise it because you did not intend it. “I didn’t mean to” is an explanation, not a response. Examine yourself: is there something here I need to change? Take responsibility for your part: “You’re right. I have been making those decisions without you, and that needs to change.” And make it concrete. “I’ll do better” is not a plan. “From now on, anything over this amount we decide together” is. Give it a number, a boundary or a schedule, something your partner could later see you keeping. Vague promises feel generous and produce nothing, and the next conversation is harder, because you have already agreed and still nothing has moved.

Slide 15 · When you disagree, or need time

This chapter does not mean you must accept whatever you are told. You may hear it fully, understand it properly, and still disagree. That is legitimate. And you do not have to confess to something untrue to end a conversation. A marriage where one account always wins because the other has stopped contesting is not honest.

Sometimes what you hear is too large for an immediate answer. You may say, “I hear you. I need to think about this properly, because I don’t want to give you a defensive answer.” Then name when you are coming back, as in Lesson 2, and come back. Sometimes the faithful response is a change, with specifics. Sometimes the truth requires an apology. Sometimes a boundary. Sometimes another conversation. And sometimes, “I understand your concern, and I don’t agree.” What must not happen is that you dismiss the concern without considering it.

Slide 16 · Moses listened

In Lesson 4 we saw Jethro raise a hard thing well. Here is the other half. “So Moses listened to the voice of his father-in-law and did all that he had said.” Moses chose able men, and they judged the people, while the hard cases came to him. Moses did not only hear the advice. He acted on it, and the burden that had been too heavy for him was shared. He heard, and something changed. As in Lesson 4, this is not a marriage passage. We are drawing one practical lesson: being heard means something when it leads somewhere.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Notice what defensiveness teaches, and stop teaching it. Listen before you answer, and repeat it back. Find the truth inside what you hear. And decide what the truth requires of you. That is what it means to be quick to hear and slow to speak. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise: the other side. If you have already completed the Lesson 4 conversation, you can now swap roles and practise the other side. If you waited until after this lesson, complete the first conversation when you are ready, and return to this exercise later. You do not need to do two difficult conversations in one sitting. Agree who raises it, when, and the forty-minute limit.

The one raising it writes the same three sentences: the event, the effect, the ask, and runs the four tests. The one receiving it prepares differently. Write down three answers. What do I usually do when I feel criticised? Go quiet, explain, counter-attack, apologise to make it stop? Name yours. What is my early warning sign? A tightness, a heat, a sudden clarity about what you are about to say. In the conversation, that sign is your signal to ask a question instead. And what is the sentence I most want to say, that I am going to hold? Write it down, so it is out of your head and on the page.

Slide 19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: the one raising it speaks, then stops. The receiver follows an order, and the order is the exercise. One: ask a question. Two: summarise what you heard, and ask whether it is accurate. Three: only now, respond. If you feel your warning sign, ask another question instead. Then do not end on understanding. The receiver says what it requires: what I’m going to change; or where I understand and where I disagree; or I need time, and I’ll come back on this date; or what I can do, and what I can’t. Write it down, with “by when”. Then the one who raised it answers honestly: did you feel heard, or did you feel handled? And in one month, ask: did anything actually change? If clear commitments keep going unmet, seek help rather than repeating the technique.

Slide 20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger.” And, “But be doers of the word, and not hearers only.” God hears us, and his word changes us. We listen, and let it change something.

Slide 21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Father, you hear us when we speak, and you are patient with us. Make us quick to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger. When the hard thing is about us, give us humility to find what is true, and courage to change. Guard us from punishing honesty, and from accepting what is false. Make us doers, and not hearers only. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
