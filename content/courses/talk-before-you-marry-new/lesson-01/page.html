Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Name what you’re afraid of

Welcome to Talk Before You Marry. I’m Pastor Richmond Kobe. For more than twenty years I have sat with couples, before the wedding and long after it. In this course I want to sit with the two of you the way I would if you came into my office together.

Across fifteen lessons we will take four steps together: make honesty safe; learn the skills; walk through the nine rooms; and keep talking. This course is not about the wedding, which will take one day. It is about the thirty or forty years afterwards, in which you will need to keep telling each other the truth.

And we begin with a question most couples would rather not ask: what are we already avoiding, and why does it feel like peace?

2 · “We never fight”

A couple sat in my office a few years ago and told me, with some pride, that they never fought. I have learned to slow down when I hear that.

As we talked, a pattern came out. When he was hurt, he went quiet. When she was angry, she withdrew. Neither of them wanted a confrontation, so the difficult conversations simply stopped happening. No shouting. No slammed doors. And no vulnerability either.

It held for years. They kept eating together, going to church, raising their family. But something underneath had gone cold. And the wife said something in that session that I have never forgotten: “I thought we were at peace. I didn’t realise we had just stopped talking.”

3 · Silence is not the same as peace

Silence can look like peace, because nobody is arguing. But real peace can hold a difference that both people are free to talk about. Avoidance hides the difference, and makes it harder to deal with later.

So a strong marriage is not one where the couple never disagrees. It is one where they can disagree without either of them feeling the ground shift: where they can speak honestly, listen without defending, forgive properly, and come back together afterwards.

Before the wedding, you have a gift: the chance to notice how you already handle tension, while you are still free to decide what kind of marriage you are building. And we begin where the Bible first shows a man and a woman hiding.

4 · Where are you?

Our passage is from the third chapter of the Bible. The Scripture readings in this course are from the English Standard Version. Genesis chapter 3, verses 8 to 10.

“And they heard the sound of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the man and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God among the trees of the garden. But the LORD God called to the man and said to him, ‘Where are you?’ And he said, ‘I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself.’”

5 · Covering, then hiding

First, notice what came just before it. At the end of chapter 2, the man and the woman “were both naked and were not ashamed.” Nothing hidden, and nothing to fear. Then, in chapter 3, they disobey God. And the first thing they do is cover themselves. The second thing they do is hide.

This passage is about sin, and about shame and fear before God. That is its meaning, and I want to be careful with it. I am not saying that a couple who avoid a hard subject are committing the sin of Genesis 3, and none of the patterns in this lesson are being called sins.

But the passage shows us something true about the human heart ever since. In the man’s own words: “I was afraid, … and I hid myself.” Fear makes people hide. And hiding can feel much safer than it is.

6 · The God who comes looking

Then notice what God does. He does not wait for them to come out. He calls: “Where are you?”

God already knew where they were. His question, “Where are you?”, brought them out of hiding and called them to answer him. The encounter would reveal their disobedience and its consequences. Yet even here, God took the initiative to seek them. And later in the same chapter, before they leave the garden, the Lord God makes garments of skins and clothes them.

From the very beginning, the Bible shows us a God who comes looking for people who hide. In Jesus, he came all the way. That is why Christians, of all people, can learn to stop hiding from each other. We are fully known, and we are still loved.

7 · Point 1: Why silence feels like peace

Our first point: why silence feels like peace.

Picture an engaged couple deep in wedding planning. Venue, flowers, the guest list, who sits where. They talk for hours. And the whole time, one of them is carrying a significant debt that the other has never seen the size of.

One thinks, “We’ll figure it out after we get married.” The other thinks, “I don’t want to start an argument before the wedding.” Neither says anything. And because nothing is said, the relationship feels smooth, so they take the absence of a difficult conversation as evidence of peace.

I have said this to many couples. If you cannot talk about money while you are engaged, marriage will not make that conversation easier. It will make it more expensive.

8 · Point 2: Six patterns

Our second point is six patterns that keep couples from the conversations they need.

These are not diagnoses, and they are not fixed personality types. Most couples will recognise more than one, and often one in each of you. The point is not to label each other. It is to notice what is happening between you, so that you can choose differently.

9 · Avoidance and assumption

The first is avoidance. Avoidance rarely announces itself. It sounds reasonable. “It’s not the right time.” “We don’t need to talk about that yet.” “I don’t want to upset them.” “We’ll deal with it later.” Each sentence is defensible on its own. That is the problem. Postponement never feels like a decision, so nobody notices they are making one, over and over, about the same subject. And the conversation does not disappear. It waits.

The second is assumption. Avoidance is knowing there is a conversation and dodging it. Assumption is not knowing there is a conversation at all. A couple both say, “We’re going to build our life together.” He pictures living near his parents, because that is what his family does. She pictures a home of their own, at a distance. Nobody lied. They simply filled in the blanks, each in their own handwriting. The correction is small: replace “I assume you…” with “Tell me what you think about…”.

10 · People-pleasing and defensiveness

The third is people-pleasing. A couple are discussing Christmas: where will they spend it once they are married? She wants to be with her own family. He wants to be with his parents, and says so with some force. She feels the room tighten, and hears herself say, “Whatever you want is fine.” It is not fine. She has traded an honest answer for a calm evening. Kindness is not the same as a constant yes. Sometimes the most loving sentence available to you before marriage is: “I love you, but I see this differently. Can we talk about it?”

The fourth is defensiveness. She raises something that has been sitting with her for weeks: his work is taking so much of his time, and she is worried about their life after the wedding. He hears an accusation: “So you think I’m a bad partner?” Within one sentence the subject has changed. Do that four or five times, and she stops raising concerns at all. The correction is one question, asked before anything else: “Help me understand why this matters to you.”

11 · Conflict avoidance and unspoken expectations

The fifth is conflict avoidance. Some people learned early that raised voices mean something is about to go badly. So a couple disagree about the wedding. She becomes frustrated, and her voice rises; not dramatically, just enough. He stops mid-sentence, goes quiet, and says, “Fine. We’ll do it your way.” Afterwards he says simply, “I just wanted the argument to stop.” The person who raises their voice remains responsible for their behaviour. If someone feels intimidated, the answer is not to demand that they become less afraid. The person whose behaviour is causing fear must take responsibility for changing it. And an ordinary disagreement in which a voice rises is not the same as a repeated pattern of threatening or controlling behaviour; that needs outside help, not only better communication.

The sixth is unspoken expectations. Assumption says, “I believe I know what you expect of me.” An unspoken expectation says, “I expect something from you, and I have never told you what it is.” On the surface, newly married couples argue about dishes and laundry. Underneath, they are discovering that they married each other holding two different pictures of what a marriage is, and neither ever showed the other theirs.

12 · Point 3: The map you brought from home

Our third point: what you learned about conflict before you met. You learned it by watching what happened in your house when the temperature rose. By the time you were an adult, you had a map. You did not choose it, and you may never have examined it, but you are using it, and so is the person you are marrying.

Some maps say: when people are angry, they talk it out. Others say: stay quiet until it passes. Or: raise your voice; whoever holds their ground longest wins. Or: somebody leaves. Or: someone gets punished. Or: nothing ever changes, so why speak?

Now put two of those maps in one marriage. One of you wants to settle it tonight; the other needs space. Understanding both reactions helps. It does not make pursuing someone who has asked for space acceptable. Respect the pause, and agree a time to come back.

This is not about blaming your parents. Keep what was good, question what was harmful, and tell each other what your map is.

13 · Point 4: The question underneath

Our fourth point is the question underneath. Underneath these patterns there is often a question nobody says out loud: what does this disagreement mean about us?

Near the end of a session, the quieter one often asks me, “Pastor, is it normal for us to disagree this much?” That is rarely the real question. The real question is: are we going to make it?

So let me answer it plainly. Disagreement is not a prediction of failure. Two people raised in different houses, carrying different assumptions about money and faith and family and time, are going to want different things. Having different opinions does not automatically mean you have chosen badly. What matters is what those differences reveal, how you respond to them, and whether both of you can discuss them honestly and respectfully. You are marrying an actual person, and not a reflection of yourself.

14 · Better questions

How often and how intensely you disagree does matter; frequent or distressing arguments can be a good reason to slow down and seek help. But counting arguments cannot answer the real question. These questions come closer.

Can we disagree without it becoming an attack on each other’s character? Can one of us tell the truth without being punished for it afterwards? Can we listen when we do not like what we are hearing? Can we repair properly after one of us has hurt the other? And can we stay committed to each other while a difference is still unresolved between us?

A quiet relationship may be safe, or it may be silenced. A loud one may be respectful, or it may be harmful. So watch whether both of you are free to speak.

15 · Love that speaks

Proverbs 27 puts it sharply: “Better is open rebuke than hidden love. Faithful are the wounds of a friend.” Proverbs is speaking of friendship and wise counsel: love that never speaks up when something is wrong is not much use. Applied to the two of you, it means this: the loving thing is not always the quiet thing.

A healthy engagement is one where you have learned, from experience with each other, that disagreeing does not mean you are losing each other. The goal was never to become a couple who agree about everything. The goal is to become a couple who can tell each other the truth, and still be safe.

16 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Silence is not the same as peace. Six patterns keep couples from the conversations they need. Each of you brings a map from home. And underneath it all is a question, “Are we going to make it?”, that disagreement alone cannot answer. Like the man and the woman in the garden, we are tempted to hide. But the God who came looking for them is with you as you learn to come out.

17 · Prepare on your own

Now the first exercise, which sets the pattern for the whole course: on your own first, then together. Do not skip the part on your own; answers written with your partner watching are written for an audience.

Take separate rooms, or separate times. Each of you writes down three subjects you have been steering around in this relationship. To find them, ask yourself: What have I decided we’ll deal with after the wedding? What have I started to bring up, and then changed my mind about? What do I hope they already know, so that I don’t have to say it? What did I say I was fine with, when I wasn’t? And what would I be relieved never to have to discuss?

For each subject, write how long you have been avoiding it, and what you fear will happen if you raise it. Be specific. And if your list is blank, that is allowed. Do not invent a problem to complete the exercise.

18 · Talk together

When you have both written your lists, sit down together. But first, one thing. If either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: choose the subjects you are comfortable bringing into this conversation. You do not have to hand over your entire private list. Take turns sharing, and listen without interrupting. Tonight you are not trying to solve everything. You are beginning to understand what has been left unspoken. Then talk about three questions. Did anything you heard surprise you? Did anything you shared feel risky to say? And which of these subjects feels hardest to raise?

Then choose one manageable subject, not the hardest one, for your first real conversation in Lesson 4, after you have learned how to raise a hard thing. Write it down. That is your next step.

19 · Remember

Remember these two words of Scripture. “But the LORD God called to the man and said to him, ‘Where are you?’” And, “Better is open rebuke than hidden love.” God comes looking for people who hide. And love, in the end, is willing to speak.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Lord God, you came looking for us when we were hiding, and in Jesus you came all the way to find us. Thank you that we are fully known by you, and still loved. Show us the subjects we have been steering around, and the fears beneath them. Give us courage to speak truthfully, patience to listen carefully, and wisdom to recognise what is good, what must change, and when we need help. Make our home a place where the truth is safe. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
