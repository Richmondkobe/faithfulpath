Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Tell yourself the truth first

Welcome back. This is Lesson 3, and it begins Part 2 of the course: learning the skills. The first two lessons were about the relationship. This one is about you, alone, before your partner is in the room. Here is the idea in one line: you cannot say what you have not admitted, even to yourself.

So the question for this lesson is simple to ask and harder to answer: what do I actually expect, and where did it come from?

2 · “I just thought he would know”

Most people cannot tell you what they expect from a marriage. Ask, and you hear: “I’m fairly flexible.” They are not lying. They genuinely cannot see them.

A couple marries, and within a few months she finds that he regularly makes plans with friends without mentioning them to her first. She is deeply upset, in a way that surprises even her. So I ask: what did you expect him to do instead? “I don’t know. I just thought he would know to tell me.” Why did you think he would know? A pause. Then: “Because that’s what my father did. He always talked about plans with my mother first.”

She was not being unreasonable. She held a real picture of what consideration looks like, and nobody had ever asked her to say it out loud, because to her it was not an expectation at all. It was simply what married people do. Disappointment often reveals an expectation that reflection has not yet brought into view.

3 · Deep water

So before we look at your expectations, we open the Scriptures. Our passage is a single proverb, Proverbs chapter 20, verse 5.

“The purpose in a man’s heart is like deep water, but a man of understanding will draw it out.”

4 · Search me, O God

And alongside it, the prayer that closes Psalm 139. “Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!”

5 · A proverb, and a prayer

Proverbs is a book of short sayings of wisdom for ordinary life. It is not a book about marriage, and this proverb is not about engagement. It is about how hard it can be to know what is really in a person’s heart. In a land where water was precious, a deep well held life, but you could not reach it by looking.

Psalm 139 begins, “O Lord, you have searched me and known me!” It ends with David asking the God who already knows him completely to search him still. Together they teach us that honest searching happens before God. We do not only look inward. We ask him to show us what is there.

6 · The word for “purpose”

One word in that proverb gives this lesson its shape. The Hebrew word translated “purpose” is ʿēṣâ. It often means counsel or a plan, the intention someone holds and acts on. The proverb says that what a person intends can lie in the heart like deep water: real, and hard to reach.

Then the verb. A man of understanding will “draw it out”. The Hebrew verb is dālâ, the ordinary word for drawing water. The same verb is used when Moses draws water for the flock in Exodus chapter 2. Understanding does not invent what is in the heart. It patiently brings up what is already there.

7 · The well and the bucket

Proverbs is speaking about understanding another person, and you will need that skill. In this lesson we first turn it towards ourselves. Before you can tell your partner what you expect, you need to draw up what is in your own heart, in the lamplight of God’s presence, not alone with your anxieties.

What comes up will not all be the same. Some will be fresh and right, and should be said clearly. Some will be inherited, and you may not want to keep it. But you cannot judge what you have never drawn up.

8 · Point 1: How you find out what you expected

Our first point is how you find out what you expected. The expectations can be about anything. Money, and how much is discussed before it is spent. Whether one of you is expected to notice what needs doing without being told. Church, and whether you go together. Your parents, and how much say they have. None of these will feel like expectations to you. They will feel like the way things are.

The most useful question I know for surfacing them is this: when have I thought, about anyone, not only my partner, “of course they should have known better”? That sentence can point to an unnamed expectation. But some standards, including honesty and freedom from harm, are not merely personal preferences. A strong reaction is worth understanding: what happened, what did I expect, and was a reasonable boundary crossed?

9 · Find it, trace it, decide

Now a caution, because this can be misread in two directions. It does not mean every expectation you hold should be surrendered. Some of your expectations are entirely right, and you should state them clearly and hold to them. Anyone who concludes that all their needs are just baggage has learned the wrong lesson, and will be easier to mistreat afterwards. Nor does it mean every strong reaction was an overreaction. Sometimes the other person really did behave badly.

The goal is narrower than that, and harder. Find it. Trace where it came from. Then decide, deliberately, whether it needs to be communicated, adjusted, or let go. That is why you do this alone first. Alone you can discover what is actually there.

10 · Point 2: The rules you never knew were rules

Our second point is the rules you never knew were rules. Your definition of normal was assembled somewhere: your family, your culture, your church. You did not choose it. You absorbed it, and stopped noticing it, the way you stopped noticing your own accent. The person you are marrying assembled theirs somewhere else.

He assumes that couples who love each other check in during the day. She grew up where everyone was busy and nobody expected to hear from anyone. Then one evening he says, “You didn’t check on me once today. If you really cared, you would have.” And she is genuinely bewildered: “Why would I? I was at work.” Their preferred levels of contact differ, and that can be discussed. But “If you really cared” turns one preference into a test of love.

So before you conclude that your partner is uncaring, ask: have they broken an agreement we made, or a rule I never told them existed? A broken agreement calls for accountability; an unspoken expectation calls for clarification. Neither excuses disrespect or harm.

11 · Three questions from the side

Three questions help bring these rules to the surface. They work better than asking yourself directly what you expect, because they approach from the side. What did my family always do that I assumed every married couple does? What would genuinely surprise me if my spouse didn’t do it? And the sharpest of the three: what would make me think something is wrong with our marriage, even if my spouse saw nothing wrong at all?

The answers are rarely dramatic. Married couples eat dinner together, or they eat separately when schedules differ. Holidays belong to the wider family, or to the two of you. Big decisions are discussed, or one of you simply makes them. Almost none of these has ever been said out loud, because to the person holding them they are not opinions. They are simply how a marriage works.

12 · Custom or conviction?

For Christian couples: some of what you consider normal genuinely is biblical conviction. Much of it is your culture, your church tradition, or your parents’ marriage wearing biblical clothing.

In Rome, believers disagreed about food and special days. Paul wrote, “One person esteems one day as better than another, while another esteems all days alike. Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind.” Paul is speaking there about matters of conscience among believers in a church, not about marriage. But the principle helps us here: not every custom is a command. When one person’s family customs are treated as God’s requirements, the other person is no longer free to discuss them honestly. A family tradition has been given an authority that Scripture itself may not give it.

13 · Point 3: Preference, conviction or deal-breaker

Our third point is preference, conviction or deal-breaker. Not every expectation carries the same weight, and much trouble comes from failing to sort them.

A preference is something you would like and can live without. “I’d prefer we spend most evenings together.” That is worth saying and negotiating, but an evening with friends is not evidence that something is wrong.

A conviction is something you hold because of your faith, your values, or your understanding of what a marriage is. “I believe our marriage should be built on Christ.” Two people with genuinely different convictions here are not disagreeing about evenings. They are proposing to build different buildings on the same plot.

A deal-breaker is something you cannot accept in a marriage at all. “I cannot marry someone who does not want children.” That may be a genuine incompatibility. Do not decide its importance for each other. And intimidation or violence is not simply another preference to negotiate.

14 · Sorting honestly

The most common failure is inflation: treating preferences as convictions, and convictions as deal-breakers. Within a few sentences, “I would prefer” becomes “If you loved me, you would.” That turns a preference into a test of love. Your partner can love you entirely and still not have known you wanted the dishes done before bed.

There is a quieter failure in the opposite direction. Some people deflate everything. They talk themselves out of every expectation and arrive at the wedding having agreed to a marriage they never wanted. That is not humility. That is Lesson 1 with better vocabulary.

Two questions do most of the sorting. Is this something I need, something I believe, or something I simply like? And, if my partner never changed on this, could we still have a healthy marriage? These categories help you think, but they are not completely separate. A conviction may also be a deal-breaker. The question is not only what you believe, but whether you could freely and wisely marry someone who disagrees with you about it. And one warning: do not call something a deal-breaker because you are angry about it today. If you discover a serious concern, you may pause, and seek advice.

15 · The log and the speck

Jesus gave a picture that belongs here. “Why do you see the speck that is in your brother’s eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye? Or how can you say to your brother, ‘Let me take the speck out of your eye,’ when there is the log in your own eye? You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye.”

Jesus warns against judging others while excusing ourselves. He does not forbid helping a brother: look at yourself first, and then you will see clearly.

Here is a specifically Christian trap. It is easy to reclassify a preference as a conviction by attaching a verse to it. Then your partner cannot disagree with you without appearing to disagree with God, which is exactly what rule four from Lesson 2 forbids. If you find yourself reaching for a verse to give weight to something that was, an hour ago, just what you wanted, stop. A genuine biblical conviction should be examined through Scripture, not defended by attaching an isolated verse to something we simply prefer.

16 · Point 4: Say what you actually want

Our fourth point is to say what you actually want. Couples tell me they agree about money, children and faith. Then I ask what that means in practice, and the room goes quiet. They have exchanged headlines, not the article underneath.

So get specific. Money: joint accounts or separate, and how much can either of you spend without asking? Sex: what do you each expect, and what were you each taught, at home and in church? Some of that teaching was good, and some was fear dressed as holiness. Children: how many, when, and what if it does not happen? Church: every Sunday, the same church, and how much ministry? Family: how often you visit, and how much say parents have.

Then say it plainly. This is what I want. This is why I want it. This is what I would be hoping for from you. What do you want? Without that last sentence, you have issued terms. And if you discover that you do not know what you want, “I don’t know” is a real answer. Write it down.

17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Disappointment can reveal what you expected. Some of your firmest rules were never spoken. Sort them honestly: preference, conviction or deal-breaker. And say what you actually want, and ask what your partner wants. Draw up what is there, before God, and you will bring something true to the conversations ahead. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise: the Private Inventory. Read this first: your answers are not shared with your partner through this course. Nothing you type here is saved. If you keep a written copy, choose somewhere private. Written for nobody, an inventory produces the answers that are actually there. Take several sittings.

Part One is this lesson’s three questions. Part Two takes the five areas. For each, write what you want, specifically. Then write where it came from: your family, your church, a past relationship, or your own thinking. Then mark it P, C or D. Never downgrade a safety concern. Part Three asks three hard questions, including this one: is there anything I have been hoping marriage will change? Marriage does not usually change these things. It reveals them. Begin with one small step: finish the sentence, “Of course they should have known…”

19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else, do something very limited. Do not exchange inventories. Instead, each of you answers one question out loud: what surprised you about your own answers? Not what you wrote, but what surprised you about it. If you both feel comfortable, you may agree that you will use your private inventories to prepare for the rooms ahead. You do not have to exchange your private pages, and neither person needs to disclose an answer before they are ready. And write the date you expect to begin Lesson 6, the first room.

20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “The purpose in a man’s heart is like deep water, but a man of understanding will draw it out.” And, “Search me, O God, and know my heart!” The God who already knows you is the safest place to begin telling yourself the truth.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Lord God, you have searched us and known us. Search our hearts now, and show us what is there. Help us to tell the difference between what we prefer, what we believe, and what we truly need. Keep us from using your word to win, and give us courage to say plainly what we want. Lead us in the way everlasting. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
