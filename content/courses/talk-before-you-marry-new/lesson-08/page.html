Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · The third door

Welcome back. This is Lesson 8, and we open the third room of the house: family and boundaries. A couple can genuinely love their parents, mean well in every direction, and still create serious problems, because they have never clarified where the family they came from ends and the marriage they are making begins.

So the question for this room is this: how do we honour our parents and still build a new home?

Slide 2 · Leave and hold fast

Scripture: Genesis 2:24

Our passage is Genesis chapter 2, verse 24.

“Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh.”

Slide 3 · The first marriage

Scripture: Genesis 2:22–24; Matthew 19:4–6

This verse comes straight after the creation of the woman. God brings her to the man, and he says, “This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh.” Then comes the word “Therefore”. This is why a man leaves his father and mother: marriage creates a new bond.

Jesus quotes this verse when he teaches about marriage, in Matthew chapter 19. The verse speaks of the man leaving. But the principle applies to both of you. In marriage, each of you leaves one household, and together you make another.

Slide 4 · Honour, for life

Scripture: Exodus 20:12; Ephesians 6:2–3; 1 Timothy 5:4

But the same Scripture says, “Honor your father and your mother.” Paul repeats it for believers in Ephesians chapter 6. And in his first letter to Timothy, writing about the care of widows in the church, he says children and grandchildren should “make some return to their parents.” Honour is not something you leave behind at the wedding. It continues for life, and as parents grow older it often becomes practical care. Paul is speaking particularly about caring for widows in genuine need. The passage teaches family responsibility, but it does not prescribe an unlimited financial obligation or require a couple to agree to every request from relatives.

So the Bible holds both together. Leaving changes the order of loyalties. It does not end the duty of love.

Slide 5 · The words: leave, and hold fast

Scripture: Genesis 2:24

Two Hebrew words give this lesson its shape. The first is azav, translated “leave”. It means to leave or depart from. The second word is davak, translated “hold fast”. It means to cling or hold fast, and elsewhere it is used of people who keep close to God.

Together, in this verse, they describe the formation of a new marriage bond. That does not necessarily require moving far away from your parents. But it does mean recognising the new relationship, and learning to make decisions together.

Slide 6 · Two homes, and a new one

Here is the picture for this room: two family homes on two hills, and a path from each to a new home between them. The paths stay open. Visits go both ways, and love travels along them. But the new home has its own door, and the people who live there decide when it opens. A boundary is a gate, not a wall.

Slide 7 · Point 1: What usually goes wrong

Our first point is what usually goes wrong. Couples discuss their families in terms of feeling rather than arrangement. “I love my parents.” “My family is very important to me.” Both are fine. Neither answers a single practical question. Who makes decisions about your home? How often will parents visit? Can a parent stay indefinitely? How much money will you provide? What happens when one of them criticises your spouse? And underneath them all: when your spouse and your parents disagree, who comes first?

Slide 8 · No united position

The most common version is no united position before the wedding. His mother expects to visit whenever she likes; that is how his family has always worked. She expects visits to be arranged in advance; that is how hers works. Neither has ever raised it. Then they are married, and it happens, and he says: “That’s my mother. You can’t tell her she needs permission to come to her son’s house.” What she hears is: my mother’s comfort matters more than yours.

They appear to be arguing about a visit. They are arguing about something considerably larger: are we a married couple who decides things together? The same thing happens just as often the other way round, with her family.

Slide 9 · Loyalty conflict

A second failure is loyalty conflict. Someone feels that setting any boundary with a parent is a betrayal of that parent. So they will not say no to their family, and instead expect their spouse to absorb the consequences quietly. Resentment builds fast, and it builds towards the spouse rather than the parent, which is precisely backwards.

Picture it. Her father rings most evenings with advice about their money, their jobs, even their furniture. She never tells him that this is a decision for the two of them, because she cannot bear to hurt him. So her husband swallows it, evening after evening. He is not angry with her father. He is becoming angry with her. A boundary she would not set has become a burden he is carrying.

Slide 10 · Point 2: Leaving and holding fast

Scripture: Genesis 2:24

Our second point is leaving and holding fast. “Honour your father and mother” is sometimes treated as though parents keep authority over you permanently. That is not what Christian marriage teaches. The point is not that you stop loving your parents. Marriage creates a new primary unit, and that unit must be able to make its own decisions.

You still honour your parents. But honour is not the same as obedience to every parental demand. You can say, “I love you, Mum, but my wife and I have decided differently.” Or, “Dad, I appreciate the advice, but this is something the two of us need to decide.” Said kindly and firmly, sentences like these protect the marriage and the relationship with your parents at the same time.

Slide 11 · A boundary is not rejection

You can support your parents financially without giving them a vote in your household. You can visit often without letting them govern your marriage. You can disagree with them respectfully and remain a son or daughter who honours them.

A boundary is not rejection. Often it is the opposite. It says: I love you enough to have an honest relationship with you, and I will not let this relationship run our marriage.

Slide 12 · Point 3: Where Christian couples get this wrong

Our third point is where Christian couples get this wrong: Scripture used as leverage. One says, “My mother is my mother; the Bible says I have to honour her.” The other replies, “I’m your wife; you’re supposed to choose me.” Now you are in a loyalty contest with verses as ammunition, which is exactly what rule four in Lesson 2 forbids: Scripture is not a weapon.

Four things instead. Honour your parents. Leave your parents. Hold fast to your spouse. And build a marriage where extended family is loved without being allowed to govern it.

Slide 13 · Point 4: The arrangements

Our fourth point is the practical one: visits, holidays, privacy and support. Be extremely practical here. Vagueness is what causes the trouble. The conversation has four parts. Part one is the arrangements. Try to be specific. Where you are not ready to agree, record the question, what you need to find out, and when you will return to it. You do not need to settle every family arrangement in one evening. How often do we expect to see each set of parents? Can family visit our home without checking with both of us first? Will either set of parents have a key? Can our parents stay with us, and for how long? What financial responsibility do we each have towards our parents, and what is the actual figure? If a parent needs money, who decides? And where do we spend Christmas, Easter and the significant days? Is it the same every year?

Slide 14 · Part two: What we tell our parents

Part two is information. When we disagree with each other, what can I tell my parents? That question matters more than couples expect. When parents become the place where every marital conflict gets reported, something unfortunate happens: you forgive each other, and your parents go on carrying the offence. They may remember it far longer than you did. This does not mean you can never ask for advice. It means you agree, together, what is shared and with whom. Protect the privacy of ordinary disagreements. But neither partner needs the other’s permission to seek confidential help about abuse, threats, coercion or anything that makes them feel unsafe. Keeping the marriage private must never mean keeping harm secret. And who else finds out about our disagreements: a sibling, a friend, a pastor? What is the line?

Slide 15 · Parts three and four: Conflict, and the uncomfortable question

Part three is conflict. What do we do when one of my parents criticises you? What do we do when my parents want something that contradicts what we have already decided together? And who speaks to their own parents when a boundary needs setting? Where it is safe and practical, each of you should take responsibility for speaking to your own relatives. You may need to speak together, or to seek help. The point is not to leave one partner alone to absorb hostility.

Part four is the uncomfortable question. When it is safe, each of you may consider: “When we are married, who do I expect to disappoint sometimes: my parents or my spouse?” Discuss it together if you are both comfortable. You may pause, or return to it later. You cannot keep everyone comfortable all the time. Discuss how you will make a fair decision, taking each person’s needs seriously. Holding fast to your spouse does not mean that your parents never matter, and loyalty to a spouse never means concealing harm or dismissing a child’s needs. It means that, when you must choose, you choose together.

Slide 16 · If you find a real difference

Some differences about visits, holidays and family support can be worked through with honest conversation. But pressure to marry, intimidation, isolation or controlling behaviour are not ordinary differences. If these are present, do not try to resolve them through a joint exercise. Seek confidential support independently. No one should be pressured into marriage by family or community.

Now the differences that can be worked through. Different expectations about closeness: agree on visits, notice and private time in ways both of you can freely accept. Respect cultural differences, but never use them to excuse intrusion, isolation or control.

Financial obligation to parents: as we saw in Lesson 6, name the actual amount and the actual expectation, and do not resolve it in one evening. A parent who does not accept the marriage: talk about it now, and decide together what you will and will not accept; it should not be managed by the person they disapprove of. A parent who is already governing decisions: if one of you cannot make a significant decision without a parent’s approval, marriage will not change that. Be honest about it, and consider talking with someone outside both families. A parent who will need care: ageing parents are not a hypothetical. If one of you is the likely carer, or an only child, say so now, and talk about what it will mean: where they would live, who provides, and what it costs.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Talk about arrangements, not only feelings. Leave and hold fast, and keep honouring your parents for life. Do not use Scripture as leverage. And agree visits, holidays, privacy and support specifically. The question before the wedding is not, do you like my family? It is: what boundaries will protect our marriage while we continue to love our families well? That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 20 · Remember

Scripture: Genesis 2:24; Ephesians 6:2

Remember these words of Scripture. “Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife.” And, “Honor your father and mother.” Both are true at once: a new home, and lifelong honour.
