For further study (optional)

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · The second door

Welcome back. This is Lesson 7, and we open the second room of the house. Many couples arrive at their wedding having discussed the venue, the money, where they will live and how many children they hope for, and having said almost nothing about intimacy. Some have been taught, rightly, to keep sex for marriage. Almost none were taught how to talk about it beforehand.

So the question for this room is this: what are we each carrying into this part of marriage?

Slide 2 · Before we begin

Before we begin, a word about how we will walk through this room. Couples come with different histories. Some of you have kept sex for marriage. Others bring previous relationships, a previous marriage, or experiences you did not choose. No one in this room is less worthy of love. This lesson is about expectations, consent and honest talk, not an account of what anyone has done. Nothing graphic is needed, here or in your conversation.

And one thing is true throughout: a no must always be respected, and pressure is never your fault. If anything in this lesson touches something painful, you may stop, and come back to it when you are ready.

Slide 3 · Mutual, in both directions

Scripture: 1 Corinthians 7:3–5

Our passage is from Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter 7, verses 3 to 5.

“The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband. For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does. Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer; but then come together again, so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control.”

Slide 4 · Written to married people

Scripture: 1 Corinthians 7:1–5; Hebrews 13:4

Paul is answering a letter. “Now concerning the matters about which you wrote,” he says. It seems some in Corinth believed it was holier for married couples to stay apart. Paul corrects that. In marriage, he says, husband and wife give themselves to each other, and any time apart should be by agreement, for a limited time, for prayer.

So notice: these verses describe marriage, not engagement. We are using them to prepare you for what marriage will ask. Before the wedding, Scripture calls us to holiness: “Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled.” That is not only a restriction. It is a preparation, keeping something for the place where it belongs.

Slide 5 · Naked and not ashamed

Scripture: Genesis 2:25

In Lesson 1 we met a verse that describes the garden before shame entered: “And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed.” Nothing hidden, and nothing to fear. Then, after the fall, they covered themselves and hid.

Genesis shows us a picture of innocence before shame entered human experience. God made men and women, and God made marriage, and his design includes this part of life. We cannot return to Eden simply by having a conversation. But in marriage, we can learn to speak truthfully, respect one another’s boundaries, and create a relationship in which difficult subjects can be discussed without humiliation or fear.

Slide 6 · The word: has authority over

Scripture: 1 Corinthians 7:4

One word in that passage gives this lesson its shape. The Greek word translated “has authority over” is exousiazo. It means to have authority or rights over something. Paul uses it twice in verse 4, in the phrase “does not have authority over”: first of the wife, and then of the husband.

In a world where husbands and wives did not always enjoy equal social standing, Paul’s reciprocal wording is striking. He applies the same language to the wife and to the husband. Neither is given a licence to control or coerce the other. Each belongs to the other, in both directions. It is a picture of mutual self-giving. It has sometimes been taught as a claim of entitlement, and that reading has done real harm. So let us say it plainly: marriage is never an entitlement to another person’s body.

Slide 7 · Point 1: Different histories, no shame

Scripture: 1 John 1:9

Our first point: different histories, and no shame. Two people may come to this room by very different roads. Where there is sin in the past, Scripture offers real forgiveness: “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.” A forgiven past does not have to be relived for your partner’s comfort.

And where there are experiences you did not choose, hear this clearly: what was done to you was not your fault. Healing is not a test you must pass before you are worthy of marriage. Both of you arrive at the same door, and both receive the same welcome.

Slide 8 · What usually goes wrong

What usually goes wrong in this room? First, silence. Second, shame about the questions themselves. Someone has real questions about their body, their history or their fears, and cannot ask them, because a voice somewhere says a good Christian should not be thinking about this. Third, mistaking attraction for preparation. Strong chemistry tells you almost nothing about expectations of affection, frequency, boundaries, or what happens when one of you is not interested. Fourth, imported expectations, from films, friends, novels or pornography. Most people do not know they carry them until reality contradicts them.

And the largest misconception: “Once we’re married, it will all happen naturally.” The wedding changes your commitments. It does not make comfort, confidence or shared desire automatic.

Slide 9 · Point 2: What we were taught

Our second point: what we were taught, by family and church. Many of us learned about sex from family, from church, and from friends, often by implication rather than instruction. Was it spoken about, or only implied? What did I come to believe was shameful? Those messages are still operating. Which of them do I still believe, and which do I want to leave behind?

Then ask what you believe now. What is this part of marriage for? One of you may think of it mainly as physical closeness. The other may connect it with emotional bonding, with covenant, or with children. Those are different frameworks, and they produce different disappointments if they are never discussed.

Slide 10 · Point 3: Mutuality, not entitlement

Scripture: 1 Corinthians 7:4

Our third point: mutuality, not entitlement. Paul’s words describe each belonging to the other, in both directions. Mutuality can never justify coercion. Consent must be freely given, and it can be withdrawn at any time. Silence, a previous yes, or the marriage itself is not continuing permission. Stop if either person says no, seems unsure, or wants to stop. No one should have to bargain for the right to refuse.

No must be safe in your marriage. If it is not, that is not a difference of expectation. It is something else, and it needs help from outside the two of you.

Slide 11 · Language for learning each other

How will you tell each other what is welcome, what is uncomfortable, what is frightening, and what is too much? You need actual language for this. Mind-reading will not develop on its own. Simple sentences help, said without drama: “Can we try that differently?” “I like this.” “I’m not comfortable with that.” “I need more time.”

Some people will also want to say, “I’m not in the mood tonight, but I want to be close to you.” That sentence may express what you feel, but it is optional. You do not owe affection or an explanation to make a no valid.

Slide 12 · The wedding night, and health

Take the pressure off the wedding night. Films have trained many of us to expect something effortless and unforgettable. The reality is often less cinematic. You may be exhausted. You may be nervous. You may laugh. You may need to stop. None of that means anything is wrong with your marriage. The wedding night is the beginning of learning each other. It is not an examination.

Pain is a reason to stop, not a test of commitment. If intimacy is painful or difficult, seek help from a qualified healthcare professional; help is available. And before marriage, ask a healthcare professional about health matters, testing and contraception. This is preparation, not an accusation.

Slide 13 · Point 4: The conversation

Our fourth point is the conversation itself. It comes in five parts, and it should be taken slowly. Part one is what you were taught, which we have just described. Part two is what you believe now. What do I think sex is for in a marriage? What does physical affection mean to me outside of sex? What do I hope it will be like between us?

Part three is the fears. What am I nervous about? What am I afraid you will expect of me? What am I afraid I won’t be able to give you? This is the part of the room where couples go quiet, and the silence is usually not because there is nothing to say.

Slide 14 · Part four: The agreements

Part four is the agreements. How will we tell each other what we want, in the moment and afterwards? What will we do when one of us wants to and the other doesn’t? Not if, but when: desire will not always align, and couples who have never discussed it handle it worst. How do we make sure a no is never punished? What is our position on contraception, and have we both actually thought about it? And what does each of us consider off-limits, and can we say so without having to justify it?

Slide 15 · Part five: The hard one

Part five is the hard one. When it is safe, each of you may consider this question: “Is there anything about my history, my struggles or my expectations that you need to know before we marry?” You do not owe an account of experiences you did not choose, or graphic details. Share relevant present-day information about health, consent, commitments and expectations in a way that is safe and appropriate. You may pause, seek confidential advice, or return to the conversation later.

If something difficult comes out, listen without pressing for details. Thank the person for what they chose to share. Ask what support they want, and allow time before you respond further.

Slide 16 · If you find a real difference

Some differences in expectations can be worked through with patience and honest conversation. But pressure, coercion or refusal to respect a no are not ordinary differences. They require a different response: stop the joint exercises and seek confidential support independently. You do not have to persuade your partner that your boundary is valid.

Now the differences that can be worked through. Different expectations about frequency: discuss the difference without declaring one level correct or promising a quota. Consent applies each time. Different comfort levels: also workable, and the more cautious person’s boundary holds. It is not a problem to be gradually overcome.

Pornography, past or present: if it is a current struggle, take it seriously and seek appropriate support. Where it is safe, discuss how it affects your expectations and commitments before marriage. It does not disqualify you from marriage, and marriage will not fix it. Painful experiences that still affect you were not your fault. You deserve support at a pace you choose, and a counsellor with trauma experience can help.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Different histories are met without shame. Name what you were taught, and what you believe now. Each belongs to the other, in mutual self-giving, never as an entitlement. And take the conversation slowly. Here is the principle for this room: the goal is not to enter marriage knowing everything. It is to enter marriage knowing you can approach difficult subjects with honesty, respect and freedom. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise. First, on your own, prepare for the room. Begin with one small step: write down one thing you were taught about sex that you want to examine. Keep your inventory from Lesson 3 beside you. Then write your answers to the first three parts: what you were taught, what you believe now, and your fears. These answers are yours. In the conversation, you share only what you freely choose. Keep private health details out of anything you share, unless you freely choose to include them.

Slide 19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: review your rules from Lesson 2, and allow 45 to 60 minutes. Each of you has prepared privately. During the conversation, share only what you freely choose to share. You may pass on a question, pause, or return later. Discuss present-day expectations, consent and boundaries, without requiring either person to reveal private reflections or painful experiences. Before you leave the room, record your agreements about consent, communication and seeking help.

Slide 20 · Remember

Scripture: Genesis 2:25; Hebrews 13:4

Remember these words of Scripture. “And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed.” And, “Let marriage be held in honor among all.” God designed this part of life, and he can heal what has been hurt in it.

Slide 21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Father, you made us, body and soul, and you called it good. Take away the shame that keeps us silent, and heal what has been hurt. Teach us to give ourselves to each other freely, with honour and with care. Make our home a place where a no is safe and every word is heard. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
