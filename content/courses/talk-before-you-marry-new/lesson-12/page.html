For further study (optional)

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · The seventh door

Welcome back. This is Lesson 12, and we open the seventh room of the house: past relationships. The past becomes present tense very quickly in this room. A new relationship can start carrying weight that belongs to an old one, and neither person can quite see where it is coming from.

So the question for this room is this: what from the past needs to be known, and what has grace changed?

Slide 2 · Put off, put on

Scripture: Colossians 3:9–11

Our passage is Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter 3, verses 9 to 11.

“Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator. Here there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all.”

Slide 3 · A new way of speaking

Scripture: Colossians 3:9–14

Paul was writing to a church. The new life in Christ changes how believers speak to one another. Because you have put off the old self and put on the new, “Do not lie to one another.” And a few verses later he goes on: “Put on then, as God’s chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience, bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other.” That is written to a whole church. We apply it here to a couple preparing for marriage, and we say so. Truthfulness, and compassion, belong together in this room.

Slide 4 · A new creation

Scripture: 2 Corinthians 5:17

Paul also says, “Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come.” That is good news for anyone with a past, which is everyone. The grace of God means your past does not have to be your identity. But it does not mean the facts evaporate. Both of those truths will matter in this room.

Slide 5 · Point 1: Not everything you carry is sin

Before we go any further, here is our first point, and it needs to be heard first. Not everything you carry is sin. A person may carry grief from a relationship that ended. Betrayal by someone they trusted. Responsibilities from the past, such as a child or a family duty. Or experiences of harm for which they were not responsible. What was done to you is not your sin to confess. Later in this lesson we will read a psalm about confessed sin and forgiveness. Please do not hear your wounds called sins. They are not.

Slide 6 · What usually goes wrong

What usually goes wrong in this room? Three things. First, comparison. Someone hears, “I was in a serious relationship before. It ended badly,” and soon the questions start. “Did you love her more than you love me? What did she have that I don’t?” The conversation has become a competition with someone who is not in the room and cannot lose.

Second, fear that the story repeats. If a previous partner was unfaithful, ordinary lateness becomes suspicious. The partner is being assessed partly on what somebody else did. And third, inherited fear in the person who was hurt: “The last person left when it got difficult. This one probably will too.” A previous betrayal may leave someone fearful that the same thing will happen again. They may find themselves watching for signs of danger or seeking repeated reassurance. Those feelings deserve understanding, but they do not justify checking a partner’s messages, restricting their friendships or controlling their movements. Past hurt can explain why trust feels difficult; it does not excuse controlling behaviour.

Slide 7 · Point 2: What does your partner need to know?

Our second point is relevance: what the past can and cannot tell you. Two pieces of advice circulate in Christian settings. “Tell your partner absolutely everything.” And, “Your past is under the blood; don’t talk about it.” Neither is adequate. The better principle sits between them: your partner deserves the information they need to make an informed decision about marrying you. They do not need every private detail of your history.

So the question is not, “What can I possibly confess?” It is, “What does this person need to know, because it materially affects our marriage?”

Slide 8 · What materially affects the marriage

That includes a previous marriage. An existing child. Significant financial consequences from a past relationship. Ongoing legal or family ties. A serious pattern that could affect this marriage. Anything that would reasonably change their decision to marry you. One question helps: is there anything my partner could discover later that would reasonably make them say, “Why didn’t you tell me before we married?” If something comes to mind, consider why it matters, and how it can be discussed safely. But a partner’s wish to know every private detail does not itself create a right to that detail.

Slide 9 · Honest, not graphic

Sexual history needs particular care. A detailed catalogue of previous experiences can create painful comparisons and mental images that are difficult to forget, without producing anything useful. But neither should someone conceal significant history, ongoing consequences, or anything that will directly affect the marriage. There is a difference between honesty and exhaustive disclosure. You can be truthful without being graphic.

Something like: “There are things about my past relationships that I believe you should know before we marry. I want to be truthful about anything that affects our future, but I don’t think describing every private detail would help either of us. I’m willing to discuss what matters, at a pace that is safe and respectful.” That is honest. It is not an interrogation. And do not disclose something painful as a test of whether your partner will prove their love by how they receive it.

Slide 10 · Point 3: Two questions behind the questions

Our third point is disclosure, and what it is for. There are two questions behind the questions. To the one hearing it: ask yourself what you are actually afraid of. Perhaps you fear that you are not enough, or that the same thing will happen again. Perhaps you are concerned about trust, consequences that remain, or information you did not previously know. Name the real concern rather than repeatedly asking for more details that may not help you decide.

To the one disclosing: am I telling this because my partner needs to know, or because I need them to carry my guilt? Your future spouse may offer care and grace, but neither of you can be solely responsible for the other’s recovery. Support beyond the relationship, from a pastor or counsellor, can help you both.

Slide 11 · Disclosure, safely

So plan how to share what materially affects the marriage. You may need more time, or qualified support. And do not force a disclosure if doing so could put you at risk.

The one receiving uses what you learned in Lesson 5: question first, repeat it back, and only then respond. And here is a rule for this room: the one receiving may ask what they need to know in order to decide. They may not ask for detail whose only function is to picture it. If you are not sure which you are asking for, ask yourself what you would do with the answer.

Slide 12 · Point 4: When I kept silent

Scripture: Psalm 32:1–5

Our fourth point is forgiveness without pretending. Here is a psalm of David about confessed sin. Remember: this is about sin, not about wounds.

“Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. Blessed is the man against whom the LORD counts no iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no deceit. For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long. For day and night your hand was heavy upon me; my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer. Selah.

“I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, ‘I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,’ and you forgave the iniquity of my sin. Selah.”

Slide 13 · Grace and honesty together

Scripture: Psalm 32:3, 5

David’s silence was heavy. Confession brought relief and forgiveness. David’s psalm teaches us about the burden of unconfessed sin and the mercy of God’s forgiveness. It does not give us a list of what to tell a future spouse. But alongside Paul’s command not to lie to one another, it reminds us that grace and truth belong together. God’s forgiveness must not be used as a reason to conceal information that would materially affect another person’s decision to marry.

So two things, one for each of you. To the one with the history: “God has forgiven me” must never become a reason to withhold information your future spouse reasonably needs in order to marry knowingly. Grace received is not a substitute for honesty given. To the one receiving it: do not use a disclosure as a weapon. You may still ask relevant questions, need time, or discuss consequences that remain unresolved. Grace does not require pretending that trust has already been restored.

Slide 14 · The principle, and the closing question

Here is the principle for this room. Tell the truth your partner needs in order to know who they are marrying. Do not hide consequential realities. And do not turn honesty into a demand for details that produce only comparison and shame.

Then one final question: “Now that you know this part of your partner’s story, can you make a free and honest decision about marrying them, without punishing them for it afterwards?” If the answer is yes, it needs to be actually yes, including in three years, during an argument about something else. The goal is not two people with spotless histories. Almost nobody has one. It is two people entering marriage without secrets that would have changed the decision, and without letting old relationships become new competitors.

And hear this too. You may forgive someone and still decide that marriage is not the right step. You may need more time, counsel or clarity. A decision not to marry is not automatically unforgiveness, and nobody should use grace to pressure you into saying yes.

Slide 15 · The conversation

The conversation comes in five parts. Share what you freely choose, and you may pass on a question or return to it later. Part one is relevance, done alone, first, on paper. Is there anything in my past that materially affects this marriage? Is there anything I am hoping never comes up? Part two is what your history taught you: about yourself, what to expect from a partner, and what to fear. Part three is the present. Do I compare you to anyone? What makes me jealous, and what does that jealousy actually want? Am I maintaining any contact or relationship that materially affects the trust and commitments we are preparing to make? Is there anything about that contact I need to explain honestly? These are questions to ask yourself, not a demand for passwords, or to hand over a phone. Part four is disclosure, planned and safe. And part five is the closing question. When it is safe, consider it first on your own: can I make a free decision about marrying you with this information, while committing not to use it to shame or punish you? Do not answer quickly. “I need time” is a far better answer than a yes you cannot keep.

Slide 16 · If you find a real difference

Some differences here can be worked through with patience. But jealousy or past hurt never justifies surveillance, isolation or control. If either of you is being checked, isolated or controlled, stop the joint exercises and seek confidential support on your own.

Now the differences that can be worked through. Significant undisclosed history is not a difference; it is information. Take days or weeks with it, not one evening. Notice whether your distress is about the fact itself, or about not having been told; those are different injuries. Persistent comparison is not something the other person can fix by being better; work through it with someone before the wedding. Ongoing emotional attachment to an ex: say it now. It is far harder to discover after a wedding. And a demand for every private detail may come from fear, but it can also become a form of pressure or control. Discuss what is genuinely relevant without turning the conversation into an interrogation.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Not everything you carry is sin. Tell what your partner needs in order to decide. Be honest, not graphic, and keep it safe. And forgive without pretending. The goal is two people entering marriage without secrets that would have changed the decision, and without old relationships as new competitors. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise. First, on your own, prepare for the room. Begin with one small step: write down one thing your partner needs to know, and one thing grace has changed. Keep your inventory from Lesson 3 beside you, as your own private reference. Then write your answers to the first three parts: relevance, what your history taught you, and the present. You do not need a written catalogue of your history. Nothing you type on the lesson page is saved.

Slide 19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: review your rules from Lesson 2, and allow 45 to 60 minutes. Work through the five parts, sharing what you freely choose. You may pass on a question, stop earlier, or agree when to continue. Before you leave the room, record only the next steps you both agree are useful. You do not need a written catalogue of private history. Allow time for questions, or support.

Slide 20 · Remember

Scripture: Colossians 3:9–10; 2 Corinthians 5:17

Remember these words of Scripture. “Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices and have put on the new self.” And, “The old has passed away; behold, the new has come.” Grace changes who you are becoming. Honesty lets the one you love see it.

Slide 21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Father, you know all of our story, and you have not turned away. Heal what others have done to us, and forgive what we have done. Give us the courage to tell the truth, and the grace to receive it without shame. Continue your renewing work in each of us, and guide us towards what is true and wise. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
