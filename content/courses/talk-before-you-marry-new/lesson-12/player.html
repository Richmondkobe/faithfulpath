Lesson 12 · Book chapter 6, Room 7 · Part 3: Walk Through the Nine Rooms

Room 7 · Past Relationships

What from the past needs to be known — and what has grace changed?

Colossians 3:9–11

“Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator.”Colossians 3:9–10 (ESV)

“Here there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all.”Colossians 3:11 (ESV)

A new way of speaking

  • Paul writes to a church: new life changes how believers speak to one another
  • “Do not lie to one another”
  • “Put on then… compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience…” Colossians 3:12–13

compassion kindness humility meekness patience

“…forgiving each other”

Applied here to a couple preparing for marriage. Truthfulness and compassion belong together.

A new creation

“Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come.”2 Corinthians 5:17

Your past does not have to be your identity. It does not mean the facts evaporate.

1. Not everything you carry is sin

Not everything you carry is sin.

grief betrayal responsibilities harm you were not responsible for

What was done to you is not your sin to confess.

Please do not hear your wounds called sins. They are not.

What usually goes wrong

Comparison

A competition with someone who is not in the room and cannot lose

Fear that the story repeats

A partner assessed on what somebody else did

Inherited fear

A previous betrayal can make trust feel difficult. Those feelings deserve understanding.

Past hurt can explain why trust feels difficult; it does not excuse controlling behaviour.

2. Relevance: what the past can and cannot tell you

  • Not: “Tell your partner absolutely everything.”
  • Not: “Your past is under the blood; don’t talk about it.”

Your partner deserves the information they need to make an informed decision about marrying you. They do not need every private detail.

What materially affects the marriage

  • A previous marriage
  • An existing child
  • Significant financial consequences
  • Ongoing legal or family ties
  • A serious pattern that could affect this marriage

“Is there anything my partner could discover later and reasonably say, ‘Why didn’t you tell me before we married?’”

A wish to know every private detail does not itself create a right to that detail.

Honest, not graphic

Honesty is not exhaustive disclosure. You can be truthful without being graphic.

“There are things about my past relationships that I believe you should know before we marry. I want to be truthful about anything that affects our future, but I don’t think describing every private detail would help either of us. I’m willing to discuss what matters, at a pace that is safe and respectful.”

Do not disclose something painful as a test of your partner’s love.

3. Disclosure: what is it for?

To the one hearing it: what am I actually afraid of?

  • Not being enough? The same thing happening again?
  • Trust, consequences that remain, or information I did not know?
  • Name the real concern.

To the one telling it: “Am I telling this because my partner needs to know, or because I need them to carry my guilt?”

Support beyond the relationship, from a pastor or counsellor, can help you both.

Disclosure, safely

  • Plan how to share what materially affects the marriage. You may need time, or qualified support.
  • Do not force a disclosure if it could put you at risk.
  • Ask what you need to know in order to decide, not for detail whose only function is to picture it.

4. Forgiveness without pretending · Psalm 32:1–5

This is about sin, not about wounds.

“Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. Blessed is the man against whom the Lord counts no iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no deceit.”Psalm 32:1–2 (ESV)

“For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long. For day and night your hand was heavy upon me; my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer. Selah”Psalm 32:3–4 (ESV)

“I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, ‘I will confess my transgressions to the Lord,’ and you forgave the iniquity of my sin. Selah”Psalm 32:5 (ESV)

Grace and honesty together

Silence was heavy. Confession brought relief.

Psalm 32 is about confession to God. It does not give us a list of what to tell a future spouse.

With “Do not lie to one another”, it reminds us that grace and truth belong together.

Two things, one for each of you

  • To the one with the history: grace received is not a substitute for honesty given.
  • To the one receiving it: do not use a disclosure as a weapon. Grace does not require pretending trust is already restored.

The principle, and the closing question

Tell the truth your partner needs in order to know who they are marrying.

  • Do not hide consequential realities.
  • Do not turn honesty into a demand for details that produce only comparison and shame.

“Now that you know this part of your partner’s story, can you make a free and honest decision about marrying them, without punishing them for it afterwards?”

Not spotless histories: no secrets that would have changed the decision, and no old relationships as new competitors.

You may forgive someone and still decide that marriage is not the right step. A decision not to marry is not automatically unforgiveness. Nobody should use grace to pressure you into saying yes.

The conversation · share what you freely choose

  • Relevance (alone, first, on paper): is there anything in my past that materially affects this marriage?
  • What your history taught you: about yourself, what to expect, what to fear
  • The present: comparison? What does my jealousy actually want?
  • Any contact that materially affects the trust and commitments we are making? Anything I need to explain honestly?
  • Questions to ask yourself, not a demand for passwords or a phone.
  • Disclosure, planned and safe
  • The closing question (when it is safe, first on your own): can I decide freely, and commit not to shame or punish you?
  • “I need time” is better than a yes you cannot keep.

If you find a real difference

Jealousy or past hurt never justifies surveillance, isolation or control. Stop the joint exercises; seek confidential support on your own.
Undisclosed history

Not a difference, but information. Take days or weeks, not one evening.

Persistent comparison

Not something the other can fix by being better. Work through it with someone before the wedding.

Attachment to an ex

Say it now. It is far harder to discover after a wedding.

A demand for every private detail

May come from fear, but can also become pressure or control. Discuss what is genuinely relevant, without an interrogation.

Bringing it together

  1. Not everything you carry is sin
  2. Relevance: what your partner needs to decide
  3. Disclosure: honest, not graphic, and safe
  4. Forgiveness without pretending

No secrets that would have changed the decision, and no old relationships as new competitors.

Prepare on your own · Room 7

First step: one thing your partner needs to know, and one thing grace has changed

Write your answers to parts one to three privately. No written catalogue of your history is needed.

Nothing typed is saved

If either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part.

Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

Talk together

  • Share what you freely choose. You may pass or pause.

Record only the next steps you both agree are useful. Allow time for questions or support.
