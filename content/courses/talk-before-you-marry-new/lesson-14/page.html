Our Conflict Covenant

A starting set. Keep the commitments to safety, honesty and respect; adapt the practical details, and add what applies to you.

  • We will not use conflict to punish each other.
  • We will not threaten the relationship because we are angry.
  • We will tell the truth before resentment becomes silence.
  • We will take a break when we are overwhelmed, and we will come back.
  • We will protect private information while remaining free to seek wise, confidential support.
  • We will apologise without waiting for the other to apologise first.
  • We will not demand agreement as proof of love.

This covenant records our intentions. It does not replace accountability, appropriate support or the freedom to reconsider marriage. Print this page, and sign and date it on paper if you wish. Keep it with your Rules from Lesson 2. The page does not save it.

The closing question (when it is safe, consider it first on your own): Five years from now, when we have a disagreement we cannot quickly solve, what do you want to be able to say about how I treated you?

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · The ninth door

Welcome back. This is Lesson 14, and we open the ninth and last room of the house: conflict itself. The other eight rooms were subjects. This one is not. Conflict is not a topic you disagree about. It is what happens while you discuss the other eight.

So the question for this room is this: who are we going to be when conflict comes?

Slide 2 · Be angry and do not sin

Scripture: Ephesians 4:26–27, 31–32

Our passage is Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter 4, verses 26 and 27, and then verses 31 and 32.

“Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil.”

“Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice. Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.”

Slide 3 · Written to a church

Scripture: Ephesians 4:25–32; Psalm 4:4

Paul was writing to a church, about how believers speak and live with “one another”. Lesson 2 read this whole passage as the foundation for your rules. Here we apply the same words to a couple preparing for marriage, and we say so. When Paul writes, “Be angry and do not sin”, he is quoting a psalm. Psalm 4 says, “Be angry, and do not sin; ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent.”

Slide 4 · The word: be angry

Scripture: Ephesians 4:26

One word in that verse gives this room its shape. The Greek word translated “be angry” is orgizesthe. Paul uses the wording of the Greek translation of Psalm 4. Interpreters understand this command in slightly different ways. One common reading is that anger may arise, but it must not lead us into sin. Paul’s emphasis is clear: do not allow anger to become an occasion for wrongdoing, or to settle into bitterness. Then Paul adds, “do not let the sun go down on your anger.” And in verse 31, he describes anger that has settled in and grown: bitterness, wrath, clamour, slander.

Slide 5 · Euodia and Syntyche

Scripture: Philippians 4:2–3

Here are two people from the New Testament. Paul wrote, “I entreat Euodia and I entreat Syntyche to agree in the Lord.” They were not his opponents. They had “labored side by side with me in the gospel.” And Paul went on, “Yes, I ask you also, true companion, help these women.” He does not tell us what they disagreed about, and we should not guess. But it is a picture worth keeping: two faithful people, a real disagreement, and help asked for, without shame. Faithful people disagree. The question is what they do next.

Slide 6 · Point 1: Why this room is different

Our first point: why this room is different. The other eight rooms were subjects: money, sex, family, children, faith, roles, the past, and what you are marrying into. This room is what happens while you discuss them. So it is less another lesson, and more the two of you finally looking at your conflict as a pattern, from the outside.

Slide 7 · What usually goes wrong

Before we look at what usually goes wrong, one thing must be clear. This room is about ordinary conflict between people who can safely disagree. Abuse is not a shared communication loop. Responsibility for abusive conduct belongs to the person using it, and the language of “our cycle” must never be used to share out that blame.

Now, the obvious irony: talking about how you fight becomes a fight. “Whenever we argue, you shut down.” “I shut down because you attack me.” “I only get sharp because you never listen.” Here is one possible conflict cycle. One person raises something. The other becomes defensive. The first becomes more intense. The second withdraws. The first pursues harder. The second shuts down completely. Each can describe what they reacted to, and each is still responsible for their own actions. So the question is not, “Who is the problem?” It is, “What happens between us when something becomes emotionally difficult?”

Slide 8 · Fewer arguments is not the goal

The other mistake is to define a good marriage as one with fewer arguments. One couple argues often and repairs well. Another rarely argues, because one of them stopped speaking honestly two years ago. The second couple looks better, but may be facing a serious problem that is harder to see. What matters is not only how often you disagree, but whether both people are free to speak, and what happens during and after a disagreement.

Slide 9 · Point 2: Becoming observers

Our second point: becoming observers of your own marriage. What usually starts a difficult conversation between us? Who pursues, and who withdraws? Some couples fall into that pattern. Others escalate together, or both avoid the issue, and roles may change by topic. Describe your actual pattern rather than trying to fit a label. What do I do when I feel misunderstood? What do I do when I feel unsafe? What makes it escalate, and what usually brings it back down? Then, what happens to each of us physically when we start to become overwhelmed? A raised voice, sudden silence, tears, pacing, leaving the room, talking faster, going completely flat. Learning to recognise your own signals is what makes the break signal from Lesson 2 usable.

Slide 10 · Look at your own heart first

Scripture: James 4:1–3; Romans 12:18

James asks, “What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you?” That is a question to ask of your own heart, not a weapon to use on your partner’s. And not every disagreement comes from selfishness. Couples also disagree because of limited information, competing responsibilities, or legitimate differences. And Paul says, “If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all.” If possible. So far as it depends on you. Peace is your part to seek. It does not always depend on you alone.

Slide 11 · Point 3: What are we each trying to protect?

Our third point is a question that can help us understand ordinary conflict: what are we each trying to protect when we fight? One person may be protecting themselves from feeling controlled. The other, from feeling abandoned. One is fighting for respect; the other is fighting for safety. Once you see that, the behaviour starts to make sense, including your own. Understanding what each person is trying to protect can help explain ordinary conflict. It does not excuse intimidation, or make one person responsible for the other’s harmful choices.

Slide 12 · Repair, and knowing where you are

Then the repair questions. How long do our unresolved conflicts stay unresolved? Who usually moves first towards repair? Is it always the same one? If the same person always repairs, that is not a personality difference. It is an arrangement, and the one who always moves first will eventually get tired. Can I say, “I was wrong”? Can I change my position after the conversation has started? And finally, when a difficult conversation ends, do we both know where it has reached? Many couples stop talking without resolving anything, and treat the silence as resolution. You need a way to recognise where the conversation has reached. Perhaps the issue is resolved. Perhaps you have agreed on a next step, or a time to return to it. Ending the conversation does not always mean the matter is settled, and neither person should be pressured to say that it is.

Slide 13 · Point 4: Your Conflict Covenant

Our fourth point: your Conflict Covenant. Lessons 1 to 5 taught you skills. This room asks something larger: who are we going to be when conflict comes? That is not a technique. It is an identity, and it is decided in advance, or it is decided by whoever is angriest at the time. So each of you completes three sentences, alone. When I am hurt, my instinct is to. What I need my spouse to remember about me during conflict is. And, the person I want to become when we disagree is. The third one is the important one. The first two describe who you are. The third describes who you are choosing to become, and it is the only one you have any control over.

Slide 14 · A starting set

Then, together, you write your covenant. Here is a starting set. We will not use conflict to punish each other. We will not threaten the relationship because we are angry. We will tell the truth before resentment becomes silence. We will take a break when we are overwhelmed, and we will come back. We will protect private information while remaining free to seek wise, confidential support. We will apologise without waiting for the other to apologise first. We will not demand agreement as proof of love. Keep the commitments to safety, honesty and respect. Adapt the practical details, and add what applies to the two of you.

Slide 15 · The closing question

One question closes this room. When it is safe, consider it first on your own, and share it if you choose. Five years from now, when we have a disagreement we cannot quickly solve, what do you want to be able to say about how I treated you? The goal was never a couple who never disagrees. It is a relationship in which both people can speak honestly, and take responsibility when they cause harm. So the sentence you are building towards is not, “We never had conflict.” It is, “Even when we disagreed, I knew I was safe with you.”

Slide 16 · If you find a real difference

Some differences here can be worked through with patience. But intimidation, threats, or using conflict to punish or control is not an ordinary difference. If either of you is afraid, stop the joint exercises and seek confidential support on your own.

Now the differences that can be worked through. If talking about how you fight becomes a fight, use the break signal, and come back at the time you agreed. If the same person always repairs, name the arrangement, and agree how the other will move first next time. If silence is treated as resolution, agree whether the issue is resolved, paused or needs another conversation. If one of you has stopped speaking honestly because difficult conversations are routinely avoided, acknowledge the pattern and consider appropriate support together. But if silence comes from fear, threats or intimidation, do not raise it through the joint exercise. Seek confidential support independently.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together: the whole house

Let us bring it together. This room is what happens while you discuss the others. Become observers of your own pattern. Ask what each of you is trying to protect. And decide in advance who you will be, in your Conflict Covenant. You have now walked through all nine rooms of the house. The goal is not a couple who never disagrees. It is a couple who knows how they fight, what they refuse to do to each other, and how they find their way back. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise. First, on your own, prepare for the room. Begin with one small step: complete the sentence, “The person I want to become when we disagree is.” Keep your inventory from Lesson 3 beside you, as your own private reference. Then complete the other two sentences, and the observer questions about your pattern, your signals and repair. These answers are yours. Nothing you type on the lesson page is saved.

Slide 19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: review your rules from Lesson 2, and allow 45 to 60 minutes. Share what you freely choose from your three sentences. Ask questions; do not argue. You may pass on a question, stop earlier, or agree when to continue. Then write your covenant together, and sign and date it on paper if you wish. Keep it with your Rules from Lesson 2. That page told you what to do. This one says who you are.

Slide 20 · Remember

Scripture: Ephesians 4:26, 32

Remember these words of Scripture. “Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger.” And, “Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.” Anger will come. Kindness and forgiveness are chosen.

Slide 21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Father, you know how we speak when we are hurt. Keep our anger from becoming sin, and our silence from becoming bitterness. Teach us to move first towards repair, and to say, “I was wrong.” Make us people who are safe for each other, and guide us towards what is true and wise. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
