Room 9 · Conflict Itself

Who are we going to be when conflict comes?

Ephesians 4:26–27, 31–32

“Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil.”Ephesians 4:26–27 (ESV)

“Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice. Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.”Ephesians 4:31–32 (ESV)

Written to a church

  • Paul writes to a church about how believers speak and live with “one another”
  • Applied here to a couple preparing for marriage
  • “Be angry, and do not sin; ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent.” Psalm 4:4

The word: be angry

ὀργίζεσθε

orgizesthe · “be angry”

A command form, in the words of the Greek translation of Psalm 4:4

Anger must not lead us into sin or settle into bitterness.

Interpreters understand this command in slightly different ways.

Euodia and Syntyche (Philippians 4)

“I entreat Euodia and I entreat Syntyche to agree in the Lord. Yes, I ask you also, true companion, help these women, who have labored side by side with me in the gospel”Philippians 4:2–3

Two faithful people, a real disagreement, and help asked for

Paul does not tell us what they disagreed about.

1. Why this room is different

The other eight rooms were subjects. This room is what happens while you discuss them.

Look at your conflict as a pattern, from the outside.

What usually goes wrong

This is for ordinary conflict between people who can safely disagree. Abuse is not a shared communication loop. Responsibility for abusive conduct belongs to the person using it.
  • “Whenever we argue, you shut down.”
  • “I shut down because you attack me.”
  • “I only get sharp because you never listen.”

One possible conflict cycle

  1. One raises something
  2. The other becomes defensive
  3. The first becomes more intense
  4. The second withdraws
  5. The first pursues harder
  6. The second shuts down

Not “Who is the problem?” but “What happens between us when something becomes emotionally difficult?”

Fewer arguments is not the goal

  • One couple argues often, and repairs well.
  • Another rarely argues, because one stopped speaking honestly.
  • What matters: whether both are free to speak, and what happens during and after a disagreement.

2. Becoming observers of your own marriage

  • What usually starts a difficult conversation? Who pursues, and who withdraws?
  • Some escalate together, or both avoid. Roles may change by topic.
  • What do I do when I feel misunderstood, or unsafe? What makes it escalate, and what brings it back down?
  • What happens to each of us physically when we become overwhelmed?

Look at your own heart first

“What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you?”James 4:1

A question for your own heart, not a weapon for your partner’s. Not every disagreement comes from selfishness: limited information, competing responsibilities, legitimate differences.

“If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all.”Romans 12:18

3. What are we each trying to protect?

What are we each trying to protect when we fight?

from feeling controlled from feeling abandoned respect safety

This can explain ordinary conflict. It never excuses intimidation, or makes one person responsible for the other’s harmful choices.

Repair, and knowing where you are

  • How long do our unresolved conflicts stay unresolved?
  • Who usually moves first towards repair? Always the same one? That is an arrangement.
  • Can I say “I was wrong”? Can I change my position?
  • Agree whether the issue is resolved, paused or needs another conversation. No one is pressured to say it is settled.

4. Your Conflict Covenant

Our Conflict Covenant

Not more techniques: who you intend to be when it is hard

  1. When I am hurt, my instinct is to…
  2. What I need my spouse to remember about me during conflict is…
  3. The person I want to become when we disagree is…

The third is the only one you have any control over.

A starting set

  • We will not use conflict to punish each other.
  • We will not threaten the relationship because we are angry.
  • We will tell the truth before resentment becomes silence.
  • We will take a break when we are overwhelmed, and we will come back.
  • We will protect private information while remaining free to seek wise, confidential support.
  • We will apologise without waiting for the other to apologise first.
  • We will not demand agreement as proof of love.

Keep the commitments to safety, honesty and respect. Adapt the practical details, and add what applies to you.

The closing question

“Five years from now, when we have a disagreement we cannot quickly solve, what do you want to be able to say about how I treated you?”

When it is safe, consider it first on your own. Share it if you choose.

Not “We never had conflict,” but “Even when we disagreed, I knew I was safe with you.”

If you find a real difference

Intimidation, threats, or using conflict to punish or control is not an ordinary difference. Stop the joint exercises; seek confidential support on your own.
Talking about fighting becomes a fight

Use the break signal, and come back at the time you agreed.

The same person always repairs

Name the arrangement, and agree how the other will move first next time.

Silence treated as resolution

Agree whether it is resolved, paused or needs another conversation.

Honesty stopped through avoidance

Acknowledge the pattern, and consider appropriate support together.

Silence that comes from fear

Do not raise it through the joint exercise. Seek confidential support independently.

  4. Decide in advance who you will be: your Conflict Covenant
