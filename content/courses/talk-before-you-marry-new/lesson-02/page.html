Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Make honesty safe

Welcome back. Lesson 1 ended with a question that many couples carry quietly: are we going to make it? In that lesson, you named what you have been avoiding. Before you start talking about any of it, there is something to build first: the ground those conversations stand on.

So this lesson is about building a relationship that can hold disagreement, where that question stops being live every time you disagree. And it asks a simple question: how do we make it safe to tell the truth before we ask for it?

2 · Contract or covenant?

Start with what you actually think marriage is. Not what you would say in church, but what you assume when the room gets tense.

A contract normally defines particular obligations and conditions. That has its place, but marriage is more than an exchange of services. If, underneath it, a marriage is treated as an exchange, honesty becomes expensive: if my partner is disappointed in me, the arrangement itself may feel in question, and the safest thing I can do is keep them satisfied. That is how you end up with the six patterns from Lesson 1.

Scripture describes marriage as a covenant: a solemn commitment of faithfulness before God. That commitment calls husband and wife to seek one another’s good, including when life becomes difficult. It makes room for sentences like “This hurt me”, “I don’t agree with you”, and “I’m not walking away because we had a difficult conversation.” For now, as an engaged couple, you are preparing for that covenant. You are not yet bound by it, and you remain free to reconsider your decision to marry.

3 · What covenant never means

That needs guarding straight away, because covenant has been misused. Covenant does not mean tolerating abuse. It does not mean staying silent about wrongdoing, or absorbing harm and calling it faithfulness. A commitment that requires one person to be destroyed quietly is not covenant. It is captivity wearing the language of covenant, and God is not honoured by it.

That is why engagement is a time of discernment. A promise to speak respectfully must never become a promise to marry whatever you discover, and you remain free to postpone or to end it. Now we open the Scriptures.

4 · Speak the truth

Our passage is Ephesians chapter 4, verses 25 to 32. We will read it whole.

“Therefore, having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor, for we are members one of another. Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil. Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need.”

5 · Words that build up

“Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear. And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption. Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice. Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.”

6 · Members one of another

Notice Paul’s reason for telling the truth: “we are members one of another.” Honesty is not only a private virtue. It is how a shared life holds together. Paul was speaking to believers who belonged to one body, the church. His instruction was not written specifically for engaged couples. Yet the same call to truthful, gracious speech matters deeply when two Christians are preparing to marry.

The prophet Malachi describes a wife as “your companion and your wife by covenant.” He was rebuking husbands for breaking faith with their wives, so he shows us what covenant faithfulness looks like by naming its opposite. Covenant is faithfulness that does not end when it becomes costly. That is the ground we are building.

7 · Building up

One word in that passage gives the lesson its shape. The Greek word behind “building up” is oikodomē, meaning building or edification. It comes from the world of construction and is related to the word for a house. In this verse Paul uses a form of that word to describe speech that strengthens rather than damages others, and he often uses it for the way believers strengthen one another.

So Paul is asking a builder’s question about our words. Not only, “Is this true?”, but, “Will this build?” Corrupting talk is speech that rots and tears down. Building talk is true, it fits the occasion, and it gives grace to the one who hears it. That does not mean avoiding hard truths. It means choosing words to build the other person, not to win.

8 · Point 2: The rules you agree on first

Every couple has rules for conflict. Most have never said them out loud. They were set by whoever escalated hardest the first few times, or by whoever gave in fastest, and then they hardened into habit. The alternative is to decide them deliberately, now, while nothing is at stake. You do not build the safety during the difficult conversation. You build it beforehand.

Underneath everything is one sentence: we are going to make it safe to tell the truth before we ask each other to tell the truth. In the book there are eleven rules in three groups. Here they are, in short. You will make them your own in the exercise.

9 · How we speak

The first group is how we speak. One: we will tell the truth without trying to wound. Honesty is the goal; punishment is not. Two: we will keep to one issue. A disagreement about the weekend does not become a review of the whole relationship. Three: we will speak from our own experience. “I felt ignored when you took that call” can be answered. “You never care about me” cannot.

And four, which belongs to Christian couples in particular: we will not use Scripture as a weapon. Do not quote a verse to end a conversation, win a point, or put your partner where disagreeing with you means disobeying God. Scripture is for pursuing truth, humility and repentance, including your own.

10 · How we handle tension

The second group is how we handle tension. Five: we will listen before we defend. Your explanation will still be there in five minutes. Your partner’s willingness to tell you the truth may not be. Six: either of us can ask for a break, and we will agree when to check in. You might say, “I need thirty minutes. Can we check in at eight?” Do not follow, block, touch or keep messaging to force someone to continue. A safety concern always comes first.

Seven: we will not use silence as punishment. Taking time to cool down is care. Withdrawing to make the other person suffer is not. And eight: we will not use threats of separation to win an argument. That does not stop anyone from leaving an unsafe situation, or from honestly reconsidering the engagement with outside counsel.

11 · What we do afterwards

The third group is what we do afterwards. Nine: we will not confuse explanation with excuse. “This is why I reacted that way” is worth saying. It is not the same as “So I was entitled to hurt you.” Ten: we will seek support without recruiting people to shame each other. Choose trustworthy people who help you think clearly, and remember that either of you is always free to seek confidential help; no privacy agreement may be used to isolate a partner. And eleven: we will repair the harm we cause.

Read those eleven and you will notice that most of them are not about arguing well. They are about making it safe to be told something you do not want to hear.

12 · Point 3: Choosing the moment

Our third point is choosing the moment. Here is a version I have heard often. One of you comes home carrying something from earlier in the day. It is a quarter to midnight. Both are tired. One has work in the morning. And someone says, “We need to talk about something that’s really bothering me.”

Unless it is urgent, agree on a time when you can both give it proper attention. Choose the moment when you are both most capable of hearing it, not the moment when you are most desperate to say it. But be careful, because avoidance loves to dress up as good timing. Compare “Let’s talk about it later” with “This matters to me, and I don’t want us to argue about it while we’re this tired. Can we talk tomorrow after dinner?” The second names the subject, the reason, and the time. And then you have to keep the appointment, or your partner will learn that later means never.

13 · Point 4: Repair

Our fourth point is repair. Sometimes the rules will not hold. A disagreement about money gets hot. He says, “You’re impossible to talk to.” She says, “And you’re selfish.” Nothing is resolved, and they go to bed angry.

The next morning, one of them has a decision to make, not about the money but about the night before. They can behave as though it did not happen and wait for the atmosphere to clear. That works, in the sense that by lunchtime things feel normal. But it does not remove anything. It files it. Or they can repair, which might sound like this: “I was angry last night, and calling you selfish was wrong. I don’t want to speak to you that way. I still think we have to sort out the money, but I want us to do that conversation differently.”

Notice that she has not dropped the concern. Repair separates the problem from the damage done while trying to solve it. You can be right about the money and still owe an apology for how you spoke at eleven at night.

14 · Four steps of repair

Four steps can help you begin. Acknowledge: something went wrong; name it, without explaining it yet. Own your part: your part, not a summary of theirs. “I’m sorry I shouted, but you…” is not an apology; it is a charge with an apology in front of it. Reconnect, if welcome, without demanding reassurance, touch or affection in return. And return to the issue, so that repair does not become a way of burying the subject warmly.

Repair does not have to wait for resolution. You can say, at any point, “We have not resolved the problem. But we can take responsibility for the harm we caused while discussing it.” And remember that forgiveness, reconciliation and restored trust are related but not the same. Grace does not erase consequences. Trust needs evidence of change, not only an apology.

15 · Go first

Jesus gave his own picture of repair. “So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift.”

Notice who moves: the one who remembers that someone has something against him. Jesus puts reconciliation before worship. So in an ordinary disagreement, do not always wait for the other person to make the first move. If you recognise that you have caused harm, take responsibility for your part. But Jesus’ call to reconciliation is not a command to return to intimidation or abuse. Where there is fear or coercion, safety and appropriate help come first.

16 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Marriage is a covenant, not merely a contract: prepare now to seek each other’s good, and remember that covenant never means captivity. Words that build up: true, fitting, and full of grace. Eleven rules, agreed before you need them. And when it goes badly, as it sometimes will, choose your moment and repair. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

17 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise. First, on your own, answer three questions. Which of the eleven rules will be hardest for me personally to keep? Everyone has one. If you cannot find yours, look at the last argument you had, with anyone. Which rule do I most need my partner to keep, and why? And is there something not on the list that I need in order to feel safe telling the truth? Not raising your voice in the kitchen. Not being followed from room to room. Not having the conversation over text. These are not unreasonable requests, and they are much easier to make now than to explain later.

18 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: share what you choose from your answers. Then go through the eleven rules one at a time and put them into words you will remember. Keep their protections: consent, the freedom to pause, access to outside help, and protection from intimidation are not optional. Put your own additions at the top of the list, not the bottom. Agree how you will pause, and choose a neutral signal, such as “I need a minute”, that the other always honours. Then write down the rules you have agreed upon. If you both freely wish to, you may sign and date the page as a reminder of your intentions. Signing is optional, and neither person should be pressured to do so. It is not a legal contract, or a promise to marry. And set a reminder for six weeks from now, to ask which rule you have already broken, and which one turned out to be the one you needed.

19 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “only such as is good for building up.” And, “Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you.” We forgive because we have been forgiven, and we build because we have been built up in Christ.

20 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Lord God, you have bound yourself to us in faithful love, and in Christ you have forgiven us. Make our words true, and make them build. Guard us from using your word as a weapon, and from using silence or threats to win. Give us courage to repair, and wisdom to recognise what is good, what must change, and when we need help. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
