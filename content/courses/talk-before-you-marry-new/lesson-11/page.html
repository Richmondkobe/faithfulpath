  1. Your Tuesdays. When you are both ready, share the parts of your ordinary Tuesdays that you are comfortable discussing. You do not have to hand over your entire private preparation. Listen first, then ask questions to understand what each person expects.
  2. The words. Headship, submission, partnership, each with an example. Keep using examples until you are sure you mean the same thing.
  3. The tasks. Money · cooking · cleaning · repairs · car · bills and admin · children’s schooling · doctors and appointments · each set of parents · social arrangements · church commitments. A job offer in another city · one of us wants to leave our church · one of us wants to stop working when a child arrives and the other did not expect that · one of us wants to lend a significant sum to a family member. For each: who decides, and what does the other do if they disagree?

Transcript and Scripture references

1 · Room 6: Roles

Welcome back. This is Lesson 11, and we are in the sixth room of the house: roles. In Lesson 2 you agreed rules for hard conversations, and one of them was this: we will not use Scripture as a weapon. In this room, that rule is tested more than anywhere else.

So let me say at the start what this lesson will and will not do. It will look carefully at what the Bible says about husbands and wives. It will explain fairly how sincere Christians read it. It will not choose a side for you. And then it will spend much of its time on the question that matters most on an ordinary day: when we use the same words, do we mean the same marriage?

2 · Same words, two pictures

Two people can agree entirely on the phrase “biblical roles” and be holding two completely different pictures of what their marriage will look like.

One says “headship” and means: the husband carries a particular responsibility for the family, and should lead by serving. The other hears “headship” and understands: the husband gets the final decision whenever we disagree. One hears “submission” and thinks: I gladly respect and support my husband’s leadership. Another hears: my opinion is welcome, but in the end I do what he decides. Even partnership is slippery. For one person it means complete equality of authority. For another, shared responsibility, with the husband still holding a distinct role.

So do not assume that using the same theological words means you have the same marriage in mind.

3 · Tasks, then the final say

Roles and authority also get tangled together. The conversation starts with tasks: who cooks, who pays the bills, who handles the children, who deals with the car. Then, without anyone noticing, it becomes a different question: who has the final say?

A couple agree that he will handle the money and she will run the home. Then a major financial decision arrives, and they disagree. Who decides? They agree that both will work. Then a child arrives, and one of them expected the other to step back. Who decides that? Those are the questions where the real difference lives, and they are rarely asked until they cannot be avoided. Before we get to them, we open the Scriptures.

4 · Submitting to one another

Our passage is Ephesians chapter 5, verses 21 to 33. It is a longer reading, so we will take it in three parts.

“submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ. Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife even as Christ is the head of the church, his body, and is himself its Savior. Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands.”

5 · As Christ loved the church

“Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish. In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies. He who loves his wife loves himself. For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church, because we are members of his body.”

6 · This mystery is profound

“‘Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.’ This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church. However, let each one of you love his wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband.”

7 · Life in the Spirit

This letter was read aloud to the whole church. Just before our passage, Paul tells them to “be filled with the Spirit”, and describes what that looks like: speaking to one another in psalms and songs, giving thanks, and submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ. Our passage belongs to that description of life in the Spirit. It is not a stand-alone rulebook.

Notice, too, who Paul speaks to. He speaks to wives directly, and to husbands directly, as people who answer to Christ for themselves. And what he asks of husbands is love in the pattern of the cross: “as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her.”

8 · Submitting: the word and verse 22

Now one word, because this is where the readings begin to divide. In verse 21, “submitting” translates the Greek hypotassomenoi, a form of the verb hypotassō, which means to submit, to be subject. It can picture arranging oneself under another. In the Greek text used by most modern critical editions, verse 22 has no separate verb. It reads, quite literally, “wives, to your own husbands, as to the Lord”, and its meaning is understood from the participle in verse 21. That is why the English Standard Version supplies the word submit in verse 22. Some ancient copies of the letter do include a verb there.

This connects the instructions: Paul’s words to wives grow out of his words to the whole church. But Christians still differ over how that connection should be understood. The grammar alone does not settle the debate.

9 · What the text says, and the word “head”

So what does the text clearly say? Wives are called to submit to their own husbands, as to the Lord. The husband is called the head of the wife, as Christ is the head of the church. Husbands are called to love their wives as Christ loved the church, giving himself up for her. And the whole passage points beyond any one marriage, to Christ and the church.

The word head is kephalē. Many interpreters understand it here as a real responsibility and authority. Some egalitarian interpreters have argued that it carries a sense closer to source. That meaning is debated among scholars, and I report it as an argument, not as an agreed meaning of the word. And the debate is not settled simply by choosing one English word. We must ask how Paul uses head in this passage, and how his comparison with Christ and the church shapes its meaning.

10 · Two readings, fairly

Here, then, are the two main ways serious Christians read this passage. I will describe each as its own holders would want it described.

Those called complementarian read verse 21 as introducing the passage, and verses 22 to 33 as showing what submission looks like in particular relationships. They hold that God gives the husband a real loving responsibility to lead, always sacrificially and with his wife’s counsel, and that the wife’s respect for that leadership is part of her discipleship.

Those called egalitarian read verse 21 as governing the whole passage. They see Paul applying mutual submission within the households of his day, and they hold that in marriage significant decisions should be made together, with neither spouse holding a final say, and that serving one another in this way is part of their discipleship.

These are not just different words. They lead to different marriages. And they are held by Christians who love Christ and have read the same Scriptures carefully. Even within each reading, Christians differ about how these convictions work in practice. That is another reason to ask for examples rather than assume you agree.

11 · Shared ground, and four principles

Yet there is a great deal they share. Both readings agree that husband and wife are equal in dignity, and heirs together of the grace of life. Both agree that Christ’s self-giving love is the husband’s pattern. Both call for mutual love, respect and care. And both leave no room for selfishness, harshness or neglect.

And whatever you believe, four things are not negotiable in this course. First, Christ’s love is never an excuse for domination. Second, no interpretation of submission permits coercion or violence. Third, neither of you may use Scripture to silence a legitimate concern. And fourth, before you marry, you need to work out what your beliefs will mean in actual household decisions.

12 · A towel and a basin

Here is a picture both readings affirm. On the night before he died, Jesus, “knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands”, rose from supper, took a towel, and washed his disciples’ feet. Then he said, “If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet.”

Notice what John tells us he knew: all things had been given into his hands. Jesus did not set his authority aside to serve. He used it to serve. Whatever you believe about roles in marriage, that is the shape of every Christian kind of leadership. As Jesus said elsewhere, “even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve.”

13 · When you hold different positions

Now to the two of you. If you hold different positions, slow down. This is the room where your rule about Scripture is tested hardest. Nobody should arrive with a stack of verses intending to win. If Scripture becomes a weapon here, the other person can no longer disagree with you without seeming to disagree with God.

So take it in three steps. First, each of you explains your conviction without attacking the other’s: what do I believe Scripture teaches, and why? The other listens as you learned in Lesson 5: question first, repeat it back, then respond. Second, which passages or principles matter most to me, and how did I come to hold them? Some of us discover that we inherited a position rather than examined it. That is not shameful. It is worth knowing. Third, and this is the question that does the work: what would this belief actually require of my spouse in everyday married life?

14 · What I will not do

Let me tell you what I will not do. I will not tell one of you to become complementarian, and I will not tell the other to become egalitarian. A counsellor who settles this by declaring a winner has not helped anyone. My task is to help you understand what you each believe, and whether you can build a marriage with integrity around it.

But I will say one thing plainly. Whatever position you hold, any account of marriage that leaves one person unable to raise a concern, unable to disagree, or unable to be heard when something is wrong is not a biblical arrangement. Whatever words are used to describe it, it is a failure of the covenant. Complementarian marriages and egalitarian marriages can both be healthy. Neither can be healthy where honesty is unsafe.

15 · An ordinary Tuesday

And now the question I would put ahead of an hour of theology. If each of you described an ordinary Tuesday five years from now, what would your roles look like?

Picture it. Who gets up first? Who gets the children ready, if there are children? At four in the afternoon, where is each of you? Who cooks? Who notices that the bills are due? And who decides what happens on Saturday?

Write it separately, then compare. It is remarkable how often two people who agree perfectly in principle describe two different Tuesdays. A good deal of the time you will find that your disagreement is theological in language and practical in consequence, or that it is theological in language and makes almost no practical difference at all. Sometimes it does make a real difference. Better to know that now. And your Tuesday may also change because of illness, unemployment, caring for relatives or an unexpected responsibility. Talk about how you would adjust together.

16 · The decisions, and the test cases

Then come the decisions. What counts as a major decision in your marriage? How do you make one when you agree, and when you do not? Can either of you say no, and on what? And when a decision one of you made turns out badly, how is that raised?

That last question is worth more than most of the theology, because it shows whether your arrangement includes any correction, or whether leadership means never being answerable. Try it on real cases: a job offer in another city; one of you wanting to leave your church; a large loan to a family member.

And be clear about what saying no means. Not every disagreement gives either person an automatic veto over everything. But neither spouse should be forced, threatened or punished into agreement. Decisions affecting a person’s body, safety or fundamental wellbeing cannot be justified by appealing to headship or submission.

17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. The same words can hide two different marriages. Ephesians 5 calls the whole church to submit to one another, wives to submit to their own husbands as to the Lord, and husbands to love as Christ loved the church. Sincere Christians read it in two main ways, and they share a great deal of ground. And whatever you believe, it has to work on an ordinary Tuesday, with both of you free to speak.

18 · Prepare on your own

Here is your preparation, each of you on your own. First, write your ordinary Tuesday five years from now, from morning to night: who does what, who decides what, and where each of you is at four in the afternoon. Then write what you mean by three words: headship, submission and partnership. Give a practical example for each word. The example will help you discover whether you both mean the same thing. And finally, ask yourself honestly: what would my belief require of my spouse in everyday life?

19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: when you are both ready, share the parts of your ordinary Tuesdays that you are comfortable discussing. You do not have to hand over your entire private preparation. Listen first, then ask questions to understand what each person expects. Then go through the three words, each with its example. Then the household tasks, one at a time, deciding who does what, including the planning and remembering, not only the doing. Watch for the ones you assigned by default rather than by choice. Then the decisions and the test cases. You will not finish this in one evening, and you do not have to. And if you find that one of you cannot say no in your arrangement, whatever theology it is dressed in, stop there, and speak to someone outside the relationship.

20 · Remember

Remember these words of Scripture. “submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ.” And, “Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her.” Whatever you believe about roles, both of you are called to serve, and both of you answer to Christ.

21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Lord Jesus, you had all things in your hands, and you took a towel and washed your disciples’ feet. Teach us to use whatever strength we have to serve each other. Help us to understand what we each believe, and why. Keep us from using your word to win, and give us humility to listen. Show us what our convictions will mean on an ordinary day, and give us wisdom to recognise what we can build together, what must change, and when we need help. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
