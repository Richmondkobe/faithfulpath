For further study (optional)

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · The fourth door

Welcome back. This is Lesson 9, and we open the fourth room of the house: children. Two people can say, “We both want children,” and be holding an enormous disagreement between them without either of them knowing it. They agree on the destination, and never discuss the journey.

So the question for this room is this: what do we each picture when we say “we want children”?

Slide 2 · Unless the Lord builds the house

Scripture: Psalm 127:1–5

Our passage is Psalm 127.

“Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain. Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain. It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep.

“Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb a reward. Like arrows in the hand of a warrior are the children of one’s youth. Blessed is the man who fills his quiver with them! He shall not be put to shame when he speaks with his enemies in the gate.”

Slide 3 · A gift, not an achievement

Scripture: Psalm 127

Psalm 127 belongs to the Songs of Ascents, a collection traditionally associated with journeys up to Jerusalem. It begins with the house: unless the Lord builds it, the work is in vain. Then it speaks of children as “a heritage from the LORD”. Both the home and the family are the Lord’s work and his gift.

Notice what that means. Children are a gift, not an achievement, and not a measure of anyone’s faith or worth. And this psalm is not a promise that every couple will have children. Some faithful couples never do. The psalm’s first line holds for every home: the Lord is the true builder of the house.

Slide 4 · Bringing them up

Scripture: Ephesians 6:4; Deuteronomy 6:6–7

Scripture also speaks of the work of raising children. Paul writes, “Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.” And in Deuteronomy, God’s people are told, “You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise.”

Faith is passed on in ordinary life: at the table, on the road, at bedtime. Instruction without provocation. That is worth talking about before there is a child in the house.

Slide 5 · Hannah’s grief

Scripture: 1 Samuel 1:1–10

In 1 Samuel chapter 1 we meet Hannah and her husband, Elkanah. Hannah had no children, and year after year, when the family went up to worship, her husband’s other wife provoked her about it. Hannah wept, and she would not eat. Then she went to the house of the Lord: “She was deeply distressed and prayed to the LORD and wept bitterly.”

Hannah’s story does end with the birth of Samuel. But that is not a promise that every couple who prays will have a child. What it shows us is a woman carrying a grief, and bringing it to God.

Slide 6 · “Am I not more to you than ten sons?”

Scripture: 1 Samuel 1:8

And here is the moment that matters for this room. Elkanah says to her, “Hannah, why do you weep? And why do you not eat? And why is your heart sad? Am I not more to you than ten sons?”

Elkanah loved Hannah; the passage tells us that. He seems to be trying to comfort her, but his question could not remove the sorrow she carried. He could offer his love, but he could not give her the child she longed for. Two people in the same household can experience the same sorrow very differently. Couples still do this. The quieter one is not always the one who cares less. And the one who weeps does not always need an answer, only company.

Slide 7 · Where Christians differ

Christians differ about family planning in several ways, and it helps not to treat them as one. Questions about family size and timing often involve wisdom, health, circumstances and conscience, although some traditions also teach particular moral responsibilities here. Christian traditions differ more explicitly over contraception. Know your church’s teaching, and talk with your pastor; for medical questions, as in Lesson 7, talk with a qualified healthcare professional. And schooling involves practical decisions and convictions about how children should be formed in faith. Do not assume you know each other’s answers because you are both Christians. Strongly held convictions are often never stated.

Slide 8 · Point 1: Whether, when and how many

Our first point is whether, when and how many. Two people say yes to children, and then nothing further. Underneath, one pictures three; the other has always assumed two. Then timing, which catches more couples than anything else. “I’d like children fairly soon after we marry.” “I assumed we’d have a few years to ourselves first.” Both said yes. Neither said when. So name the years, not the vague intention.

And underneath the numbers is what children mean. For one person, family, continuity, the point of the whole thing. For the other, responsibility, financial pressure, or something closer to fear. Those meanings drive the practical positions, and they rarely get said out loud.

Slide 9 · Point 2: What parenthood looks like

Our second point is what parenthood looks like day to day, which is where most of the friction will actually occur. Will both of us work, and for how long? Who provides childcare, and what does it cost? Who gets up at night? Be honest. What happens when the child is ill and both of us have work? How do the household responsibilities shift? And what role do the grandparents have: occasional, or in and out of the house? Lesson 8 will help you here.

Slide 10 · How we were raised

Then ask the question that reveals more than the rest combined: how was I disciplined as a child, and what would I repeat? What would I do differently? Most people discover that their firm parenting convictions came straight from their own childhood, either as replication or as reaction. Better to know before you are holding a tired two-year-old at eleven at night.

Before marriage, discuss whether you can agree on a clear commitment: to teach boundaries without hitting, threats, humiliation or shaming, using calm limits, fair consequences, and repair when you get it wrong. This course recommends that approach because children need correction that protects their dignity and safety. Paediatric guidance, such as that of the American Academy of Pediatrics, also recommends positive discipline. Discuss differences respectfully and privately. But if a child is being frightened, humiliated or harmed, step in calmly to protect them. A child’s safety always comes before a united front.

Slide 11 · Point 3: When it is difficult, or does not happen

Our third point needs to be said slowly. You can plan for children. You cannot control how parenthood unfolds. You may assume pregnancy will happen easily. It may not. You may face infertility, miscarriage, complications, disability, loss, or a child whose needs you never imagined.

Those experiences expose differences you did not know existed: in how you grieve, how you pray, how you cope, what you can afford, and what you believe God is doing. If pregnancy does not come, you need to be able to grieve together, rather than looking for whose fault it is. Grief does not look the same in two people. The quieter one is not necessarily indifferent. Remember Elkanah and Hannah.

Slide 12 · Whatever comes

And if you come to disagree about treatment, or adoption, or when to stop trying, those conversations need compassion rather than accusation. They are among the hardest a marriage can face.

So do not only discuss the child you hope for. Discuss how you will treat each other when reality does not match the picture. The deeper preparation is being able to say, and mean: “We don’t know what parenthood will bring. Whatever comes, we will not turn on each other because it didn’t match what we imagined.” That promise means meeting disappointment with compassion, not blame. It never means accepting intimidation, abuse or harm, or staying silent when someone needs protection. The aim of this room is to become a couple who can face whatever comes, and stay on the same side.

Slide 13 · Point 4: If you do not plan to have children, or one of you changes your mind

Our fourth point is for couples who have agreed not to have children, and for everyone, because minds can change. Do not skip this room. If you do not plan to have children, how will you discuss an unexpected pregnancy, or a genuine change of mind, without pressure?

It happens. “You promised me we’d have children.” “I know what I said when we were engaged. I don’t feel the same way now.” There is no clean answer to that. So, one instruction for now: do not make a promise about children because you are afraid of losing the relationship. That is people-pleasing, from Lesson 1, on the highest-stakes subject there is. And do not assume marriage will settle it. If one of you definitely wants children and the other definitely does not, that is not a communication problem to work out after the wedding. It may be a genuine incompatibility, and it deserves to be faced honestly while facing it is still possible.

Slide 14 · The conversation

The conversation comes in five parts. Share what you freely choose, and you may pass on a question or return to it later. Part one is the picture. Do I want to become a parent, remain without children, or am I uncertain? How many, and when? What does our life look like the year after the first child arrives? Describe it out loud. Part two is the practical: work, childcare, nights, grandparents, and illness. Part three is how we were raised: what would I keep, and what would I never repeat? What kind of father or mother do I want to be, and what kind am I afraid I might be? Part four is what if it doesn’t go to plan. What if it takes years? What if it never happens? How do I feel about fertility treatment, and adoption? What if our child has needs we never anticipated? How do I grieve, and what do I need from you when I’m grieving?

Slide 15 · Part five: The hard one

Part five is the hard one: “Am I saying yes to children because I want them, or because I know you do?” When it is safe, consider the question honestly, first on your own. You may choose to share your answer, or return to it later. Better now than in the third year of marriage. If you do share uncertainty, let your partner listen without trying to persuade you, as you learned in Lesson 5. Honest uncertainty is not a betrayal.

Slide 16 · If you find a real difference

Some differences here can be worked through with patience. But pressure about pregnancy, contraception or becoming a parent is not an ordinary difference. If either of you is being pressured or controlled in this, stop the joint exercises and seek confidential support on your own.

Now the differences that can be worked through. Different numbers: two versus four may be negotiable, or may reflect something deeper. Do not assume one child will change anyone’s mind. Decisions about trying for pregnancy must be freely made, without pressure or interference with contraception. Different timing: name the years. Different parenting philosophies: discuss them respectfully and privately, and keep a child’s safety first. One wants children, one is unsure: ask what the uncertainty means, without assuming it is fear to be overcome, and avoid persuasion disguised as reassurance. One wants children, one does not: take this seriously as a possible fundamental incompatibility. Seek help to understand your choices, not to pressure either person into parenthood.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Talk about whether, when and how many, and name the years. Talk about what parenthood will look like, day to day. When it is difficult, grieve together. And make no casual promises. The aim of this room is not to predict every parenting problem. It is to become the kind of couple who can face the child you imagined, the child you did not imagine, and the possibility of no child at all, without losing the ability to talk, grieve, adapt and stay on the same side. Staying on the same side means compassion, never accepting harm. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 18 · Prepare on your own

Now the exercise. First, on your own, prepare for the room. Begin with one small step: write down one picture of family life you carry in your mind. Keep your inventory from Lesson 3 beside you. Then write your answers to the five parts: the picture, the practical, how you were raised, what if it doesn’t go to plan, and the hard one. Nothing you type on the lesson page is saved.

Slide 19 · Talk together

When you are ready, come together. But first, if either of you is afraid of how the other will respond, do not do the together part. Stop, and speak with someone you trust on your own first.

For everyone else: review your rules from Lesson 2, and allow 45 to 60 minutes. Work through the five parts, sharing what you freely choose. You may pass on a question, stop earlier, or agree when to continue. Before you leave the room, record each person’s present position on parenthood and timing. Do not treat uncertainty as agreement. And list any information or professional advice you need.

Slide 20 · Remember

Scripture: Psalm 127:1, 3

Remember these words of Scripture. “Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain.” And, “Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD.” Whatever your family becomes, the Lord is the builder of your home.

Slide 21 · Prayer

Let us pray. If you are watching together, you may like to pray this aloud together.

Father, you are the builder of every true home. Give us honest words about the family we hope for. When our plans are fulfilled, make us grateful; when they are not, keep us close to you and to each other. Teach us to raise any child you give us with patience, faith and love. In Jesus’ name. Amen.
