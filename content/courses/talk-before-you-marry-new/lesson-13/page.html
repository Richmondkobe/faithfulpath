For further study (optional)

Transcript and Scripture references

Slide 1 · The eighth door

Welcome back. This is Lesson 13, and we open the eighth room of the house: what you are marrying into. No one comes to marriage as a blank slate. Something existed before this marriage, and it will still be there on the other side of the wedding.

So the question for this room is this: what already has a claim on our marriage, and have we counted it?

Slide 2 · Count the cost

Scripture: Luke 14:28–30

Our passage is from Luke’s Gospel, chapter 14, verses 28 to 30.

“For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it? Otherwise, when he has laid a foundation and is not able to finish, all who see it begin to mock him, saying, ‘This man began to build and was not able to finish.’”

Slide 3 · What Jesus was teaching

Scripture: Luke 14:25–33

Great crowds were following Jesus, and he turned and told them plainly what it would cost to follow him. These words are about discipleship, not marriage. Here we draw one carefully stated application from the principle: wise people count the cost before they build. A marriage is built too, and something is already standing on the site.

Paul tells Timothy how the church should care for widows, and says that their families should “first learn to show godliness to their own household and to make some return to their parents.” And he adds, “But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith.” Paul is writing about the care of widows. But it shows that responsibility to family is part of faithful living, not an interruption to it.

Slide 4 · Ruth, Naomi and Boaz

Scripture: Ruth 2:11–12

Here is a family from the Old Testament. Naomi lost her husband and both her sons. Her daughter-in-law Ruth refused to leave her, and came with her to Bethlehem. When Boaz noticed Ruth gleaning in his field, he said, “All that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband has been fully told to me.” And he blessed her: “The LORD repay you for what you have done.” One thing to be clear about: the redeemer customs in the book of Ruth belong to ancient Israel. They are not a picture of modern engagement.

Slide 5 · A household that made room

Scripture: Ruth 4:13–17

When Boaz married Ruth, Naomi remained part of their family story. After Ruth gave birth, “Naomi took the child and laid him on her lap and became his nurse.” And the women of the neighbourhood celebrated what God had done for Naomi through Ruth. The passage does not tell us every detail of their household arrangements. But it gives us a picture of family responsibilities and relationships continuing into a new chapter of life. This is an encouragement, not a template for every marriage. But every couple has to decide, together and in the light, how the responsibilities they bring will fit into the life they are building.

Slide 6 · Point 1: Every couple has something in this room

Our first point: every couple has something in this room. A child. An ex. A parent who depends on you. A business. A debt. A promise made years ago. Marriage does not dissolve these things. It asks you to bring them into the light and decide together how they fit into the new covenant. Work through this room even if you think you have nothing here. Many couples discover something they had not thought to mention.

Slide 7 · Point 2: Six kinds of obligation

Our second point: there are six kinds of obligation, and they are not interchangeable. First, existing children and co-parenting. Second, ongoing financial support, such as maintenance or support for relatives. Third, responsibilities towards parents. Fourth, previous marriages, and whatever they left behind. Fifth, business commitments, perhaps with a partner from before. And sixth, significant ministry obligations. Each is different. A child is not a debt, and a parent is not a business. Your preparation takes each kind on its own.

Slide 8 · What a couple cannot change

Some of these obligations carry legal duties: custody, child support, court orders, contracts. A private agreement between the two of you cannot change them. So as you talk, separate the arrangements you can negotiate from the duties that need professional advice or a formal process. For questions about custody, support, previous divorce or property, speak with a qualified professional where you live.

Slide 9 · Co-parenting

The central case in this room is co-parenting. Where a child is involved, contact with their other parent may remain necessary: school decisions, transport, holidays, health and expenses. The first mistake is to think, “Once we are married, you won’t need to talk to your ex any more.” Do not assume marriage will end that contact. Then jealousy can arrive. Talk about what the communication is for, rather than treating every message as evidence of disloyalty.

The opposite failure is just as common. A parent uses “the child needs me” to justify almost unlimited access for the ex, and expects the new spouse to accept it without discussion, until the new spouse feels like a guest in their own marriage. Children need predictable care. They should never be made responsible for managing tension between adults. The child’s welfare, and any court-ordered arrangements, come first. Boundaries here are about roles, communication and decisions, not about controlling another person’s phone or relationships.

Slide 10 · The step-parent’s role

Two oversimplified pieces of advice often cause difficulty: “You’re the parent now,” and, “It’s not your child; stay out of it.” Neither produces a workable household. A step-parent becomes a significant adult in a child’s life, and that role has to be built deliberately. Early on, the child’s parent usually carries primary responsibility for discipline and major decisions, because trust and a workable caregiving role develop over time. A wedding does not create them, and a household agreement does not create legal authority. But the step-parent should not live in the home with no legitimate voice either.

To the parent: make room for your spouse’s concerns without making the child compete for care. To the step-parent: do not try to replace the child’s other parent. And above all, a child must never feel responsible for choosing between their parent and the new spouse.

Slide 11 · About the ex

One question has to be answered honestly: is this relationship genuinely about co-parenting, or is there still emotional attachment? Necessary communication about a child is not evidence of romantic attachment. But hiding conversations, discussing marital problems with the ex, seeking emotional comfort there, or letting the ex influence decisions that belong to the new marriage is a real boundary failure. Lesson 12 asked you to say any ongoing attachment out loud. Here is the principle. A child’s other parent may remain part of your family responsibilities, but they do not become a third decision-maker in your marriage. Necessary co-parenting communication should be respected. Questions about boundaries should be discussed openly, without surveillance or control, and without interfering with the child’s welfare or lawful arrangements. Decisions about the child may still need to be made jointly with the child’s other parent, according to their responsibilities and lawful arrangements. That is different from giving them authority over the new marriage.

Slide 12 · Point 3: Marriage does not cancel what came before

Our third point: a new marriage does not cancel previous obligations. Here is one example. A man has supported his elderly mother financially for years, and his partner learns during the engagement that he expects this to continue indefinitely. He says, “That’s my responsibility. I’d never abandon my mother.” She says, “I respect that. I also thought our income would mainly be for building our own household.” Neither of them is wrong. But there is a substantial expectation between them that was never negotiated.

Four questions cover all of it. What existed before this marriage that will still affect it? Who knew about it, and when? What does it require: time, money, emotional energy, space in our home? And then, what boundaries protect the marriage while the obligation is honoured?

Slide 13 · Point 4: Legitimate, but not unlimited

Scripture: Psalm 15:4

Our fourth point: a previous obligation can be entirely legitimate without having unlimited authority over your marriage. Psalm 15 describes the character of someone who may dwell with God, and among other things, it is someone “who swears to his own hurt and does not change.” A godly person keeps their word, even when it costs them. That is not about keeping a promise that requires wrongdoing or harm, and it does not give every past promise permanent authority over a marriage. But honouring a child, caring for an ageing parent and keeping a promise you made are all good. And none of them means your spouse has no voice in how they affect your shared life. The task is not to pretend the past does not exist. It is to make sure the past does not quietly govern the future.

Slide 14 · The conversation

The conversation comes in five parts. Share what you freely choose, and you may pass on a question or return to it later. Part one is the inventory. Each of you begins privately, using the six kinds of obligation: what do I bring into this marriage, and what does each require of me? When it is safe, share the information your partner needs to understand the commitments that will affect your marriage. You may use your sheet to guide the conversation, but you are not required to hand over your private notes. Part two is what has not been said. The one with the obligations asks, “What am I asking you to accept that I have never actually discussed with you?” The other asks, “What part of this concerns me most?” Name the real concern. Part three is co-parenting, if it applies: contact, holidays, school and medical decisions, house rules, and what you never say about the other parent in front of the child. Part four is money. What leaves our household every month for obligations from before? Could it increase, and who decides? What happens in a difficult year?

Slide 15 · Part five: The hard question

Part five is the hard question. When it is safe, consider it first on your own, and share it if you choose. What will loyalty to this marriage look like when my child’s needs and my spouse’s needs appear to conflict? The word appear is doing work there. A child having a genuine need does not automatically mean the spouse must lose. Some of these tensions can be worked through with patience, honest discussion and appropriate support. Others may reveal responsibilities that cannot be changed, or differences that need more time and careful consideration. The aim is not to make a child and a future spouse compete, but to understand what each person needs and what faithfulness requires. And if, after counting the cost, either of you needs more time, or decides not to go ahead, that is not failure. It is what counting the cost is for.

Slide 16 · If you find a real difference

Some differences here can be worked through with patience. But control, threats, or using a child against a partner is not an ordinary difference. If either of you is being controlled or threatened, stop the joint exercises and seek confidential support on your own. And take any concern for a child’s safety seriously.

Now the differences that can be worked through. Undisclosed obligations: if something in part one is new information, stop and take time with it. Different expectations about the step-parent’s role: agree on an initial role, and revisit it in six months, because it will change as trust builds. A financial obligation that neither of you has quantified: get the actual number, then decide together what happens if it grows. A child who is resistant to the marriage: listen without assuming the reason, and seek appropriate child or family support. And if one of you feels they have no standing in their own home, say it in this room. It does not improve on its own.

Slide 17 · Bringing it together

Let us bring it together. Every couple has something in this room. Name the six kinds, each on its own. A new marriage does not cancel what came before. And an obligation can be legitimate without governing your marriage. A child’s other parent may remain part of your family responsibilities, but not a third decision-maker in your marriage. Sit down and count the cost together, before you build. That is how two chairs turn, a little more towards each other.

Slide 20 · Remember

Scripture: Luke 14:28; Psalm 15:4
