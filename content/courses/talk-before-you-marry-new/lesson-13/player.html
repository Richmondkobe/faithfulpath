Room 8 · What You’re Marrying Into

What already has a claim on our marriage — and have we counted it?

Luke 14:28–30

“For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it? Otherwise, when he has laid a foundation and is not able to finish, all who see it begin to mock him, saying, ‘This man began to build and was not able to finish.’”Luke 14:28–30 (ESV)

What Jesus was teaching

  • Jesus is speaking about the cost of following him, not about marriage.
  • One carefully stated application: wise people count the cost before they build.

“…first learn to show godliness to their own household and to make some return to their parents.”1 Timothy 5:4

“But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith…”1 Timothy 5:8

Written about the care of widows: care for family is part of faithful living.

Ruth, Naomi and Boaz (Ruth 2)

“All that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband has been fully told to me”Ruth 2:11

“The Lord repay you for what you have done”Ruth 2:12

The redeemer customs in Ruth belong to ancient Israel. They are not a picture of modern engagement.

A family story continuing

“Then Naomi took the child and laid him on her lap and became his nurse.”Ruth 4:16

The passage does not tell us every detail of their household arrangements.

An encouragement, not a template for every marriage

1. Every couple has something in this room

a child an ex a parent who depends on you a business a debt a promise made years ago

Marriage does not dissolve these things. Bring them into the light, and decide together.

Work through this room even if you think you have nothing here.

2. Six kinds of obligation, not interchangeable

1 Existing children and co-parenting
2 Ongoing financial support
3 Responsibilities towards parents
4 Previous marriages
5 Business commitments
6 Significant ministry obligations

Each is different. A child is not a debt, and a parent is not a business.

What a couple cannot change

  • Some obligations carry legal duties: custody · child support · court orders · contracts
  • A private agreement between you cannot change them.
  • Separate what you can negotiate from what needs professional advice or a formal process.

For custody, support, previous divorce or property: a qualified professional where you live.

Co-parenting

  • Contact with a child’s other parent may remain necessary: school, transport, holidays, health, expenses.
  • Do not assume marriage will end that contact.
  • Talk about what the communication is for, rather than treating every message as disloyalty.
  • The opposite failure: “the child needs me” used to justify almost unlimited access, until the new spouse feels like a guest in their own marriage.
  • Children need predictable care, and are never made responsible for tension between adults.
  • The child’s welfare and any court-ordered arrangements come first. Boundaries concern roles, communication and decisions, not another person’s phone.

The step-parent’s role

  • Not: “You’re the parent now.” Not: “It’s not your child; stay out of it.”
  • A role built deliberately. Trust and a workable caregiving role develop over time.
  • A household agreement does not create legal authority.
  • To the parent: make room for your spouse’s concerns without making the child compete for care.
  • To the step-parent: do not try to replace the child’s other parent.
  • A child must never feel responsible for choosing between their parent and the new spouse.

About the ex

Is this relationship genuinely about co-parenting, or is there still emotional attachment?

Necessary communication about a child is not evidence of romantic attachment.

Hiding conversations, discussing marital problems, seeking emotional comfort there: a real boundary failure.

A child’s other parent may remain part of your family responsibilities, but does not become a third decision-maker in your marriage.

  • Respect necessary co-parenting communication. Discuss boundaries openly, without surveillance or control.
  • Decisions about the child may still be made jointly with the child’s other parent. That is different from authority over the new marriage.

3. A new marriage does not cancel previous obligations

  • “That’s my responsibility. I’d never abandon my mother.”
  • “I respect that. I also thought our income would mainly be for building our own household.”
  • Neither is wrong. But the expectation was never negotiated.

Four questions

  1. What existed before this marriage that will still affect it?
  2. Who knew about it, and when?
  3. What does it require: time, money, emotional energy, space in our home?
  4. What boundaries protect the marriage while the obligation is honoured?

4. Legitimate, but not unlimited

A previous obligation can be legitimate without having unlimited authority over the marriage.

“who swears to his own hurt and does not change”Psalm 15:4

Never a promise that requires wrongdoing or harm. No permanent authority for every past promise over a marriage.

Make sure the past does not quietly govern the future.

The conversation · share what you freely choose

  • The inventory: each of you begins privately, by the six kinds.
  • When it is safe, share what your partner needs to understand the commitments that will affect your marriage.
  • You are not required to hand over your private notes.
  • What has not been said: “What am I asking you to accept that I have never actually discussed with you?”
  • “What part of this concerns me most?” Name the real concern.
  • Co-parenting, if it applies: contact, holidays, decisions, house rules, what we never say about the other parent
  • Money: what leaves our household every month? Could it increase, and who decides? A difficult year?

Part five · The hard question

“What will loyalty to this marriage look like when my child’s needs and my spouse’s needs appear to conflict?”

When it is safe, consider it first on your own. Share it if you choose.

Not a competition between a child and a future spouse: what does each person need, and what does faithfulness require?

More time, or a decision not to go ahead, is not failure. It is what counting the cost is for.

If you find a real difference

Control, threats, or using a child against a partner is not an ordinary difference. Stop the joint exercises; seek confidential support on your own. Take any concern for a child’s safety seriously.
Undisclosed obligations

If something is new information, stop and take time with it.

The step-parent’s role

Agree on an initial role, and revisit it in six months.

An unquantified obligation

Get the actual number. Then decide together what happens if it grows.

A child resistant to the marriage

Listen without assuming the reason. Seek appropriate child or family support.

No standing in your own home

Say it in this room. It does not improve on its own.

Bringing it together

  2. Six kinds of obligation, each counted on its own
  3. A new marriage does not cancel what came before
  4. Legitimate, but not unlimited

Sit down and count the cost together, before you build.
