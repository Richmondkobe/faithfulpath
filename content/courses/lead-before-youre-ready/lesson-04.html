Welcome to Lesson 4, and to the second part of the course: Learn the Craft.

In this lesson you will learn why a general announcement may not be enough, what Jethro and Jesus can teach you about choosing people deliberately, and a seven-part invitation you can use this week.

Let's begin with Scripture. Exodus chapter 18, verse 21.

"Moreover, look for able men from all the people, men who fear God, who are trustworthy and hate a bribe, and place such men over the people as chiefs of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens."

Israel is camped in the wilderness, a few months out of Egypt, and Moses is drowning. Jethro, his father-in-law, visits the camp and watches Moses sit as judge from morning until evening while the people stand around waiting. His assessment is blunt: what you are doing is not good. You will wear yourself out, and the people with you. Then he gives the solution. Choose capable people, give them real responsibility, and let them judge the ordinary cases.

The Hebrew word translated "able" is chayil. It means strength, capability, worth. It is the same word used in Proverbs 31 for the woman of valour. Notice what Jethro does not say. He does not say, find people who are willing. He does not say, find people who are free on Thursdays. The criteria are character: capable, God-fearing, trustworthy, not greedy. And these people are selected, not volunteered. Moses goes and chooses. And they are given real decisions. Jethro does not suggest Moses keep all the authority and hand out the chores. A team that cannot decide anything is not a team. It is a set of errands.

One clarification before we go on. In its original setting, Moses was selecting men to serve as judges in ancient Israel. The principle we are drawing from the passage is that people entrusted with responsibility should be chosen deliberately, for character and capability. Apply that principle within your church's own theology, governance and eligibility requirements.

Jesus did the same thing more deliberately still. Luke 6 tells us he spent a whole night in prayer, and when morning came he chose twelve. Most of us choose in the corridor after a service. And Mark 3 adds the purpose: he appointed twelve that they might be with him, and that he might send them out. Being with him came first. He did not recruit twelve functionaries to cover the workload. He built people, and the work came out of that.

Now here is where most new leaders begin. You stand up, describe the need, say the ministry is desperate for help, and ask anyone who feels led to see you afterwards. Every new leader tries it once. Sometimes nobody responds. Sometimes someone responds whose gifts or availability do not match the role you had in mind. A general appeal leaves people unsure whether the role suits them or what it involves. So keep an open, fair route for people to express interest, and use the same role criteria and checks for everyone. Then let personal invitations complement that process. A clear, personal invitation is what most often builds a team, and done well it does not turn recruitment into a circle of favourites.

So who do you ask? Look for four things. Character first; Acts 6 set that bar even for food distribution. Faithfulness in something small already. Some evidence of the gift; match people to what they are good at, not to your empty slots. And teachability over talent. You can train a humble person with average ability. You cannot train someone who already knows. One more thing: do not recruit only people like yourself. It produces a team with one set of strengths and one enormous blind spot.

So pause here for a moment. Who in your church is faithfully doing something small that nobody notices? Take a moment before you go on.

Pause here. Take a moment before you go on.

Here is the tool for this week. I call it the seven-part invitation. It is unglamorous, and it works.

One: invite them personally and privately, one at a time. Not by group message. Not from the front. Two: ask for a specific role, not for help. "We need volunteers" gets nothing. "I'd like you to run the welcome team at the second service" gets an answer. Three: say why you asked them. This is the part new leaders skip, and it is the most powerful sentence in the conversation. "I've watched how you talk to people who come in on their own, and I think you'd be very good at this." People say yes to being seen. Four: give them the whole picture up front. The tasks, the hours, the commitment, the training, who they report to, and any eligibility criteria, references or safeguarding checks required before they begin. Those are part of the invitation, not details for later. Five: set an end date. "Would you take this for six months, and then we will both review it?" Six: let them say no. Say it explicitly: "It's completely fine to say no, and I'd rather have an honest no than a reluctant yes." Then mean it. Seven: give them time to answer. "Take a week, pray about it, talk with anyone affected. Let me know next Sunday."

And when they say no, take it graciously, and take it as information rather than rejection. Do not ask them to explain or defend it. Sometimes a no is about timing. Sometimes the role is simply not a fit. Accept the first clear no, and ask again only if they welcome a future conversation.

So this is your step. One conversation. Write down the approved open roles in your ministry, and beside each put the name of one potentially suitable person, not simply the most available one. Before you approach anyone, make sure the role is approved, that you have authority to recruit for it, and that you know the eligibility, reference and safeguarding requirements. Remember that a personal invitation is an invitation to explore the role, not a promise of appointment. Then choose one person and invite them this week, using all seven parts. Do not do all five at once. Do one properly, notice how it goes, and let it teach you before you do the next.

One truth to carry with you this week. People say yes to being seen. Invite the person, not the gap.

Help me to ask honestly, to give the whole picture, and to accept a no with grace. Build your people through this ministry, and let the work come out of that. Amen.

Take one step

One invitation. Seven parts.

Write down the approved open roles in your ministry. Beside each, write the name of one potentially suitable person, not simply the most available person.

Before approaching anyone, confirm that the role is approved, that you have authority to recruit for it, and that you understand the eligibility, reference and safeguarding requirements. A personal invitation is an invitation to explore the role, not a promise of appointment.

Choose one person and invite them to a personal, private conversation this week:

  1. For this invitation, speak with them personally and privately rather than relying on a public appeal or group message.
  2. Describe a specific role, rather than asking generally for help.
  3. Explain why you thought of them.
  4. Give the complete picture: tasks, hours, duration, training, supervision and any required checks.
  5. Set an end date and review point.
  6. Tell them it is completely acceptable to say no, and mean it.
  7. Give them time to consider their answer.

One conversation. Do one properly, notice how it goes, and let it teach you before you do the next.

If they say no: receive it graciously, as information rather than rejection. Do not ask them to explain or defend their decision. Revisit the invitation only if they welcome another conversation.
