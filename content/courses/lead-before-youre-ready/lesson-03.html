Welcome to Lesson 3.

In this lesson you will learn how unclear responsibilities create avoidable conflict in a first year of leading, what a Roman soldier's words illustrate about authority, and the four questions that will settle where your role begins and ends.

Let's begin with Scripture. Matthew chapter 8, verse 9. A centurion is speaking to Jesus.

"For I too am a man under authority, with soldiers under me. And I say to one, 'Go,' and he goes, and to another, 'Come,' and he comes, and to my servant, 'Do this,' and he does it."

This happens in Capernaum, a garrison town on the Sea of Galilee. A centurion commanded around eighty men, and he stood inside a chain of command that ran all the way up to Rome. He comes to Jesus about a paralysed servant. Jesus offers to come to the house, and the centurion says that is not necessary. Just say the word. Then he explains his reasoning, and this is the part to sit with.

The word he uses is exousia. In the New Testament, exousia is not raw power. It is delegated authority, the right to act because someone above you has given you that right. And notice the logic. He does not say, "I am a man with authority." He says, "I am a man under authority." His ability to give an order rests entirely on the fact that he stands inside a chain. When he says "go," what makes it work is not his personality. It is the empire standing behind him.

Jesus calls this the greatest faith he has found in Israel. Let me be careful here. Matthew 8 is primarily about the centurion's faith in Jesus' authority. His description of being under authority also gives us a useful illustration, but not a complete model, of how delegated responsibility works. A Roman army is not a blueprint for a church. But the soldier grasped something the religious professionals had missed.

Now apply that to yourself. Whatever authority you carry in your ministry is delegated. It came down to you, it is not generated by you, and it operates only within the area it was given for. That is not a demotion. It is the only kind of authority that holds weight.

Here is why this matters so much in your first year. Many first-year conflicts grow out of unclear responsibilities. You made a decision you thought was yours and discovered it wasn't. You waited for permission on something everyone assumed you would just handle. You told a volunteer something the pastor had already told them differently. You were criticised for a decision you had no part in making. Situations like those often reflect unclear boundaries rather than poor character, and boundaries can be drawn.

Scripture speaks to both sides of this. Hebrews 13:17 tells believers to submit to their leaders, because those leaders keep watch over souls as people who must give an account, and it adds a reason that is easy to skip: so that they may do it with joy and not with grief. Your pastor carries a weight of responsibility. Making that weight heavier costs everyone, including you. And 1 Peter 5 speaks to the other side: elders are to shepherd willingly, not lording it over those entrusted to them, but being examples. Leaders who serve without domination, and a community that cooperates with humility. Accountability belongs on both sides.

But let me be clear about what submission is not. Submission is not silence, and it is not agreement. In Galatians 2, Paul confronts Peter in Antioch, to his face, in public, because Peter's behaviour was pulling the church apart. Peter was senior to Paul in every visible way, and Paul still spoke. Acts 15 shows the whole church arguing out a serious dispute in the open, in the right room, and reaching a decision together. Honest disagreement, raised constructively, is part of a healthy church. What is not healthy is undermining decisions through gossip, or recruiting allies against someone. And one more thing: concerns about harm, coercion or misconduct are different again. Those follow a reporting route, and they should never be suppressed for the sake of appearances. You do not have to confront the person first, and you do not wait for an implicated leader's permission.

So pause here for a moment. Think of one decision you made in this role in the last month. Were you certain it was yours to make? Take a moment before you go on.

Here is the tool for this week. Four questions, in one twenty-minute conversation with whoever you report to.

Question one: what decisions may I make independently? Get real examples. Spending up to what amount, changing a meeting time, choosing songs, adding a volunteer.

Question two: what requires approval, and what requires prompt reporting? Spending, teaching, appointments, changes of role. Safeguarding concerns always follow the reporting process, whatever else is agreed.

Question three: what do you want to hear about even when I've handled it? A complaint, a volunteer quitting, a pastoral situation in the team.

Question four: how do you want me to raise a disagreement, and when?

So this is your step. Ask your pastor or ministry head for twenty minutes this week, and take the four questions with you. Write the answers down while you are there. Then email them back the same day: "Here is what I understand we agreed." A clear record prevents misunderstandings later. Record only the role agreement; keep names and sensitive pastoral details out of an ordinary email. And if an urgent concern needs attention, use the appropriate route now, rather than waiting for this meeting.

One truth to carry with you this week. Delegated authority is the only kind that holds weight. Know where yours begins and ends, and you will lead with far more freedom, not less.

Let me close with a short prayer.

Lord, thank you that I do not have to generate my own authority. Help me to serve gladly under the people you have placed over me, to speak honestly when I disagree, and never to stay silent where someone could be harmed. Give me clarity about my role this week, and peace inside its limits. Amen.

Take one step

Twenty minutes. Four questions.

Ask your pastor or ministry head for twenty minutes this week, and take these four questions with you:

  • What decisions may I make independently? Ask for real examples.
  • What requires approval, and what requires prompt reporting?
  • What do you want to hear about even when I’ve handled it?
  • How do you want me to raise a disagreement, and when?

Write the answers down while you are there. The same day, email them back: “Here is what I understand we agreed.” Keep that record in your church’s usual system, not in this course. Record only the role agreement; do not include names or sensitive details from pastoral or safeguarding situations in an ordinary email.

Do not wait for permission to report a safeguarding concern. Safeguarding concerns always follow your church’s reporting process, whatever else is agreed. If an urgent concern needs attention, use the appropriate route now rather than waiting for this meeting.
