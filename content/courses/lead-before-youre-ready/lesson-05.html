Read the transcript

Welcome to Lesson 5.

In this lesson you will learn why recruiting is the easy part, how Jesus trained people by sending them out and then debriefing them, and one conversation that will do more for your team than any appeal from the front.

Let's begin with Scripture. Ephesians chapter 4, verses 11 and 12.

"And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ."

Paul is writing to the church in Ephesus about how Christ has structured his body. And notice what leaders are for. They are given to equip the saints for the work of ministry. The emphasis is on preparing people to participate, not on keeping ministry in the hands of a few.

The word translated "equip" is katartismos. It comes from a verb used in Mark 1 for James and John mending their nets, and it was used by physicians for setting a broken bone. It means to restore something to working order, to fit it for its purpose. That is your job description with volunteers. Not to use them. To mend, fit and prepare them, so that they can serve well.

Now here is what actually happens in most ministries. People say yes, serve for a few months, drift to the back of the rota, and then quietly stop. Nobody has a conversation about it. The leader concludes the church is uncommitted, and starts asking for volunteers again. A ministry can look as if it has a recruitment problem when it really has a retention problem.

People leave for many good reasons: work, health, caring responsibilities, a move, a change of calling. But three preventable problems deserve your attention. They never knew exactly what was expected. Nobody ever said thank you in a way that landed. And they stopped growing: year three looked exactly like year one, and nobody asked what they wanted to become.

Look at how Jesus did it. Mark 6 says he called the Twelve and began to send them out two by two. He did not send them alone, and he did not send them empty-handed. Matthew 10 records the briefing he gave first: where to go, what to say, what to take, what to do when a town would not receive them. Then Mark 6:30 records what happened when they came back. The apostles gathered around Jesus and reported to him all they had done and taught. That is the pattern. Brief, send in pairs, debrief.

And when the seventy-two came back in Luke 10, thrilled that even the demons submitted to them, Jesus did not dismiss the joy. He redirected it: do not rejoice that the spirits submit to you, rejoice that your names are written in heaven. He debriefed the people, not just the results. He was interested in what serving was doing to them. Most of us send volunteers out with a vague description and never ask how it went. Then we wonder why they fade.

So here is the truth for this lesson. Recruiting someone is the beginning of leadership, not the end. If your only contact with your volunteers is about their tasks, you have a rota, not a team.

So pause here for a moment. Name the volunteer most at risk of quietly fading out in the next three months. What is actually going on with them? Take a moment before you go on.

Pause here. Take a moment before you go on.

Here is the tool for this week. It has two parts.

The first is the training sequence that actually transfers a skill. Four steps. I do it, you watch: explain what you are doing as you go, including the small judgement calls. We do it together. You do it, I watch: they lead, you say nothing unless something is going badly wrong, and you give feedback afterwards, privately. And finally, you do it, I'm available: you step out. You are reachable, but you are not standing there. Most leaders jump from step one to step four and call it delegation. The person fails, the leader takes the job back, and both conclude the volunteer wasn't up to it. And when you do hand something over, delegate the outcome, not just the task. "Make sure the room feels welcoming by nine-thirty" produces someone who notices the heating is off and fixes it. "Put out forty chairs" produces someone who puts out chairs. Agree the outcome, the limits and the standard first. Safety, accuracy and the responsible handling of money are never optional standards.

The second part is the review. Every role should have a review date, the six-month point from last week's invitation. At the review, ask three questions and mean all three. What part of this do you most enjoy? What part drains you? Do you want to continue, change, or stop? Make "stop" a genuinely acceptable answer. A volunteer who leaves well, thanked and blessed, tells other people that serving here is a good experience. A volunteer who leaves by disappearing tells them the opposite. And when someone isn't working out, never let a reliability or attitude problem run for months without a direct conversation. Consider whether a different role would fit better. But concerns about abuse, dishonesty or unsafe conduct go through the appropriate process. You do not transfer that kind of problem to another team.

So this is your step. Take the person you thought of a moment ago, and invite them to an unhurried conversation this week, in an appropriate setting, with no agenda about tasks. Tell them beforehand that you want to hear how serving is affecting them, not to assess their performance. Ask three things: what do you enjoy about serving? What has been difficult or draining? And, is there anything affecting your capacity that you would like me to know? Let them share only what they are comfortable sharing, and listen without trying to solve everything. Then thank them for one specific thing you have watched them do. Name the action, and name why it mattered. And if they tell you they want to stop, receive that without guilt or pressure. That single conversation will do more for your team than anything else in this lesson.

One truth to carry with you this week. You have a team when you know the people, not just the rota.

Take one step

One unhurried conversation. No task agenda.

Think of one volunteer who may be receiving tasks without enough attention, training or support. Invite them to an unhurried check-in this week, in an appropriate setting. Tell them that you want to hear how serving is affecting them, not assess their performance.

Ask three questions and listen:

  1. What do you enjoy about serving?
  2. What has been difficult or draining?
  3. Is there anything affecting your capacity that you would like me to know? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

Thank them for one specific thing you have watched them do. Name the action and explain why it mattered.

If they want to stop, receive their decision without guilt or pressure. Thank them and agree any necessary handover. If they want a lighter load or a temporary break, listen first and agree what happens next, involving your supervisor where approval or cover is needed.

Listen without trying to solve everything or investigate a disclosure. Do not promise absolute confidentiality. If they disclose harm or a safeguarding concern, explain what you need to do next and follow the appropriate reporting procedure.

Keep in mind: the four training steps and three review questions are in the transcript and the worksheet. This week’s step is only the conversation.

The four training steps

  1. I do it, you watch. Explain the small judgement calls as you go.
  2. We do it together.
  3. You do it, I watch. Feedback afterwards, privately.
  4. You do it, I’m available. Reachable, but not standing there.

The three review questions (at the six-month review): What part of this do you most enjoy? What part drains you? Do you want to continue, change, or stop?
