Welcome to Lesson 1.

In this lesson you will learn why feeling unqualified does not mean you were wrongly chosen, what God actually said to the leaders who argued with him, and one small step you can take now that could protect your leadership for years.

Let’s begin with Scripture. Second Corinthians chapter 3, verses 5 and 6.

“Not that we are sufficient in ourselves to claim anything as coming from us, but our sufficiency is from God, who has made us sufficient to be ministers of a new covenant.”

Paul wrote this letter to the church in Corinth, a wealthy, status-conscious port city, and he wrote it under attack. Some in that church were questioning whether he was qualified at all. In that world, travelling teachers arrived with letters of recommendation, and Paul had none to show. So a chapter earlier he asked the question every new leader eventually asks: “Who is sufficient for these things?” Chapter 3 is his answer.

The word Paul uses is hikanos. It means sufficient, adequate, enough. Enough for the task. And Paul, trained under Gamaliel, more familiar with the Scriptures than almost anyone alive, says plainly that he is not hikanos in himself. His sufficiency comes from God. If that is true of Paul, then the pressure you are feeling to be enough is a pressure God never put on you.

Let me take you to a story. In Exodus 3, Moses is eighty years old and keeping sheep in the desert of Midian. He is not a rising leader. He is a man who tried to help his people forty years earlier, got it badly wrong, and ran. God speaks to him from a burning bush and tells him to go to Pharaoh. And Moses argues. Who am I, that I should go? What if they ask your name? What if they don’t believe me? I have never been a good speaker. And finally, in Exodus 4, verse 13: please, send someone else.

Five objections. God answers four of them patiently. He is angry only at the fifth, and not because Moses felt inadequate, but because Moses used his inadequacy as a reason to refuse.

Now notice what God never does. He never tells Moses he is wrong about himself. He never says, “Actually, you’re an excellent speaker.” He answers a completely different question. In Exodus 3:12 he says simply, “I will be with you.” The issue was never Moses’ capacity. It was God’s presence.

Jeremiah heard the same thing. He said, “I am too young.” God did not argue about his age. He said, go where I send you, say what I tell you, and do not be afraid of them, because I am with you. Presence, not capacity.

So here is where you are. Somebody asked you to lead. Maybe the pastor, maybe a ministry head, maybe nobody asked and the need was simply sitting there until you picked it up. You said yes. And somewhere between saying yes and today, a quiet thought arrived: they’ve made a mistake. I’m not the right person for this.

I want to tell you something before we go one step further. That feeling is normal. Feeling inadequate does not, by itself, mean you were wrongly chosen. Several leaders in Scripture voiced serious doubts when God called them. Their stories invite us to bring our doubts to God and ask for help, rather than treating confidence as the test of a calling.

But let me be careful, because “called” can be misunderstood in two directions.

First, calling is not the same as feeling certain. Most people never get a supernatural confirmation. More often the call arrives as a need you can see, a nudge you cannot shake, and the confirmation of people who know you. If your pastor and your church see something in you, take that seriously. God frequently speaks through the body before he speaks in your emotions.

Second, calling is not permission to stay as you are. God did not require Moses to be qualified, but he did require him to go. When Paul describes an overseer in 1 Timothy 3, he talks about character: self-control, gentleness, the ability to teach and to care for people. Character is central, and character grows. Being called does not download the skills of running a meeting or recruiting a volunteer into you. You will need to learn them, and this course exists so that you can. And calling is never permission to bypass the training, supervision or safeguarding requirements of your role.

So pause here for a moment. Finish this sentence without softening it: the main reason I feel unqualified to lead is… Take a moment before you go on.

Here is the tool for this week. I call it Three Things I Do Not Know.

In Acts 4:13, the trained religious leaders of Jerusalem looked at Peter and John and saw that they were, in the Greek, agrammatos: unlettered, without formal rabbinic training. Fishermen, standing in front of the theological faculty of their nation. And the verse ends with the detail that matters: the council recognised that these men had been with Jesus. Their courage did not depend on credentials. Neither does yours. But notice that Peter and John had spent three years learning. Being with Jesus was their training. Your uncertainty can keep you teachable, and it should also show you exactly where you need help.

So this is your step. Write down three things you do not know how to do in this role. Be specific. Not “leadership,” but “how to prepare Thursday’s meeting,” or “how to train a new volunteer.” Then take that list to the person who put you in the position, and ask them to help you with one of the three. Do it this week.

Asking for help early sets a pattern that will protect you for years. Waiting until you are drowning sets a very different one.

One truth to carry with you this week. You are not required to be ready. You are required to be willing, teachable, and honest about what you do not know.

Let me close with a short prayer.

Lord, I did not choose myself for this, and I do not feel ready. Thank you that my sufficiency is from you and not from me. Where I am tempted to say “send someone else,” give me the courage to go. Where I do not know what I am doing, give me the humility to ask. Be with me, as you were with Moses. Amen.

I’ll see you in Lesson 2.
