Welcome to Lesson 2.

In this lesson you will learn why your own life with God cannot be replaced by your service, how Jesus protected his time with the Father when the need was at its highest, and two practical decisions that will keep you spiritually alive while you lead.

Let's begin with Scripture. First Timothy chapter 4, verse 16.

"Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching. Persist in this, for by so doing you will save both yourself and your hearers."

Paul is writing to Timothy, a young leader he has left in charge of the church in Ephesus. Ephesus was a large, wealthy city with a famous temple, competing teachers, and a church full of strong opinions. Timothy was not old, he was not naturally confident, and Paul knew it. So this letter is full of direct instruction. And in the middle of it comes one of the most direct instructions to a leader anywhere in Scripture: watch yourself, and watch what you teach.

The word Paul uses for "keep a close watch" is epechō. It means to hold your attention on something, to fix your eyes on it and not let go. It is the word you would use for a sailor watching the coastline. And notice that Paul joins two things together: your life and your teaching. Not one or the other. What Timothy teaches and how Timothy lives both matter to the people he serves. A leader's conduct can support the message, or quietly undermine it.

There is a line in Song of Songs that has followed me through twenty years of ministry. In chapter 1, verse 6, the young woman says that they made her keeper of the vineyards, but her own vineyard she has not kept. In context she is talking about working in the family's fields under the sun while her own appearance suffered. But every leader I have ever met eventually reads that verse and goes quiet, because it describes exactly what happens to us. You are put in charge of other people's spiritual growth, and somewhere in the middle of it, you stop tending your own.

It happens faster than you would expect. Within a few months of leading, you notice that you have started reading the Bible only to find something to say. Your prayer life has become planning. You are producing spiritual food constantly and eating almost none of it.

If anyone could have justified skipping personal prayer for the sake of urgent ministry, it was Jesus. Mark chapter 1 describes an exhausting evening in Capernaum. The whole town gathered at the door, and Jesus healed many. The next verse tells us that very early, while it was still dark, he got up, went out to a solitary place, and prayed. Simon and the others came looking for him, and there is an edge in their words: everyone is searching for you. They could not understand why he had disappeared when everyone wanted him. Luke tells us this was not a one-off. Jesus regularly withdrew to quiet places and prayed, even as the crowds grew. If the Son of God, in a three-year ministry, made time to be alone with the Father while people were literally waiting outside the door, then your ministry is not too urgent for it either.

So here is the truth for this lesson. Your public service cannot replace your private life with God. You are the instrument here. Nobody else can maintain it for you.

Let me add one more picture, from Acts 6. The Greek-speaking widows in the Jerusalem church were being overlooked in the daily food distribution. This was a real injustice in a fast-growing church. And the Twelve said something striking: it would not be right for us to neglect the word of God in order to wait on tables. They did not dismiss the problem. They arranged for seven trusted people to take responsibility for it, and they protected their own responsibility for prayer and the word. You will face a version of this in your first year. There will be a real need, you will be the obvious person, and saying yes will quietly cost you the hours you were using to stay spiritually alive. Learning to say, "this matters, and here is who should carry it," is a skill we will come back to in Lesson 5.

So pause here for a moment. When did you last read the Bible with no intention of using it for anyone else? Be honest about the date. Take a moment before you go on.

Pause here. Take a moment before you go on.

Here is the tool for this week. It has two parts, and both are small.

First, a fixed time, not a hopeful intention. "I'll pray when I get a chance" reliably becomes no prayer. A modest fixed time you actually keep beats an ambitious one you abandon by February.

Second, a trustworthy person who can ask honest questions. A mature peer, a pastor, a mentor, or an experienced leader outside your direct team, someone discreet, who can ask about your conduct, your relationships and your spiritual life. Agree what you will discuss, how often you will meet, and the limits of confidentiality. This is not surrendering your privacy. It is making sure someone can see you.

So this is your step. Put one protected appointment for prayer and personal Bible reading in your calendar this week. Make it modest enough to keep. Then, as a follow-up, approach one trustworthy person and ask whether they would consider meeting with you once a month. If nobody comes to mind, ask the person who appointed or supervises you to suggest someone. Protect the prayer appointment before you fill your calendar with the next round of ministry tasks.

One truth to carry with you this week. Keep your own vineyard. You cannot feed people from a field you have stopped tending.

Lord, you know how quickly I begin reading your word only to find something to say. Give me back the hunger to hear from you for myself. Help me keep the time I set aside, and give me one person who can ask me honest questions. Keep me close to you while I serve. Amen.
