"Can a Baptist date a Pentecostal?" "I'm Pentecostal and he's Anglican — is that being unequally yoked?" "What should I expect if I date someone from a Pentecostal church?"

I get versions of these questions often, and the honest answer to all of them starts the same way: it depends what the difference actually is. Denominational labels hide as much as they reveal. Two people from the same denomination can be spiritually miles apart, and two people from different denominations can share everything that matters. So this article does two things. It describes, fairly, what dating a Pentecostal Christian may involve for someone from a different tradition, because that is the question most people are asking. It then offers a framework for sorting denominational differences — any denominational differences — into those you can live with, those you must address before engagement and the rarer differences that may reveal that you do not share the same Christian foundation.

## First, what "equally yoked" is and is not about

Paul's instruction not to be "unequally yoked with unbelievers" (2 Corinthians 6:14) was written to a church in pagan Corinth about partnership with unbelief, and applying it to marriage is a reasonable and long-standing inference. But the yoke is about allegiance and direction, not about the sign outside the church. It says nothing about denominations. Two Baptists can be unequally yoked; a Pentecostal and a Presbyterian can be equally yoked. I have written about this at more length in [What Does "Equally Yoked" Mean in Christian Dating?](/articles/what-does-equally-yoked-mean), and this article assumes that foundation: a shared, personally owned faith in Christ, producing fruit, belonging somewhere, and going in the same direction.

If you have that, denominational difference is a real question but a secondary one. If you do not, no amount of denominational agreement will supply it.

## What dating a Pentecostal Christian may involve

Pentecostalism is not one thing. It is a family of churches and movements — classical Pentecostal denominations, charismatic congregations within older denominations, independent churches, and large African, Latin American and Asian movements — and they differ considerably from one another. What follows is a general picture, offered so that someone from outside the tradition knows what questions to ask. It is not a description of any particular church, and the only way to know what your partner's church actually believes and expects is to ask careful questions and attend for yourself.

**The gifts of the Spirit are expected, not merely permitted.** Pentecostal churches generally believe that the gifts described in the New Testament — tongues, prophecy, healing, words of knowledge — continue today and are a normal part of church life. Many, though not all, teach that speaking in tongues is the initial evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit. If you come from a tradition that believes those gifts ceased with the apostles, or that is quietly uneasy about them, this is the first difference to name. It is a real theological difference. It is also one that many mixed couples live with well, provided each respects the other's conscience and neither treats the other as a second-class Christian for it.

**Worship is expressive.** Raised hands, spoken responses, extended singing, prayer aloud and in unison, and sometimes tongues in the service. For someone from a liturgical or quiet tradition this can be unfamiliar, and for a Pentecostal a silent, ordered service can feel cold. Neither reaction is a verdict on the other's spirituality. But if you marry, you will need a church you can both belong to, and worship style is one of the things that decides whether that church exists.

**Church involvement tends to be high.** Midweek services, prayer meetings, cell groups and an expectation of active participation are common. This is a strength — a Pentecostal believer is often very well known in their congregation, which means there are people who can speak to their character. It also shapes a shared life: your evenings, your weekends, your circle of friends.

**The pastor's voice can carry considerable weight.** In many Pentecostal churches, the pastor is deeply involved in members' lives, including their relationships, and may be consulted about — or expect to be consulted about — a decision to marry. In a healthy church this is a gift: an experienced person who knows both of you and cares about you. But it needs the same test as any counsel. A pastor may advise. A pastor who claims the authority to decide whom you marry, or who uses office, prophecy or the fear of spiritual consequences to secure a decision, has stepped beyond counsel, and the [article on testing spiritual claims](/articles/god-told-me-to-marry-you) applies to leaders as fully as to partners.

**Prophetic words about relationships happen.** "God showed me you two will marry" is said in Pentecostal settings more than in most, sometimes from the platform. Paul's instruction to a church that prized prophecy was to weigh it (1 Corinthians 14:29), and you are among those who weigh it. A word that names a person does not bind you, and a no given after honest prayer is not rebellion.

**Standards of holiness are often visible.** Some Pentecostal churches — by no means all — hold to particular expectations about dress, entertainment, alcohol, or the roles of men and women, and some expect members to marry within the church. These vary enormously between congregations and countries. Do not assume; ask. And notice where a standard is a matter of conscience your partner holds sincerely, and where it is a matter of the church's expectation that will bear on both of you.

**Spiritual language is fluent.** Pentecostal believers tend to speak readily and naturally about God's leading, prayer, and spiritual experience. That is a good thing, and it can also, as any fluency can, disguise the absence of what it describes. Spiritual vocabulary is evidence of exposure to Christianity, not of transformation by it. Look, as you would with anyone, at fruit: how they treat people who cannot help them, how they respond to correction, whether they keep their word.

If you are the Pentecostal partner reading this, turn the questions around. Your partner's quieter tradition is not necessarily lukewarm because it is quiet. Pentecostals themselves differ over how baptism in the Holy Spirit relates to speaking in tongues. In 1 Corinthians 12:29–30, Paul's rhetorical questions expect the answer that not all exercise the same gifts, including tongues; Christians debate how that passage relates to the classical Pentecostal doctrine of initial evidence. Whatever view you hold, do not treat a believer who differs as a second-class Christian. And a church with a written liturgy has not replaced the Spirit with a book; many of its words are Scripture.

## Sorting the differences

Here is the framework I use with couples from any two traditions. It has three groups, and the work is in sorting honestly.

**Foundational — a difference here means you are not equally yoked.** Historic Christianity is centred on the Triune God; the full divinity and humanity of Jesus Christ; his atoning death and bodily resurrection; salvation through him; and the authority of Scripture. Mainstream Pentecostal, Baptist, Anglican, Presbyterian, Methodist, Lutheran and most other Protestant traditions agree on these. But not every group that calls itself a church affirms those convictions, and movements associated with several traditions — including Pentecostalism — may deny the Trinity, redefine the identity of Christ or teach a fundamentally different account of salvation. If you discover that you and your partner mean fundamentally different things by "Christian," that is not merely a denominational difference. It raises the deeper question of allegiance and partnership to which Paul's warning speaks.

**Liveable — real, but part of the texture of a marriage.** How and when to baptise. Styles of worship. Views on the end times. The continuation of spiritual gifts. Whether to drink alcohol. Many strong marriages contain differences on these, and both people grow from them. The test is whether each of you can respect the other's conscience without contempt, and whether you can find a church you can both belong to.

**Secondary in theology, primary in daily life — must be settled before engagement.** These are the ones that catch couples. If one of you believes children must be baptised as infants and the other believes they must not, that decision cannot be postponed once a child arrives. If one of you tithes as a conviction and the other regards it as optional, that becomes a monthly conversation. If one of you comes from a church that expects members to marry within it, the difference is not only between the two of you but with a community. If one of you cannot imagine leaving your current church and the other cannot imagine joining it, you do not yet have a church to build a marriage in. None of these automatically requires the end of a relationship. All require specific conversation now rather than a vague hope that the difference will resolve itself. If no shared practice is possible without one person violating a settled conviction, the difference may reveal a genuine incompatibility.

## The questions to ask each other

Not in one sitting, and not as an interrogation. Over time, in ordinary conversation.

- What does your church actually teach about the gifts of the Spirit, and what do you personally believe?
- If we married, where would we worship? Could you belong at my church? Could I belong at yours? What would each of us find hard?
- How would we baptise children, and when?
- What does your church expect of its members — in giving, in attendance, in dress, in who they marry — and which of those are your own convictions?
- How involved is your pastor in members' relationships, and how would you respond if they disagreed with our decision?
- Has anyone given you a prophetic word about us? What did you do with it?
- What in my tradition do you find hard to respect? What in yours do you think I find hard?

The final two questions matter most. A couple who can name what they each find difficult in the other's tradition, without contempt, are far better placed than a couple who have agreed not to talk about it.

## When it is workable, and when it is not

In my experience, a denominational difference between two believers who share the foundation is workable when three things are true: each respects the other's conscience; the daily-life questions have been addressed specifically before engagement; and there is a real, named and visited church to which both could belong. Where those hold, the difference often becomes a strength. Each partner is stretched by the other, and their children grow up with a wider Christian world than either parent had.

It is not workable when one partner regards the other's tradition as sub-Christian and expects them to be corrected by marriage; when a church or family will not accept the marriage and one partner will not stand with the other; or when the daily-life questions keep being deferred because raising them causes conflict. In those cases the problem is rarely the denomination. It is the relationship.

And it is no longer merely a denominational question if you discover that the two of you hold fundamentally different convictions about who Christ is or how a person is saved. That is not a secondary difference to manage. It falls within the deeper concern about allegiance and partnership that makes Paul's warning relevant to marriage.
